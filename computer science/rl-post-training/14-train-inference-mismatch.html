Training-Inference Mismatch: Why the Rollout Engine and the Trainer Disagree on Probabilities, and the Four Families of Fixes

Two engines run the same weights and report different probabilities for the same token, so the "on-policy" batch is quietly off-policy. This module traces where the disagreement enters (reduction order, precision, the sampler, MoE routing, indexer top-k, tokenization), what it does to a run, and the four fix families the 2025-2026 reports use: correct statistically, fix the numerics, remove the source, or mask the damage. You can use it to instrument a rollout and trainer pair, find the dominant source for a new architecture, and pick the cheapest fix that covers it.

1. The mismatch, defined and measured

1.1 What "same weights" does not give you

A rollout engine and a trainer load one checkpoint. For a sampled token \(a_t\) in state \(s_t\), the engine reports \(\log\pi^{infer}(a_t\mid s_t)\) while sampling; run the trainer on the same token ids and it returns \(\log\pi^{train}(a_t\mid s_t)\). The mismatch ratio and its log are $$\rho_t=\frac{\pi^{train}_{\theta_{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi^{infer}_{\theta_{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)},\qquad \delta_t=\log\rho_t .$$ This is the \(\rho_t\) of Module 12 (Section 1.2), where the ratio a loss sees is factored exactly into optimizer drift \(r_t\) and this term. Zhong et al. call the same definition "objective-agnostic": it exists before you choose REINFORCE, PPO or GRPO6 (Eq. 1).

Two consequences need separate fixes. The first is a biased gradient. The batch is drawn from \(\mu=\pi^{infer}\) but the optimizer differentiates \(\pi=\pi^{train}\), so the update is \(\mathbb E_{y\sim\mu}[\nabla\log\pi(y)\,R]\) and not the policy gradient \(\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}[\nabla\log\pi(y)\,R]\)2 (Eq. 3). One update per rollout does not remove it: \(r_t\) is exactly 1 in that regime and the bias still lives in \(\rho_t\). The second is the deployment gap. The weights that maximize reward under \(\pi\) need not maximize it under \(\mu\), the engine that serves the model; a correction inside the loss addresses the first problem and, by construction, not the second2 (Eq. 4).

Qwen's formulation says when a token-level loss is a valid stand-in at all. The exact sequence-level importance weight is a product over tokens, too noisy to use; the token-level surrogate replaces it with a sum of per-token ratios, the first-order expansion \(\prod_t(1+\epsilon_t)\approx 1+\sum_t\epsilon_t\). That holds only while every \(\epsilon_t\) is small, and each token's ratio splits into the training-inference discrepancy and policy staleness, so the surrogate is valid "only when both" are minimized7 (Abstract, §2.3-2.4). The discrepancy is one of the two terms that decide whether the algorithm approximates the right objective, not a rounding error beneath it.

1.3 Length turns a small per-token gap into a large sequence-level one

The sequence-level ratio is the product of token ratios, so its log is the sum \(\sum_t\delta_t\). Averaged over sequences drawn from \(\mu\), \(\mathbb E_\mu[\log\rho(y)]=-\mathrm{KL}(\mu\Vert\pi)\), which is \(-kT\) for a per-token KL of \(k\) nats over \(T\) tokens (derived). Qi et al. plot the sequence log-ratio against length for R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, sampled at temperature 1.0 with no top-p so that \(\mu\) is comparable to \(\pi\), and fit a slope of −1.01 per thousand tokens in BF16 against −0.07 in FP16, with sequence-level KL[µ‖π] of 7.64 against 0.322 (§3.5, Fig. 2). Reading the slope as \(1000k\) gives a per-token KL of about \(1.0\times10^{-3}\) in BF16 and \(7\times10^{-5}\) in FP16 (derived), which sits inside the dense range in Table 1. Figure 3 draws the two fits.

Why that matters follows from the variance of the weight. If the sequence log-ratio is Gaussian with mean \(-\sigma^2/2\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the lognormal that keeps \(\mathbb E_\mu[\rho]=1\), then \(\mathrm{KL}=\sigma^2/2\) and \(\mathbb E_\mu[\rho^2]=e^{\sigma^2}\). The fraction of a batch that is effectively usable under sequence-level weights, \(1/\mathbb E[\rho^2]\), is \(e^{-\sigma^2}=e^{-2\mathrm{KL}}\). With Qi et al.'s average KL values that is \(e^{-15.3}\approx2.3\times10^{-7}\) in BF16 and \(e^{-0.64}\approx0.53\) in FP16 (derived). It is a model, not a measurement: it assumes independent tokens and one lognormal, and real weights are heavier-tailed. It does predict the direction of Qi et al.'s result: sequence-level masked IS "suffers from slow convergence due to the high variance" in BF16, and in FP16 the sequence ratio "becomes much more concentrated and stable"2 (§4.2).

2. Where two engines on identical weights diverge

Six mechanisms, numbered as in Figure 1: three numerical, one about what is being sampled, and two discrete decisions that turn small noise into a different computation. Comparisons to the serving kernels you know are the writer's; the facts are cited.

2.1 Reduction order: batch size changes the answer

Floating-point addition is not associative, so any change in the order of a reduction can change a result. Figure 4 shows the smallest case worth remembering: with round-to-nearest-even, \((1+2^{-8})+2^{-8}\) is 1.0 in BF16, because each addend is exactly half a unit in the last place of 1.0 and ties go to even, while \(1+(2^{-8}+2^{-8})\) is 1.0078125 (derived). In FP16 both orders agree. The next representable number above 1 is \(1+2^{-10}\approx1.000977\) in FP16 and \(1+2^{-7}\approx1.007812\) in BF16, the eight-fold resolution gap the FP16 argument rests on2 (Table 1).

The engineering question is who chooses the order. Horace He's analysis for Thinking Machines argues that the common explanation, concurrency and atomic adds, is wrong for inference: the forward pass contains no atomic adds and is "run-to-run deterministic", so identical requests to an identical server give identical results. What varies is the batch size, because load changes it, and the kernels are not batch-invariant1 (§ "Batch invariance"). Three operations carry reductions and each breaks invariance its own way. RMSNorm may split one row across cores when the batch is small. A matmul may use Split-K, or different tensor-core instructions, to fill the GPU. Attention's decode-time Split-KV changes how many chunks the KV is cut into: a KV length of 1,000 cut four ways gives four chunks of 250, a fixed chunk size of 256 gives three of 256 and one of 232, so the trees differ unless the split size, not the split count, is fixed1. Their invariant kernels use one matmul configuration for all shapes and lost about 20% against cuBLAS, with a Triton kernel that is "not an optimized" one (no TMA)1.

The same page measures the effect in the setting where you would least expect it. Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 at temperature 0, prompted "Tell me about Richard Feynman" 1,000 times with 1,000 tokens each, produced 80 unique completions; the most common appeared 78 times, all 1,000 agreed for the first 102 tokens, and at token 103 992 continued with "Queens, New York" and 8 with "New York City". With batch-invariant kernels all 1,000 were identical1 (Experiments). That is nondeterminism within one engine; a trainer, with its own tile shapes and batch, should disagree with the engine at least as much (the writer's inference).

Two orders are specific to RL. The rollout is a decode loop with tiny matrices and the trainer processes whole sequences in large batches, so different kernels get selected9. And the rollout often runs tensor-parallel while the trainer shards with FSDP, so the all-reduce order differs; Zhang et al.'s Tree-Based Invariant Kernels fix one hierarchical order across TP sizes and report bitwise identical results between vLLM and FSDP (abstract only read)11.

2.2 Precision: where the rounding lands

Order matters only because every partial sum is rounded. The format decides how coarse the rounding is. Qi et al.'s thesis is that BF16 (8 exponent bits, 7 mantissa bits) lets "different rounding errors" in two implementations accumulate over a sequence, while FP16's 10 mantissa bits give "8 times more precision" and absorb implementation differences. FP16's range is roughly \(6.1\times10^{-5}\) to \(6.6\times10^{4}\) against BF16's \(1.2\times10^{-38}\) to \(3.4\times10^{38}\); they argue RL no longer needs BF16's range because weight and activation ranges were set in pre-training2 (§3.1, §3.4).

One result matters for an inference engineer: inference precision alone barely moves task scores. On R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B with a 32K budget, AMC23 was 62.35 (BF16), 63.10 (FP16) and 62.42 (FP32)2 (Table 2). An eval that looks identical across dtypes does not show the dtype is safe for rollouts: accuracy is a coarse average, while an importance ratio reads the probability of one sampled token.

MiniMax-M1 localized the gap by layer-by-layer comparison and found it in the LM head: "high-magnitude activations in the LM head at the output layer" were "the primary source of error", and raising the head to FP32 realigned the probabilities, with the correlation in Figure 2 rising from 0.987319 to 0.9971353 (§3.2). The report adds that the problem "did not appear in smaller, dense models with softmax attention", so the hybrid-attention architecture, or its kernels, was a precondition. The report does not say why the head is sensitive; here is an arithmetic reason that fits (derived, not MiniMax's). \(\log\pi(a)=z_a-\log\sum_v e^{z_v}\), so an error in the sampled token's own logit passes into \(\log\pi\) nearly one-to-one when that token dominates. A BF16 number in \([16,32)\) has spacing \(2^4\cdot2^{-7}=0.125\), so rounding the logit alone moves it by up to 0.0625 nats, a 6% probability change; FP16 spacing there is \(2^4\cdot2^{-10}=0.0156\), an error of at most 0.0078. Large logits put the format's step function directly on the quantity the importance ratio reads.

2.3 A different program for the same function

Inference computes one token per step from a KV cache; training computes all positions in parallel. Qi et al. note that "tokens are generated auto-regressively during inference but are processed in parallel during training", and add different parallelization strategies and precision-sensitive operations such as MoE top-k2 (§2.2). Backends compound it: the Miles post picks FlashAttention-3 for both engines because it achieves bitwise equality between prefill and decode, which suggests (the writer's reading) that other backends' prefill and decode kernels need not agree9. Quantized rollouts are the lever you may care about: a follow-up by Yao et al. is said to find the mismatch particularly severe with INT8 and FP8 rollouts, with TIS still stabilizing training (second-hand, unverified). DeepSeek-V4's post-training uses FP4 quantization-aware training for MoE expert weights and the indexer's QK path; its report does not discuss the rollout-trainer gap there12 (§1).

2.4 The sampler is part of the policy

The first three sources are noise on the same function; this one is a different function. A rollout engine samples from \(\mathrm{softmax}(z/\tau)\) after top-\(p\) or top-\(k\) truncation and renormalization, while the trainer's \(\log\pi\) is the plain log-softmax of \(z\) (Module 2 states the unbiasedness condition). Two consequences follow.

The first is what the engine reports. vLLM's --logprobs-mode has four values (raw_logprobs, processed_logprobs, raw_logits, processed_logits); "raw" is the value before logit processors and "processed" is after all of them, "including temperature and top_k/top_p", and the default is raw_logprobs13. TRL's documented vLLM server command passes --logprobs-mode processed_logprobs and --max-logprobs -114. A ratio built from the default raw value while sampling at \(\tau\neq1\) or with truncation compares the trainer with the wrong behavior policy (derived; check which mode your framework requests).

The second is support. With top-\(p\) truncation the kept set has mass \(Z_t\ge p\) and a kept token's behavior probability is \(\pi(a)/Z_t\), so the truncation alone contributes a per-token factor \(\rho_t=Z_t\in[p,1]\) and tokens outside the kept set have behavior probability 0 and never appear in a batch. At \(p=0.95\) the worst case is \(\ln0.95=-0.051\) per token, \(-51\) nats over 1,000 tokens (derived; far above typical). DeepSeek-V3.2's remedy is to preserve the top-p and top-k masks from \(\pi_{old}\)'s sampling and apply them to \(\pi_\theta\) in training, "ensuring both policies share identical action subspaces", and it reports that combining top-p sampling with this Keep Sampling Mask "effectively preserves language consistency"4 (§3.1). Qi et al. sidestep it for measurement by sampling at temperature 1.0 with no top-p "so that µ is directly comparable to π"2 (§3.5).

2.5 Discrete decisions amplify noise

A continuous function turns a \(10^{-3}\) perturbation into a \(10^{-3}\) change; a top-\(k\) selection can turn it into a different branch. DeepSeek-V3.2 states that discrepancies between inference and training frameworks, "compounded by policy updates, can result in inconsistent expert routing during inference and training even for identical inputs", inducing "abrupt shifts in the active parameter subspace"4 (§3.1). Qwen's MoE decomposition writes each engine's routed experts explicitly, so the discrepancy term includes the two engines picking different experts for the same input7 (Eq. 6). Routing drift under optimizer updates is Module 12's Section 4.4; the concern here is that routing flips with no update at all.

The sparse-attention indexer is the same mechanism one layer over. GLM-5 uses DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA)5, where an indexer retrieves the top-\(k\) most relevant KV entries with \(k=2048\), and reports that the retrieved top-\(k\) results "are critical for RL stability"5 (§3.2). Section 6.2 gives the failure.

The last discrete source is not numerical at all. If the rollout service returns text and the trainer re-tokenizes it, token boundaries, whitespace and normalization, truncation and special-token placement can differ from what was sampled, corrupting the alignment between actions and advantages. GLM-5 reports that a token-in-token-out (TITO) gateway that records the engine's token ids and feeds them to the learner is "critical for asynchronous RL training"5 (§4.1.2). Module 18 covers multi-turn trajectories; the point here is that the token id sequence must match before the probability can.

3. What the mismatch does to a run

If the mismatch were only noise it would average out. The reports that isolate it find that it does not, and how it hurts depends on where \(\pi_{old}\) comes from.

3.1 The zero-mismatch reference experiment

The cleanest evidence is from Zhong et al., who removed the mismatch rather than correcting it. VeXact is a rollout engine whose token log-probabilities are bitwise identical to FSDP's: the same Hugging Face model code in both, batch-invariant kernels with fixed tiling and reduction order (RMSNorm, batched matmul, fused MoE), attention with KV splitting disabled, and chunked prefill, CUDA graphs and pipeline parallelism to recover throughput6 (§3.1). The only difference between a VeXact run and a vLLM run is the mismatch, so any gap between the runs is attributable to it.

On-policy REINFORCE on Qwen3-30B-A3B (DAPO data) shows the vLLM run improving and then degrading after step 280, with training and validation reward falling from 0.574 and 0.293 to 0.255 and 0.067, while the VeXact run reaches 0.753 and 0.5346 (§3.2, Fig. 2). GRPO runs on Qwen3-1.7B separate the two ways of obtaining \(\pi_{old}\). With the trainer's recomputation, reward falls from about 0.87 to roughly 0.40 within 650 steps, partly recovers, then reaches near zero after about step 1,665; with the engine's own log-probabilities (bypass) it degrades once, to roughly 0.4, without collapsing. VeXact holds about 0.936 (§4.1, Fig. 3). Both ways of computing the ratio failed.

They give three observations. First, aggregate KL probes miss the early phase: in recomputation mode K1 and K3 on the PPO ratio stay "nearly flat during the first 700 steps, even though the reward is already entering the degradation phase", and rise only in bypass mode6 (Fig. 4). Second, with the zero-centered contribution \(C(r)=-(r-1)\hat A\) (same gradient as the usual surrogate, zero at \(r=1\)), the trainer-referenced contribution under vLLM separates by advantage sign, with "a larger positive shift for negative-advantage tokens", while the two references coincide under VeXact6 (Fig. 5): recomputation adds a sign-dependent skew, not uniform noise. Third, bypass uses the right denominator but evaluates the numerator in the trainer's numerics, so "the optimizer exploits numerical artifacts in the trainer's forward pass"; in the first 10 steps on GSM8K with Qwen3-1.7B, 17.3% of token log-probability changes pointed against the sign of the advantage when measured after the update, and none did under VeXact6 (§4.1). That is the deployment gap of Section 1.1 made visible: the update moved the trainer's landscape the right way and the engine that generates the next batch saw something else.

Common error

Watching KL between the old and current policy as the health signal for the mismatch. In recomputation mode that KL measures optimizer drift only, and Zhong et al. show it stays flat while the run degrades. The signal that tracks the mismatch is the per-token or sequence-level gap between the engine's returned log-probability and the trainer's, which TRL logs as sampling/sampling_logp_difference/mean and /max only when its vLLM correction is enabled14.

4. Family A: correct the gradient statistically

The first family leaves both engines alone and re-weights each update by the mismatch ratio so the expected gradient is again the trainer policy's. It is the cheapest to adopt, a change to the loss, and the reports disagree about whether it is enough.

4.1 Four forms of the same weight

Write \(\rho_t=\pi^{train}_{old}(a_t)/\pi^{infer}_{old}(a_t)\) as in Section 1.1. The estimator that is exactly unbiased multiplies the whole gradient by the sequence ratio \(\rho(y)=\prod_t\rho_t\)2 (Eq. 5). Its variance is the problem of Section 1.3, so every deployed variant trades some bias for variance, along two axes: what happens to a large weight, and which unit carries it.

  • Truncate or mask. Truncated importance sampling (TIS) caps the weight, \(\min(\rho,C)\), so a large ratio is damped but still contributes2 (Eq. 6, 9). Masked importance sampling (MIS) zeroes samples whose ratio leaves a range and keeps in-range ratios2 (Eq. 7, 10). TIS is credited to Yao et al. and MIS to Liu et al. (second-hand, posts unread). TRL implements both with an optional lower bound, "inspired by" IcePop (Section 7)14.
  • Token, sequence or in between. Per token the weight is cheap and, in the Miles post's words, "mathematically biased"; the sequence product is unbiased but has "extreme variance"; the geometric mean of the token weights sits between them and is less numerically unstable than the product9.

Shipped settings differ. TRL's GRPO trainer enables sequence-level masked IS by default for vLLM generation, with \(C_{max}=3.0\) and no lower bound15. Olmo 3 multiplies the loss by a token-level truncated weight, taking the denominator from the probabilities vLLM returns, with a cap of 2.0 in its 32B Think and RL-Zero runs and none listed for its 7B Think and 7B Instruct runs16 (§4.4.1, Table 49). Qwen's 30B MoE study caps the token weight at 57 (§4.2). Zhong et al. note why token-level TIS works: multiplying the PPO ratio \(r=\pi_\theta/\pi^{train}_{old}\) by \(\rho\) gives \(\pi_\theta/\pi^{infer}_{old}\), the bypass ratio, so TIS turns the recomputation loss into the bypass loss with a cap6 (§4.2). Module 12 (Section 1.2) shows where each design places the factor.

4.2 The price is a forward pass, not a mismatch

Qi et al. observe that the GRPO-style corrections need \(\pi^{train}_{old}\) for the sampled tokens, an extra trainer forward pass before the update, "approximately 25%" more training cost if a backward pass costs twice a forward pass2 (§1, §2.1.1). The arithmetic is Module 1's: a forward pass is about \(2N\) FLOPs per token and forward plus backward \(6N\), so the extra \(2N\) is 33% on top of an uncorrected trainer and 25% of the corrected \(8N\) (derived; I read the paper's 25% as the second). The estimator of Eq. 5 needs no such pass: it has one ratio \(\pi_\theta/\mu_{\theta'}\) and \(\pi_\theta\) comes from the loss's own forward pass. The extra pass is the price of keeping PPO's drift ratio \(r\) separate from \(\rho\) (the writer's reading of Eq. 5-10).

What that costs in wall-clock depends on whether the trainer is the bottleneck, Module 1's question. Olmo 3's footnote gives a 1,000 s step with 125 s of training16 (§4.4.3, footnote 41; Module 1, Section 5.1, reconciles it with the text's 75% idle figure): serialized, the extra 41.7 s is 4.2% of the step, and in an asynchronous design it hides behind generation until the learner's busy fraction rises from 12.5% to 16.7%. Tulu 3's 405B RLVR iteration took about 550 s of inference, 25 s of weight transfer and 1,500 s of training17 (§8.1); there the extra 500 s is 24.1% of the 2,075 s. All of it assumes a forward-only pass runs at the same utilization as the training pass.

4.3 What the correction does not buy

Qi et al.'s "sanity test" gives the controlled comparison. They keep MATH problems on which R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B scores 20% to 80% over 40 samples (1,460 questions), so a reliable algorithm should approach 100% training accuracy; each step has 64 questions with 8 rollouts and 4 gradient steps, at \(C=3\) for the IS variants2 (§4, §4.1). In BF16 the plain GRPO baseline peaks at 73% (VeRL) and 84% (Oat) before degrading, and token-level TIS "prolongs training slightly" and collapses after 82% and 88%. GSPO, which does not use the engine's probabilities at all, lasts longer than token TIS but its gradient norm became NaN after 1,200 steps in VeRL. Only sequence-level MIS stays stable in BF16, and it converges slowly and plateaus: 95% training accuracy against 99% in FP16 and 34% against 39% on AIME 2024, which they read as the deployment gap2 (§4.2). The bias-variance reading is theirs: low-variance, high-bias estimators (GRPO, token TIS, GSPO) are fast and collapse, and low-bias, high-variance ones (sequence-level IS or MIS) are stable and slow.

Other groups find the correction necessary and, with care, close to sufficient. Qwen's 30B MoE study reports that on-policy the basic policy gradient with the correction is the most stable, that removing it causes "rapid training collapse and a sharp drop in entropy", and that length normalization, used by GRPO and CISPO, is suboptimal because it breaks the first-order approximation7 (§4.3). Zhong et al. find that a token-level cap followed by sequence-level rejection on \(\rho\), not on drift, "can closely track" the zero-mismatch run, but that it "can only suppress already-generated samples and may also discard useful learning signals", and that without a zero-mismatch reference the thresholds are "largely ad hoc"6 (§4.2). The family fixes the bias at the price of variance and a forward pass; it does not touch the deployment gap.

5. Family B: change the numerics

The second family shrinks the disagreement at its size rather than correcting for it. Reduction order creates a difference; mantissa width and the places where logits are rounded set how large that difference becomes (the writer's synthesis of Sections 2.1 and 2.2). Three moves appear in the reports.

An FP32 LM head. MiniMax-M1 raised the output head to FP32, and the correlation in Figure 2 went from 0.987319 to 0.997135 and "remained stable throughout training, enabling successful reward increase"3 (§3.2). ScaleRL adopted FP32 logits in both generator and trainer after finding that it lifted the fitted asymptote \(A\) of a baseline recipe from 0.52 to 0.61, then found the effect nearly invisible inside the full recipe on a dense 8B model, where run-to-run noise on \(A\) is about 0.02, yet useful for GRPO and DAPO-style losses and for a 17B-by-16-expert MoE, where removing it reduced scalability18 (§3.2, Fig. 5b; leave-one-out and Scout results, Fig. 7 and 8b). Qi et al. say the FP32 head alone "is shown to be insufficient to prevent training collapse", citing Yao et al. and Liu et al., whose posts I could not read; that is second-hand2 (§2.2). The findings are compatible if the head is one of several sources whose weight depends on the architecture, which is how MiniMax states it. The reports give no overhead figure for the head.

FP16 in place of BF16. Qi et al. switch both engines to FP16 and report a sequence-level mismatch "approximately 24× smaller" (KL 7.64 to 0.32, Section 1.3), with no change to the algorithm: under FP16 the five algorithms they compare are "almost indistinguishable", and the plain unbiased sequence-level estimator of Section 4.1 becomes usable2 (§3.5, §4.3). Their ablation with vLLM and FSDP separates the engines: BF16 training with FP32 inference is fully stable but the FP32 inference is "nearly three times slower", while FP16 in both is the most stable and loses no speed. They extend it to a Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base MoE, LoRA, Qwen3-14B-Base with DAPO and OctoThinker-3B, with BF16 LoRA collapsing near step 600, across two frameworks, VeRL and Oat2 (§4.4, §5). Their own limits: FP16 is not claimed "universally optimal", very large models may overflow its range, and FP16 needs loss scaling, which adds a global synchronization to check for overflow in distributed training2 (§3.2, §6). Zhong et al. credit a 2026 TRL-team write-up (Dirhoussi et al.) with the same FP16 finding; I did not locate that write-up6 (§5). The measured 24-fold reduction is larger than the 8-fold finer resolution of FP16 mantissas that one rounding would explain; the paper does not decompose the difference (derived comparison).

Higher precision on one side bought stability at triple the inference cost, a poor trade for the engine that is already the bottleneck in Module 1's cost model.

6. Family C: remove the source

The third family makes the two computations agree exactly. For the continuous sources that means bitwise-identical kernels; for the discrete ones it means reusing the engine's decision in the trainer or making the selection deterministic.

6.1 Bitwise identical engines, and what they cost

Thinking Machines' invariant kernels plus a modified trainer gave bitwise identical results between sampling and training and "0 KL divergence" in an RLVR run on Bigmath with a Qwen 2.5-VL instruct 8B policy and 4,096-token rollouts, where the same run without a correction lost reward and with importance weighting held KL near 0.0011. The price in their test (Qwen-3-8B, one GPU, 1,000 sequences of 90 to 110 tokens) was 26 s for default vLLM, 55 s for unoptimized deterministic vLLM and 42 s with an improved attention kernel: 2.1 and 1.6 times (derived)1. DeepSeek-V4 is the largest public case. Split-KV is not allowed, since it would leave a partially filled last wave of SMs, so decode attention uses two kernels: one computes a whole sequence in one SM for full waves, and a second spreads one sequence over several SMs for the last partial wave, with its accumulation order designed to match the first and distributed shared memory within a thread-block cluster for the exchange. Matmul replaces cuBLAS with DeepGEMM and drops split-k in most cases, with optimizations that "match or even surpass" standard split-k in most major scenarios (the authors' claim). The backward pass gets per-SM accumulation buffers in attention and token-order pre-processing in MoE12 (§3.3). The report gives the kernel design, not a rollout-mismatch measurement.

Tensor-parallel size is the remaining reduction-order gap: TBIK's tree-structured kernels make results independent of it11. The Miles post is explicit about the burden: its mode requires invasive changes to Megatron and FSDP, is hard to reproduce, and every architecture, parallelism choice and hardware target adds complexity9.

6.2 Discrete choices: replay or determinize

For MoE routing, DeepSeek-V3.2 stores the experts used at sampling and enforces them in training ("Keep Routing", in use since DeepSeek-V3-0324)4 (§3.1); Rollout Routing Replay (R3) is the same idea, recording the inference engine's routing and replaying it in training21. Qwen's analysis finds a cost beyond storage. On-policy, R3 reduced the training-inference KL and still gave no gain, and R3 without the importance correction "still fails rapidly", from which they conclude that replay "can alter the original target policy and introduce bias"7 (§4.3). Replay lowers the measured gap and changes what is being optimized, a reason to keep the correction alongside it. Module 12 (Section 4.4, Figure 6) covers the other branch, GSPO's sequence average.

GLM-5's indexer is the case where replay is not available. The report judges storing the indexer's top-\(k\) indices at every token position "clearly impractical", because \(k=2048\) is "much larger than the k typically used in MoE" and the storage and the traffic between engines would be "enormous"; for scale, at an MoE top-\(k\) of 8 a record would hold 256 times as many indices per decision (illustrative arithmetic, the report gives no figure)5 (§3.2). What worked instead was determinism: replacing the non-deterministic CUDA and TileLang top-\(k\) operators in the training engine's indexer with torch.topk, "slightly slower but deterministic", which "yields substantial RL gains", where the other operators "caused drastic performance degradation during RL after only a few steps, accompanied by a sharp drop in entropy". The indexer's parameters were also frozen during RL to prevent unstable learning in the indexer5. The report does not say which pair of passes disagreed, rollout against trainer or two trainer passes, so what it demonstrates is that a non-deterministic discrete selection is enough to break a run, and that a deterministic one removes the problem at small cost.

7. Family D: mask what the engine's own probabilities flag

The fourth family does not try to make the estimator unbiased; it uses the engine's returned probability to decide what the update should not learn from. It borders family A (IcePop is a two-sided token-level mask on \(\rho\) that passes in-range ratios through); the difference is intent, a trust region on the data and not a correction of the expectation. Module 12 (Sections 5.2, 5.3) writes the IcePop and DeepSeek-V3.2 masks in one form; this section adds the evidence on thresholds.

IcePop comes from Ling Team's Ring-1T report. It masks tokens whose \(\rho\) leaves \([\alpha,\beta]\) and passes the rest through, and for Ring-1T uses \(\alpha=0.5\), \(\beta=5\) with a learning rate of \(2\times10^{-6}\), no KL, temperature 1.0, 480 prompts times 8 rollouts and responses up to 65,536 tokens8 (§2.3.2, §2.3.4). On Ring-mini-2.0 (16.8B total, 0.75B active) it beat TIS on AIME25 (Avg@64), lifting the base score of 63% by "over 14%" while vanilla GRPO fell by step 150; at the defaults about 1 to 2 per thousand tokens were masked, skewed toward high entropy8 (§2.4.1, App. C, Fig. 5, 12). The sensitivity result is the part to carry over: a narrow \([0.5,2.0]\) "immediately destabilizes training", the default \([0.5,5.0]\) stabilizes it, and a wider \([0.4,5.0]\) also stabilizes but admits higher-probability tokens8 (App. C, Fig. 13). GLM-5 ships \(\beta=2\), the range Ring-mini-2.0 found unstable, in a reasoning stage run entirely on-policy with a different model and stack5 (§3.2). The two do not conflict: the band is a property of the stack, to be set against a measured distribution of \(\rho\) (Section 9).

DeepSeek-V3.2 masks whole sequences with negative advantage whose divergence from the engine's returned \(\pi_{old}\) exceeds \(\delta\), reasoning that "highly off-policy negative samples can be detrimental"; \(\delta\) and any ablation are not given4 (§3.1). Because the engine's probability is the reference, the mask sees drift and mismatch together. Li et al. give a rationale for sequence-level masks: their bounds depend on the maximum token-level divergence, "a sequence-level quantity that cannot be controlled by token-independent methods like PPO clipping", and the classical \(O(T^2)\) growth falls to \(O(T^{3/2})\) and \(O(T)\). On Qwen3-8B-Base with vLLM and FSDP they report that PPO clipping at (0.8, 1.28) "exacerbates instability" against token-level IS, while masking with \(\delta=0.05\) (max divergence) or 0.001 (average) keeps the log-perplexity gap bounded10 (§1, §5, Fig. 1-2); authors' claims, one benchmark. Zhong et al. find that rejecting on the mismatch ratio \(\rho\) beats rejecting on the drift ratio, and that a token-level cap before the sequence-level rejection tracks the zero-mismatch run best6 (§4.2). The Miles post lists a veto (discard a sequence if any token's old probability falls below a threshold such as \(10^{-6}\)) and one MoE rescue, where an upper TIS bound of 2.0 collapsed and 1.5 did not, from a single trajectory in more than 300 runs9.

GSPO's sequence-level ratio averages token noise, and its paper argues that sequence likelihood tolerates training-inference precision differences, "intuitively", with no experiment (Module 12, Section 4.4)22 (§5.4). Qi et al. give partial support and a limit: in BF16 GSPO outlasted token-level TIS without using the engine's probabilities, then its gradient norm turned NaN after 1,200 steps in one framework2 (§4.2).

8. Why MoE and hybrid attention are hit harder

The Miles post puts typical K3 KL at \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-3}\) for dense models and \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-1}\) for MoE, two orders of magnitude more at the top of the range, and describes the mechanism as logit differences selecting different experts9. Qi et al. (§2.2) and Ling Team (§2.3.2) name routing as the aggravating factor28. It is a discrete amplifier: a perturbation of an ulp can flip a top-\(k\) boundary and change every downstream activation for that token (the writer's reading; Section 2.5), and the same logic covers the sparse-attention indexer (Section 6.2). Hybrid attention is the other precondition in the record: MiniMax-M1's gap appeared with lightning attention and not in smaller dense softmax-attention models3 (§3.2). Length multiplies all of them (Section 1.3), and multi-turn rollouts add the token round trip of Section 2.5 (Module 18). The counterweight is the Miles post's observation that its dense models never collapsed, so a dense softmax-attention stack may need only logging and a default mask, as in TRL915.

9. A procedure for a new architecture

This is the writer's synthesis of the reports' techniques, not a procedure any one report prescribes: log a gap statistic that reaches the tail, vary one factor at a time until the gap moves, and match the fix class to the factor that moved it.

Step 1 is a few lines. The function below takes the engine's returned log-probabilities of the sampled tokens (requested under the same temperature and truncation the sampler used, Section 2.4) and the trainer's log-probabilities for the same token ids, and returns the statistics of Table 1: mean and max \(|\delta|\), the K3 estimate of KL(engine‖trainer) on engine samples, the fraction outside a band, and the mean sequence log-ratio per thousand tokens, whose sign and size read like Qi et al.'s slope. TRL logs the first two when its correction is on14. Alert on the maximum and the outside-band fraction rather than the means (Section 1.2), and compare changes in them with the gradient norm, which moved before or with the reward in the Miles, Zhong and Ring-1T entries of Table 3.

import torch

def mismatch_report(lp_engine, lp_trainer, lengths, lo=0.5, hi=2.0):
    """lp_*: [B, T] log-probs of the sampled tokens, same token ids, same
    sampling transform on both sides; lengths: [B] valid token counts."""
    T = lp_engine.shape[1]
    valid = torch.arange(T, device=lp_engine.device)[None, :] < lengths[:, None]
    delta = (lp_trainer - lp_engine)[valid]          # delta_t = log rho_t
    rho = delta.exp()
    k3 = rho - 1.0 - delta                           # K3 estimate of KL(engine || trainer)
    seq = ((lp_trainer - lp_engine) * valid).sum(1)  # log rho(y) per sequence
    return {
        "abs_delta_mean": delta.abs().mean().item(),
        "abs_delta_max": delta.abs().max().item(),
        "k3_kl": k3.mean().item(),
        "frac_outside_band": ((rho < lo) | (rho > hi)).float().mean().item(),
        "mean_seq_logratio_per_1k_tokens": (seq / lengths * 1000).mean().item(),
    }

Steps 2 to 6 are single-factor experiments, each run in some form by a report: decoding one prompt at two batch sizes (Thinking Machines' test)1; re-running the trainer in FP32, or moving to FP16 as Qi et al. did, to see whether the gap tracks mantissa width2; turning the sampler's transforms off, as Qi et al. did for measurement; comparing expert ids or indexer index sets between engines; and diffing token ids after a text round trip5. If a first layer where hidden states diverge by more than rounding is visible, as in MiniMax-M1's layer-by-layer trace, the fix may be local, as the FP32 head was there3. Whatever moves the gap, the last box stays: Ling Team's limitations say IcePop leaves "underlying numerical discrepancies" as "a latent source of instability"8 (§5), and Zhong et al. find tuned corrections close to, not equal to, the zero-mismatch run6. Fix the source you can, and keep a threshold on \(\rho\) for what is left.

Where experts disagree

The reports agree that the engines disagree and that "same weights" is not "same policy". They disagree about where to spend the effort. Each position below is backed by runs on its own stack, and no report compares them at matched compute and architecture.

Correct it in the loss (family A and D)

Olmo 3 (token-level TIS, cap 2.0)16; TRL's default sequence-level masked IS15; Qwen's 30B MoE study7; IcePop in Ring-1T and GLM-585.

Engines and trainers will always differ, so make the estimator robust to it with a weight or a mask that works on any architecture.

  • No kernel work; one loss change, and IcePop stabilized a trillion-parameter run.
  • Zhong et al. find tuned corrections track the zero-mismatch run closely6.
  • Leaves the deployment gap: sequence-level MIS plateaued at 95% training accuracy against 99% in FP16 and 34% against 39% on AIME 20242.
  • Thresholds are stack-specific: a narrow band destabilized Ring-mini-2.08; the corrections discard signal and are "ad hoc" without a reference6.

Fix the numerics (family B)

Qi et al. (FP16 in both engines)2; MiniMax-M1 and ScaleRL (FP32 LM head)318.

The mismatch is rounding that accumulates; give the format more mantissa, or the head more precision, and the algorithmic patches become unnecessary.

  • No extra forward pass, no algorithm change; five algorithms become "almost indistinguishable" under FP162.
  • Closes the deployment gap the loss-side fixes cannot.
  • FP16 evidence is mostly one group's; range and loss-scaling limits at large scale, which the authors state2.
  • The FP32-head result splits: ScaleRL gains at the asymptote, while Qi et al. cite posts reporting it insufficient (second-hand)2. FP16 leaves KL 0.32, not 0.

Remove the source (family C)

Thinking Machines, vLLM and TorchTitan, SGLang, Miles120199; Zhong et al.'s VeXact6; DeepSeek-V3.2 and GLM-5 for routing, sampling masks and top-k45; DeepSeek-V4's kernels12.

If the two computations are bitwise identical there is nothing to correct, and the model you train is the model you serve.

  • Exactly on-policy: KL flat at 0, and a ground-truth reference for calibrating any correction6.
  • Reported overheads of 1.6x to 2.4x or 34.35%12019; invasive, covers dense models only in Miles, two copies of model code to keep in step920.
  • Routing replay can alter the target policy7.

Mostly benign on a well-built stack

The Miles and SGLang RL post9.

Across more than 300 runs the authors rarely saw a collapse, none in GLM 4.5 to 4.7 post-training, and treat their mitigations as a fail-safe for rare MoE cases.

  • Guards against over-engineering a dense, mature stack.
  • Own stack, unreviewed; dense collapses are reported with vLLM and FSDP by Qi et al., Zhong et al. and Thinking Machines261.
  • Its one collapse in 300 runs shows the tail is real.
Assessment

The mismatch alone, with everything else fixed, can collapse a run: three groups show it and Zhong et al. isolate it with a zero-mismatch control. How often it bites depends on the stack, so Miles's experience and Zhong's can both be true. The positions are layers, not rivals. On a dense softmax-attention stack, log the tail statistics of Section 9 and keep a default mask; if the tail grows, FP16 or an FP32 head is the cheapest lever, with no extra pass and an effect on the deployment gap. For MoE, share the routing and keep a correction, since replay alone changes the objective. For sparse-attention indexers, make the selection deterministic. Reserve bitwise engines for diagnosis and for scales where 1.6x to 2.4x is acceptable. One matched experiment would settle it: a single MoE, a zero-mismatch reference, each family alone and in pairs, and an evaluation on the serving engine. No report has run it.

What is changing

The vLLM and TorchTitan teams list a unified model definition and compilation support as next steps, to remove the fragility of two model copies20. TRL's main branch enables sequence-level masked IS by default and logs the gap15; read its values as a snapshot. DeepSeek-V4 aims at bitwise alignment between training and inference kernels12. Open: quantized rollouts (the evidence here is second-hand), hybrid and sparse attention beyond the two architectures reported, and whether thresholds can come from a bound such as Li et al.'s10 instead of trial. As of 2026-10-27; the Miles post is undated.
