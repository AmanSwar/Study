Reinforcement Learning for LLM Post-Training · Module 33

Choosing a Method: PPO, DPO, GRPO-Family, On-Policy Distillation, and Hybrids Under Reward, Data, Compute, and Stability Constraints

Each method in this course was built for a constraint: pairs without a reward model, a verifier without a critic budget, a teacher without RL compute. This module maps the constraints you have onto a starting method, states the evidence behind each choice with the stack, scale and strength it was measured at, says what would change it, and lists the choices that no source supports. Afterwards you can pick a starting method and the first three things to monitor for a given reward type, data, compute budget and target.

1. How to read a recommendation

Every recommendation in Sections 3 to 10 is a callout with the same parts: what to start with, the evidence and its tag, the stack and scale it was measured at, what would change it, and the first three signals to watch. The stack and scale come first because the reports differ by orders of magnitude and by engine. Tulu 3's PPO-versus-DPO test ran on two nodes1; Llama 3's DPO ran on models up to 405B2; VinePPO caps responses at 1,024 tokens8; ScaleRL spent more than 400,000 GPU-hours on dense and mixture-of-experts models of 8B to 17B-by-169. A margin measured in one of those settings does not transfer to another without a reason. Infrastructure and framework choice (Ray, Megatron, vLLM, the open-source RL libraries) is System Design Modules 36 to 38 and is not repeated here; this module treats the stack as the place where a result was measured, not as a decision.

1.1 Evidence tags

The tags are this module's rubric, not a convention in the literature. They count independent groups, not papers: Tulu 3 and Olmo 3 come from one lab, and Thinking Machines re-uses the Qwen3 table10, so neither pair counts twice. A tag attaches to a claim, so one method can carry several: DPO's cost advantage is concordant while its quality parity with PPO is conflicting.

  • concordant Two or more independent groups measured the same direction, on different stacks.
  • single One group measured it; nobody repeated it.
  • conflicting Measurements by different groups point in different directions.
  • adopted Several reports use the choice and none ablates it. Adoption is not measurement.
  • no source Nothing in the course's source map measures or states it. Any view I give there is labelled as mine.

2. Reward type and data: the first split

The reward source picks the family before anything else, because each family consumes a different object. DPO consumes (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples and does not generate during the update1. PPO, RLOO and GRPO consume prompts, generate on-policy samples and score them with a reward source; that generation is where the time goes, and in Olmo 3 the learner waits for rollouts 75% of the time5 (the report's footnote 41 gives 125 s of training in a 1,000 s step, which implies 87.5%; Module 1 flags the conflict, and both figures say generation dominates). On-policy distillation consumes prompts and a teacher's per-token log-probabilities and needs no reward function at all10.

3.1 The cost side is settled

Llama 3 and Tulu 3, two independent groups, report that PPO costs more for no gain in their setups21. Only Tulu 3 gives a number, and the arithmetic is short. PPO ran 28 h on two nodes, which is 56 node-hours; DPO ran 4 h on one node, which is 4 node-hours. The ratio is \(56/4 = 14\) in node-hours and \(28/4 = 7\) in wall-clock. At 8 GPUs per node, the node size the report states for its SFT runs, that is 448 against 32 GPU-hours (Module 26, derived). Figure 2 plots these next to the Qwen3 pair that Section 7 uses.

3.2 The quality side is not

The PPO-favoring studies and the DPO-favoring ones differ in what they tuned. Tulu 3's authors say their PPO had an untuned reward model and that "it is entirely possible that we can push up the PPO's performance even higher"1. Xu et al. tuned PPO heavily and compared it with DPO trained on the dataset's static correct and incorrect programs; their DPO produced "many meaningless code snippets" after one epoch3. Neither DPO there carries Llama 3's patches: the NLL term of coefficient 0.2, the masking of formatting tokens that stopped tail repetition, and fresh preference batches each round2 (Module 7 collects these). On a general chat target the PPO margin is up to 1.2%, against up to 8% for data quality20; on a code target with a test-suite reward the margin is 19.2 points at 34B (22.4 minus 3.2 for the better DPO variant, derived)3. Both statements are true. What moves the answer is whether the reward can be checked, not the algorithm's name.

Lambert's synthesis is the third view: "despite the simplicity of alternatives, RL-based methods continue to win out", at a far larger infrastructure cost, and RL can be surgical, since Tulu 3's math-only RL did not squash general capability22 (§3.1.6). That is a statement about verifiable skills, and Section 5 picks it up.

Recommendation: chat, instruction following, safety

Start with length-normalized DPO on pairs sampled from your latest model, in rounds. Llama 3's settings were learning rate \(10^{-5}\), \(\beta=0.1\), formatting tokens masked out of the loss and an NLL term of 0.2 on chosen sequences2; Tulu 3 found that only length-normalized DPO beat its base checkpoint overall among DPO, SimPO and length-normalized DPO1. Evidence: concordant on cost, conflicting on quality. Measured on: up to 405B for DPO; the PPO-favoring studies stop at 34B. Changes if: the target becomes a hard verifiable outcome (use a verifier, Section 5); you hold a reward model you trust and need the last one to two points (tuned PPO or RLOO); or you need a targeted gain in one checkable skill without disturbing the rest (RLVR on that skill). A matched-compute, matched-data study at 70B or above would settle the quality question; the source map has none (Module 7). First three monitors: the chosen responses' log-probability (a fall is the signal for the NLL term); response length against judged win-rate; a generation-based evaluation rather than pair accuracy.

4. Critic memory and tuning burden

4.1 What a critic costs

Mixed-precision Adam holds \(2\Psi + 2\Psi + K\Psi\) bytes per trained model with \(K=12\), which is \(16\Psi\) bytes for \(\Psi\) parameters23; a frozen model in half precision holds \(2\Psi\). PPO-RLHF trains a policy and a value model and keeps a frozen reference and reward model: \(16+16+2+2=36\Psi\), or 2,520 GB at 70B. PPO-RLVR as Tulu 3 ran it has no reward model: \(34\Psi\), 2,380 GB. GRPO with a verifier and \(\beta=0\) holds only the policy, \(16\Psi\), 1,120 GB, because TRL's default does not load the reference when the KL coefficient is 0.024 (weights and optimizer state only, derived; Module 1, Table 6 has the full list). The value model's share of PPO-RLHF is \(16/36=44\%\). ReMax measured 46% of GPU memory for the value model and its training state at 7B, and 4% for the reward model11; the derived reward-model share is \(2/36=5.6\%\). The arithmetic and the measurement agree to within two points, and the measurement includes activations.

4.2 What it buys, and why the sign flips

Three things separate the rows. The first is horizon. VinePPO's critic wins are on responses capped at 1,024 tokens8, and the only long chain-of-thought critic win needs seven modifications, among them value pretraining, decoupled GAE and a length-adaptive \(\lambda\); without value pretraining VAPO scores 11, and plain PPO scores 512. The second is tuning. R1's appendix reports that PPO at the common default \(\lambda=0.95\) "performs considerably worse than GRPO", and that only \(\lambda=1.0\) brings it "nearing that of GRPO"4 (Supp. A.3). A fixed \(\lambda=0.95\) multiplies the terminal reward by \(0.95^{100}\approx0.006\) for a token 100 steps from the end (derived from the argument in VAPO §3.112), which is why long-horizon critics learn almost nothing from the reward. The third is the baseline PPO that loses: in Ahmadian et al.'s run PPO's mean output was 16.5 tokens against 60.6 for RLOO on HH-Llama15, which looks like a collapsed run (my reading), so that margin shows PPO's fragility in a short, cheap setup and says little about a tuned critic.

Tulu 3 is outside the figure because it compared critics with each other. Its value model, initialized from a general reward model, gave the best GSM8K and average scores in its ablation of initializations, and the report lists GRPO as untested future work1 (§6.2.1, Limitations). VinePPO adds a middle path, Monte Carlo value estimates from the same states with no value network: 46.0 against PPO's 42.8 on 7B MATH, at up to 2 times the per-iteration cost at 7B8.

Recommendation: critic or no critic

Start with a group baseline (GRPO or RLOO) whenever a verifier exists and responses run past a few thousand tokens, with 8 or more samples per prompt; TRL's default group is 824. Evidence: conflicting; the group baseline wins on robustness and loses on a tuned short-response comparison. Measured on: critic wins at 7B with 1,024-token responses; group-baseline wins at a 16B mixture-of-experts, at 6.9B to 7B with 512-token contexts, and at 32B on long chains of thought. Changes if: you can pay for a policy-sized critic and apply VAPO's fixes (one documented 10.4-point AIME24 advantage over DAPO, unreplicated and counted in steps rather than GPU-hours), or the bottleneck is credit across many steps of an agent episode, where sampled credit is the more promising direction (Module 18). What would settle it is one base model, group-arm fixes from DAPO and Dr. GRPO, critic-arm fixes from VAPO, equal GPU-hours including the critic, at two response lengths; no source runs it (Module 9). First three monitors: response length (VAPO's plain PPO shrank responses until the model answered without reasoning); the share of zero-variance groups under a group baseline; training reward against a held-out score.

5.1 What each shared choice rests on

  • No KL. adopted Olmo 3 states that removal "does not lead to over-optimization or destabilized training" and shows no with-and-without run5. ProRL argues the opposite for a distilled 1.5B start, keeping KL with periodic resets17. Module 13's ledger finds no ablation behind any removal.
  • Clip-Higher, CISPO. single Each has a controlled comparison in its own paper (DAPO's ladder; ScaleRL's forward tests), with no cross-comparison on one codebase (Module 12).
  • Mismatch correction. concordant that it is needed: runs collapse without it in more than one group, and a zero-mismatch control isolates the cause2713. conflicting on how: loss-side weights, FP16 or an FP32 head, or deterministic kernels (Module 14).
  • Zero-variance filtering. concordant across DAPO, Olmo 3 and ScaleRL, which all drop dead groups1659.

Magistral and MiMo-V2-Flash are pointers only: neither report was read for this course, so no cell above rests on them (Modules 13 and 22 mention each).

5.2 Efficiency or ceiling

ScaleRL, the one study that fits compute curves, concludes that loss aggregation, normalization, curriculum and the off-policy algorithm "primarily modulate compute efficiency without materially shifting the asymptote", and its leave-one-out reversions all land within \(\pm0.02\) of the fitted ceiling \(A\) (Appendix A.8)9. The same paper is not consistent about the off-policy scheme: Appendix A.11 says the choice "affects the asymptotic performance" and not only the efficiency exponent, and Figure 6 reports that zero-variance filtering reaches a higher asymptote. Treat "details only move speed" as supported for loss aggregation and normalization on one 8B dense model, and not for filtering or the off-policy scheme (Module 15). Olmo 3 ablates only active sampling among its seven changes to GRPO (Module 31).

Recommendation: reasoning RL with a verifier

Start with the agreement set: 8 to 16 samples per prompt, token-level or prompt-level aggregation, \(\beta=0\), upper clip 0.28 or CISPO, mismatch correction on, zero-variance groups dropped; choose the advantage scale (batch-level, none, or group std) as the first ablation. Evidence: adopted for the set, single for individual pieces. Measured on: Qwen2.5-32B, Olmo 3 at 7B and 32B, 8B and 17B×16 MoE for ScaleRL, GLM-5 at 744B. Changes if: you start from a distilled checkpoint and run past 2,000 steps (KL with resets17); the model is a mixture of experts (routing replay or a sequence-level ratio, Modules 12 and 14); the attention uses a sparse indexer (GLM-5 found that non-deterministic CUDA and TileLang top-k caused drastic degradation after a few RL steps, with an entropy drop, and that torch.topk fixed it13); responses reach tens of thousands of tokens (MiniMax-M1 combined sample-level loss with token-level normalization and truncated repetitive generations28); or the reward includes a soft judge, where a nonzero KL is the safer starting point (my reading of V3.2's domain-dependent strengths). First three monitors: the share of zero-variance groups (TRL logs frac_reward_zero_std); entropy and response length, with the truncated share; the log-probability gap between sampler and trainer, alerting on the maximum and not the mean (TRL logs both)24.

6. Agentic coding and tool use

Agentic RL is verifier RL plus three costs: the rollout is a multi-turn episode in a sandbox, its length varies by an order of magnitude inside one batch, and the sampler and trainer must agree on the exact tokens. The generation bill is the first-order cost. Olmo 3's 32B run used 8 H100 nodes for training and 20 for inference; its text says inference takes about 5 times the GPU use of training, but 20 nodes against a learner busy 25% of the time gives 10, while the 7B figure of about 14 does follow (Module 1 flags both)5. 7.1 A worked cost per point, and what the teacher would have to cost

Qwen3's ratio is 17,920/1,800 = 9.96. A fairer unit is GPU-hours per point of gain, since distillation also gained more. From the 55.0 starting score, RL gained 12.6 points (67.6 minus 55.0) and distillation 19.4 (74.4 minus 55.0). Per point, RL cost \(17{,}920/12.6=1{,}422\) GPU-hours and distillation \(1{,}800/19.4=92.8\), a ratio of 15.3 (derived). Now charge the teacher. Distillation matches RL's cost per point when \((1{,}800+T)/19.4 = 1{,}422\), so \(T\approx25{,}800\) GPU-hours, 14.3 times the distillation run itself. The report states neither the hardware nor whether the teacher is inside the 1,800, and the teacher is the product of four RL-shaped stages that Qwen3 never prices. It does serve six lightweight students (five dense models and one mixture-of-experts)6, so the calculator lets you spread a guessed teacher cost over \(n\) of them. A matched-compute study with the teacher counted, several seeds and pass@k over more than 30 problems would settle RL against distillation; the source map has none (Module 21). First three monitors: pass@k at large \(k\) beside pass@1, as Qwen3 reports pass@64; the per-token reverse KL to the teacher on the student's own samples; held-out domains for regression.

9. Several skills in one model

Reasoning, agent, chat and safety skills interfere when trained together, and the reports answer in three ways (Figure 4). V3.2 merges reasoning, agent and alignment into one mixed RL stage to avoid "the catastrophic forgetting issues commonly associated with multi-stage training paradigms"26. GLM-5 runs three RL stages in sequence and ends with on-policy cross-stage distillation from its own earlier checkpoints, because "sequentially optimizing for distinct objectives can lead to the cumulative degradation of previously acquired capabilities"13 (§3.5). Kimi K3, DeepSeek-V4 and Nemotron 3 Ultra train specialists and distill them on the student's own rollouts: nine experts for K3 (three domains at three effort levels), more than ten for V4 and for Nemotron193225. Lambert's survey records the shift, notes that MiMo-V2-Flash documented it first, and writes that "labs are far from unanimous in its usage"22 (§3.2.4).

10. Calibration and abstention

A binary correctness reward pays for guessing: it does not penalize low-confidence wrong answers, so RLVR can degrade calibration14, and under 0-1 grading an abstention is strictly suboptimal33. The public repairs act at different layers (Table 9). The strongest training result is a bounded proper scoring rule added to the verifier reward: RLCR's reward is \(R=\mathbb 1[y=y^*]-(q-\mathbb 1[y=y^*])^2\), where \(q\) is the model's stated confidence, and any bounded proper rule gives both a calibration incentive and a correctness incentive, while the log score is unbounded and can reward wrong answers14 (§3). The theorem's appendix proof carries a sign slip that I do not rely on; Module 24 checks the claim separately.

12. What the evidence supports, and what it does not

Supported by at least two independent groups. Offline DPO costs far less than PPO for chat tuning, 14 times the node-hours in the one measured pair, and Llama 3 reports the same direction12. Data quality moves preference tuning more than the algorithm205. A teacher's signal beats RL for a small student on cost and pass@1 in each direct comparison46. A mismatch between rollout and trainer engines can collapse a run and must be handled2713.

Supported by one controlled study, or by adoption alone. Specialists plus MOPD beating mixed RL (one study, three domains). The group-baseline default for long chains of thought (adopted; the one critic win there needs seven fixes). Dropping KL (adopted; no with-and-without run). A Brier term for calibration (one paper, 7B). Rubric judging (design statements, no hack-rate ablation).

Not supported by anything read. That any algorithm in this module is best at frontier scale: the frontier reports ablate recipes against their own earlier versions, not against rivals. That RL "expands" capability, or that distillation does so more cheaply, beyond the one-question margins in Table 6 (Module 20). That any teacher cost, hardware, or seed variance is known for the Qwen3 pair. Anything about how TypeSafe's RLCD trains. The numbers are also dated: TRL's defaults, report versions and a V4 identifier that conflicts with its own date will move, and Module 34 lists what to re-check first.

Where experts disagree

The five disputes below are the ones that decide a starting method. Each is treated at length in the module named; here each is reduced to the condition under which one side is the better start.

A. Is DPO enough, or does online RL win for preference tuning? (Modules 7, 26)

DPO in rounds is enough

Llama 3 team; Tulu 3 authors21

Comparable score at 14 times fewer node-hours; spend the saving on data.

  • Measured cost; used to 405B.
  • Untuned PPO; no PPO numbers published by Meta.

Tuned online RL wins

Xu et al.; Ivison et al.; Tang et al.32021

PPO is ahead everywhere tested, most of all with a checkable reward.

  • Three groups agree on direction.
  • Margins up to 1.2% on chat against up to 8% for data.
Assessment

Both hold in their regimes. Without a checkable reward, patched DPO is the efficient start; with one, DPO's 0.0% on CodeContests settles it for RL. A matched 70B study would settle the chat case.

B. Is a value model worth it? (Module 9)

No critic

Ahmadian et al.; DeepSeek; most open reasoning recipes154

Last-token rewards give a critic little to learn; the group is a free baseline.

  • Saves about 44% of state in PPO-RLHF (derived).
  • Behind a tuned critic by 3.6 to 6.4 points at 7B.

A built critic has the higher ceiling

VinePPO; VAPO; Tulu 38121

Per-step credit and lower variance repay the cost once the critic is fixed.

  • 60.4 against DAPO's 50 on AIME24.
  • Seven fixes; plain PPO scored 5; unreplicated.
Assessment

Group baseline for long chains with a limited engineering budget; a critic only with VAPO-grade fixes or when credit across steps is the bottleneck. R1's \(\lambda\) result shows the gap is partly tuning.

C. How should the GRPO loss and advantage be normalized? (Module 11)

Remove the biases: no std, constant or token-level loss

Dr. GRPO; Olmo 3; V3.2 on the advantage38526

Per-response length and std division reweight the objective.

  • Unbiased; fewer knobs.
  • Small-scale evidence; reward scale sets step size.

Normalization changes speed, not the ceiling

ScaleRL9

Choose prompt-level averaging and batch-level scale; spend effort on loss type and numerics.

  • Reversions within \(\pm0.02\) of the ceiling.
  • One 8B model; its own A.11 and filtering results move the ceiling.
Assessment

Fix the length term first, since it costs nothing; do not spend an ablation budget on the std question before loss type and mismatch. The grid that would settle it does not exist.

D. Mixed RL, stages, or specialists plus distillation? (Module 22)

One mixed stage

DeepSeek-V3.2; Qwen3's last stage266

One trainer, one recipe, no forgetting by construction.

  • Flattest non-distillation baseline (0.882).
  • Per-domain signal dilutes as environments multiply.

Specialists, then MOPD

MOPD paper; Kimi K3; DeepSeek-V4; Nemotron7193225

Train each skill to its peak, then merge on the student's rollouts.

  • 0.937 and the flattest profile.
  • Needs same-origin teachers; HLE recovery 16.9%; one controlled study.
Assessment

The count of domains and the availability of parallel teams decide it. "Specialists win at frontier scale" is an inference from adoption, as Lambert's "far from unanimous" implies22.

E. RL or distillation for a strong small reasoning model? (Module 21)

Distill first

DeepSeek-R1 authors; Qwen3 team46

A dense teacher signal gives higher pass@1 at about a tenth of the GPU-hours.

  • Direction agrees across off- and on-policy variants.
  • Teacher cost uncounted; single runs.

RL remains the path past the teacher

R1 authors, same appendix; ProRL417

Distillation cannot exceed its teacher; prolonged RL from a distilled start reports gains.

  • The only route beyond the frontier.
  • About 16,000 GPU-hours at 1.5B; no matched OPD arm.
Assessment

Distillation copies a capability; RL is the option when no stronger teacher exists. Cost alone does not separate them once the teacher is charged (Section 7.1).
