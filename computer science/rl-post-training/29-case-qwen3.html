Reinforcement Learning for LLM Post-Training · Module 29

Case Study: Qwen3: Four-Stage Reasoning Pipeline, 3,995 Query-Verifier Pairs, Thinking-Mode Fusion, and Strong-to-Weak Distillation

Qwen3 (April and May 2025) documents its post-training in about three pages: a deliberately small cold start, one reasoning-RL stage on 3,995 query-verifier pairs, a fusion step that puts thinking and non-thinking behavior into one set of weights, a general-RL stage over more than 20 tasks, and, for six smaller models, distillation in place of all four. This module reads each stage with the report's own numbers, sets the pipeline beside R1's, and lists what the report does not print. Afterwards you can say why Qwen3 distilled its small models, how small its RL set is, what the later stages cost on hard reasoning benchmarks, and which claims about the recipe rest on a table and which on a sentence.

1.1 Two objectives, two routes

The report's post-training section opens with two goals. The first is thinking control: a "non-thinking" and a "thinking" mode in one model, with a user-set token budget for the thinking. The second is strong-to-weak distillation: use the large models to cut the cost of building the small ones1. The flagship models, Qwen3-235B-A22B (235B parameters, 22B activated per token) and the dense Qwen3-32B, "follow a sophisticated four-stage training process". The lightweight models, dense 0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B and 14B plus the 30B-A3B MoE, skip those stages and are distilled from the flagships1 (§1, §4, §4.5, Figure 1). The first two stages build the thinking ability; the last two add the non-thinking one1.

Three dates frame how to read it. Qwen's release post is dated 29 April 20252. The technical report went to arXiv on 14 May 2025, and the arXiv listing shows no later version as of 2026-10-271. The report therefore describes the April 2025 models only; the last section of this module covers what Qwen shipped afterwards.

1.2 Where it comes from

The nearest ancestor is Qwen's own QwQ-32B, announced on 6 March 2025. Its blog describes a two-stage RL run from a cold-start checkpoint. The first stage covers math and code only, with an accuracy verifier for math answers and a code execution server that runs predefined test cases, and no learned reward model. The second stage targets general capabilities with "rewards from general reward model and some rule-based verifiers", and the post says it used "a small amount of steps"3. Qwen3's stage 2 and stage 4 have the same shape, a verifier-only reasoning stage followed by a broader stage that admits learned rewards. That lineage is my reading; the Qwen3 report does not claim it. The documented link is more direct: QwQ-32B generates the candidate responses for Qwen3's cold-start data and is the main RL-trained baseline in the 32B-class tables1 (§4.1, Tables 13 and 15).

1.3 What the report offers as evidence

The training description is about three pages (§4.1 to §4.5, pp. 9 to 12); the rest of §4 is evaluation1. Four results carry everything this module can say quantitatively:

  • One sentence on the flagship's reasoning RL: AIME'24 rises from 70.1 to 85.1 over 170 steps (§4.2).
  • Table 22, which follows Qwen3-32B through Stages 2, 3 and 4 on 13 benchmarks (§4.7).
  • Table 21, which compares RL with on-policy distillation on Qwen3-8B from the same starting checkpoint (§4.7).
  • Tables 11 to 20, which score the final models against baselines (§4.6).

There are no RL training curves, no per-stage table for the 235B flagship, and no ablation of the 3,995-pair choice, the cold-start size or any reward type. Where a mechanism has its own module, this one points to it instead of re-deriving it: GRPO is Module 9, reward routing and judges Module 16, on-policy distillation and the cost arithmetic of Table 21 Module 21, the chat-template and budget mechanics of fusion Module 23.

2. Stage 1: a long-CoT cold start that is a filter, not a training run

The cold-start stage is described almost entirely as data selection. The dataset spans math, code, logical reasoning and general STEM, and every problem is paired with verified reference answers or code-based test cases1 (§4.1). Two filters build it.

Query filtering. Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct removes queries that are not easily verifiable, namely those with several sub-questions or those asking for general text generation. It also removes queries it can answer correctly without chain-of-thought reasoning, which the report says "helps prevent the model from relying on superficial guessing". It labels each query's domain so the mix stays balanced, and a validation query set is held out before any generation1 (§4.1).

Response filtering. QwQ-32B generates \(N\) candidate responses per remaining query. When QwQ-32B "consistently fails", human annotators assess the responses. For queries with a positive pass@\(N\), six defects are filtered out: wrong final answers, substantial repetition, guesswork without adequate reasoning, inconsistency between the thinking and the summary, inappropriate language mixing or stylistic shifts, and suspected overlap with validation items1 (§4.1).

The stated purpose explains the stage's size policy. The objective is "to instill foundational reasoning patterns in the model without overly emphasizing immediate reasoning performance", so that the model's potential "is not limited" before RL; the report therefore prefers to "minimize both the number of training samples and the training steps"1 (§4.1). The stage is under-trained on purpose. It is the format-and-style prior that Stage 2 then optimizes, and the report gives neither \(N\), nor the number of examples, nor the step count, nor the learning rate.

Two comparisons with R1 are worth holding. R1 also cold-starts, with "thousands" of long chain-of-thought examples, and gives a product reason: it wanted the reasoning to read in the first person, to begin by comprehending the problem, and to keep one language4 (§3, Supp. B.3.2). Qwen3 handles the same symptom, language mixing, on the data side: item five of the response filter removes it from the cold-start traces. R1 additionally added a language-consistency term to the RL reward, which its own ablation says costs a slight performance drop4 (§3.2.1). Second, the 235B-A22B flagship's cold start imitates a 32B dense predecessor. The stage-1 description names no other generator, so the first stage of the largest model is a distillation step from a smaller reasoning specialist (my reading of §4.1).

3. Stage 2: reasoning RL on 3,995 query-verifier pairs

3.1 Four criteria, one number

The RL set has to satisfy four criteria. The pairs were not used in the cold-start phase; they are learnable for the cold-start model; they are as challenging as possible; and they cover a broad range of sub-domains. The paragraph ends: "We ultimately collect a total of 3,995 query-verifier pairs"1 (§4.2). Figure 2 draws the data gates of both stages. The end count is the only number the report prints for either: the pool before selection, the cold-start subset size, and any pass-rate threshold behind "learnable" and "as challenging as possible" are not given.

Read together, criteria 2 and 3 bound the same band that Module 10 describes for prompt selection and that Module 11 explains through zero-variance groups: a prompt the cold-start model never solves, or always solves, gives every rollout in a group the same reward and so contributes no group-relative gradient. This is the writer's reading; the report states the criteria without a rationale.

The scale is the notable part. R1's reasoning RL data is 26K math, 17K code, 22K STEM and 15K logic prompts, 80K in all, plus 66K general prompts4 (Supp. Table 4); the text of Supp. B.3.1 adds 8K bug-fixing problems to the 17K algorithm questions, which would make 88K. \(80{,}000 / 3{,}995 = 20.0\) and \(88{,}000 / 3{,}995 = 22.0\), so the R1 set is about twenty times larger (derived; R1's counts are rounded to thousands). Qwen3 chose curation over volume, with the criteria above as the only filter on record.

3.2 What the report says about the optimizer

Four statements, all from §4.21:

  • The algorithm is GRPO, cited to Shao et al. (2024)5; Module 9 derives it.
  • The authors observe that "using a large batch size and a high number of rollouts per query, along with off-policy training to improve sample efficiency, is beneficial to the training process."
  • The authors "addressed how to balance exploration and exploitation by controlling the model's entropy to increase steadily or remain stable". That is a target; Module 13 notes that no method for reaching it is given.
  • The result is "consistent improvements in both training reward and validation performance over the course of a single RL run, without any manual intervention on hyperparameters".

No batch size, rollout count, degree of off-policyness, learning rate, KL coefficient, clip range or maximum length is printed. One later paper, ScaleRL, reads more into the report than it says, twice. It states that the PPO-off-policy-\(k\) scheme "has been used previously by Qwen3"6 (§3.1); the Qwen3 text says only "off-policy training to improve sample efficiency" and defines no scheme or \(k\). ScaleRL's appendix also lists Qwen3 among works that use interruptions to stop over-long generations during RL6 (App. A.10); the only interruption in the Qwen3 report is the stop-thinking string appended at inference time, which the report says "is not explicitly trained"1 (§4.3). I could not find an RL-time interruption in the report text. Treat both attributions as second-hand readings.

3.3 The one curve, and how many passes it implies

The report's only flagship RL measurement is one sentence: "the AIME'24 score of the Qwen3-235B-A22B model increases from 70.1 to 85.1 over a total of 170 RL training steps"1 (§4.2). That is \(85.1-70.1 = 15.0\) points, 0.088 points per step on average (derived). The start is not labelled; by its place in the pipeline it is the cold-start checkpoint. A "step" is not defined, and with several minibatches per rollout the number of rollout batches and the number of optimizer updates differ by that factor (Module 1).

The number of unknowns matters because "3,995 pairs" is only small if the batches are. With \(P\) prompts per step and \(S\) steps, the run makes \(P\cdot S\) prompt draws and \(P\cdot S/3{,}995\) passes over the set. R1-Zero drew 32 unique questions per step4 (§2.1); at \(P=32\), 170 steps is 5,440 draws, 1.36 passes. At \(P=128\) it is 21,760 draws, 5.45 passes. The report does not say which regime it is in, and so does not say whether the set is seen about once or more than five times.

One more reading error to avoid. The final Qwen3-235B-A22B scores 85.7 on AIME'24 in thinking mode1 (Table 11), against 85.1 after reasoning RL. The two numbers sit in different parts of the report, and the report does not say that 85.1 was scored under Table 11's protocol (64 samples per question, 38,912 maximum output tokens for AIME). A +0.6 difference is therefore not evidence that Stages 3 and 4 were free at 235B scale. The only stage-wise measurement is the 32B model's, where AIME'24 goes from 83.8 to 81.4 (Section 5.4).

Caveat: GRPO in the report, GSPO in a later paper

The Qwen3 report names GRPO and predates GSPO; its arXiv listing has only v1 (14 May 2025)1. The GSPO paper from the same team (v2, 28 July 2025) says "we have successfully applied GSPO to the RL training of the latest Qwen3 models"7 (§5.1), without naming models, stages or dates. Both can be true: the April 2025 models used GRPO as documented, and models released later may have used GSPO. Neither document says which checkpoint or which stage used which. Do not cite the Qwen3 report for GSPO, and do not read the GSPO paper's sentence as independent confirmation, since it comes from the same team. Module 12 covers the algorithm.

4. Stage 3: thinking-mode fusion, read for its data

The mechanics of fusion, meaning the /think and /no think flags, the empty think block kept in non-thinking samples, the rule that in a multi-turn chat the model follows the last flag, and the stop-thinking string that implements the budget, are Module 23 §5's subject. This section stays with what the stage does to the pipeline's data.

Fusion is "continual supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the Reasoning RL model"1 (§4.3). Its dataset has two halves with different origins. The thinking half is produced by rejection sampling on Stage 1 queries "using the Stage 2 model itself", so that the Stage 2 model's performance "is not compromised by the additional SFT". The non-thinking half is "carefully curated" across coding, mathematics, instruction following, multilingual tasks, creative writing, question answering and role-playing, scored with automatically generated checklists, with the share of translation tasks raised to help low-resource languages. The report gives no sample counts, no thinking-to-non-thinking ratio and no epochs.

Three readings follow, all the writer's. First, the thinking half is the policy's own filtered output, so it asks the model to change little in thinking behavior; the non-thinking half carries the new behavior. That is a regularizer by construction, and Table 22 measures how well it works: in thinking mode this stage alone moves AIME'24 from 83.8 to 81.9 and LiveCodeBench v5 from 68.4 to 67.21 (Table 22, printed deltas of -1.9 and -1.2). Second, fusion puts an off-policy step between the two RL stages, so Stage 4 starts from an SFT checkpoint and not from the RL one; the report does not discuss what that does to the entropy that Stage 2 managed. Third, the checklist grading of non-thinking data is the report's only rubric-style filter; Module 16 covers rubrics as rewards, and here they select SFT data.

The budget ability is described as "not explicitly trained but emerges naturally as a result of applying Thinking Mode Fusion"1 (§4.3). Module 23 weighs that claim against designs that train the budget.

5. Stage 4: general RL over more than 20 tasks

5.1 Capabilities, tasks and where the stage sits

The stage "aims to broadly enhance the models' capabilities and stability across diverse scenarios", with "a sophisticated reward system covering over 20 distinct tasks, each with customized scoring criteria"1 (§4.4). The report lists five target capability groups and not the tasks themselves:

  • Instruction following: content, format, length and structured-output requirements.
  • Format following: responding to the /think and /no think flags by switching modes, and using the <think> and </think> tokens consistently. This is the RL stage that polishes the mode switching fusion installed (Module 23 gives the ThinkFollow numbers).
  • Preference alignment: helpfulness, engagement and style on open-ended queries.
  • Agent ability: invoking tools through designated interfaces, trained with multi-turn rollouts against real environment feedback.
  • Specialized scenarios: for example retrieval-augmented generation, where reward signals aim to reduce hallucination.

The MOPD paper files this report under "mixed RL": samples from different domains in one training batch, each still scored by its own per-domain reward and advantage8 (§1). Qwen3 is both things at once, a cascade across stages (reasoning RL, then an SFT step, then general RL) and a mixture inside Stage 4. Module 22 compares mixed RL, cascades and multi-teacher distillation; the Qwen3 report itself offers no comparison among them.

5.2 Three reward types, and how little of the map is printed

The report names three reward types1 (§4.4), each with the reason it gives:

  1. Rule-based. Described as "also useful for general tasks such as instruction following (Lambert et al., 2024) and format adherence"; "well-designed rule-based rewards can assess the correctness of model outputs with high precision, preventing issues like reward hacking". The citation is Tulu 3, whose RLVR stage verifies IFEval-style constraints9 (§1).
  2. Model-based, with a reference answer. Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct scores the response against the reference. The stated benefit is "more flexible handling of diverse tasks without requiring strict formatting, avoiding false negatives that can occur with purely rule-based rewards".
  3. Model-based, without a reference. A reward model trained on human preference data assigns scalar scores. It "does not depend on a reference answer", handles "a broader range of queries" and enhances "engagement and helpfulness".

The matrix is sparse because the report is. It does not say which of the 20-plus tasks uses which type, how a 0/1 rule reward, a judge score on an unstated scale and an RM scalar are combined or normalized, what the RM's size or training data are, what the judge prompt looks like, or how many steps the stage ran. Module 16 covers what such a router has to settle (the judge's failure modes, reference-conditioned scoring and rubric alternatives), and Module 19 the hacking that a scalar RM invites.

R1's authors reach the first two types independently: rule-based reward models and LLMs that judge correctness "against a predefined ground-truth" are, in their words, "robust mechanisms for mitigating issues related to reward hacking", with the LLM route limited to well-defined, concise answers4 (Supp. G.1). For open-ended data R1 used reward models too, and capped their exposure: preference reward entered only in the last 400 of 1,700 second-stage steps, because longer exposure "may lead to reward hacking"4 (§3.2.2). Qwen3 prints no equivalent exposure figure, no hacking observation and no cap on the preference RM (type 3). It also scores Stage 4 with a fixed, previous-generation judge, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, against policies up to 235B parameters; whether that judge's ceiling bound the stage is not discussed.

5.3 Agent training: one sentence of mechanism

The report's whole description of agent RL is one sentence: "During the RL rollout, the model is allowed to perform complete multi-turn interaction cycles with real environment execution feedback, thereby improving its performance and stability in long-horizon decision-making tasks"1 (§4.4). It names no environment, tool set, turn cap, reward definition or sandbox. Module 17 lists what an environment contract has to specify, and Module 18 how multi-turn credit assignment works; neither can be filled in from this report.

The measurements are in Table 22. The public tool-calling benchmark BFCL v3 moves from 68.4 to 70.3 in thinking mode at Stage 4 (+1.9) and from 61.5 to 63.0 in non-thinking mode (+1.5). The in-house ToolUse benchmark, which scores "intent recognition, format accuracy, and parameter accuracy" across single-turn, multi-turn and multi-step calls, moves from 70.4 to 85.5 (+15.1) and from 73.2 to 86.5 (+13.3)1 (Table 22). The two largest Stage 4 gains in thinking mode, ToolUse (+15.1) and ThinkFollow (+10.2), are both on in-house benchmarks the report built to monitor the capabilities that stage trains. That does not show overfitting; the in-house sets measure format and stability behaviors BFCL may not. It does mean the biggest agent number has no external check.

5.4 What Stages 3 and 4 bought, and what they cost

Table 22 is the report's only stage-by-stage measurement, and it is for the dense 32B model, not the flagship. Figure 4 redraws its thinking-mode columns as the change at each stage and the total from Stage 2 to Stage 4.

Four things follow. The scores and deltas are the report's; the sums, shares and counts below are derived from them.

  • The gains are in what the stages train. From Stage 2 to Stage 4: ToolUse +22.2, CounterFactQA +17.7, IFEval +12.0, Multi-IF +11.6, LengthCtrl +10.9, Arena-Hard +7.0, LiveBench +6.3. The public agent benchmark BFCL v3 gains +1.3.
  • The costs are on the hardest reasoning benchmarks. AIME'24 falls 2.4 points (83.8 to 81.4) and LiveCodeBench v5 2.7 (68.4 to 65.7); MMLU-Redux and GPQA-Diamond move by -0.5 and -0.4. The report conjectures the cause is training on "a broader range of general tasks" and states that it chose to accept the trade-off for versatility1 (§4.7); Module 23 quotes the passage.
  • Most of the AIME'24 loss arrives at the SFT step, not the RL step. Stage 3 contributes -1.9 of the -2.4, 79% (derived); for LiveCodeBench v5, -1.2 of -2.7, 44%. The report attributes the drop to "these two training stages" together and offers no split; its own table supports one. With one run per cell, differences under a point should not carry an argument.
  • In non-thinking mode, Stage 4 costs almost nothing. Between Stage 3 and Stage 4 eleven of twelve shared rows rise (AIME'24 +2.5, GPQA-Diamond +4.3, ToolUse +13.3) and only MMLU-Redux falls (-1.0)1 (Table 22). The regression is a thinking-mode phenomenon.

For scale on the benchmark itself: AIME has 30 questions per year and the report averages 64 samples per question1 (§4.6), so one question's worth of accuracy is \(100/30 = 3.33\) points. A 2.4-point change is 0.72 of one question's worth (derived), and the report prints no seeds or intervals; Module 20 treats how much such differences can bear.

6. Strong-to-weak distillation: the route for six models

6.1 Two phases, no RL

The distillation pipeline covers five dense models (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B) and the 30B-A3B MoE1 (§4.5). It has two phases.

  1. Off-policy distillation. The outputs of teacher models, generated in both /think and /no think modes, are combined for response distillation. The report says this gives the students "basic reasoning skills and the ability to switch between different modes of thinking".
  2. On-policy distillation. In the report's words, "prompts are sampled, and the student model produces responses in either /think or /no think mode. The student model is then fine-tuned by aligning its logits with those of a teacher model (Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B) to minimize the KL divergence."

The motivation is a result from "preliminary experiments": directly distilling output logits into lightweight students improves performance while "maintaining fine-grained control over their reasoning processes", and it removes the need to run the four-stage process for each small model. The introduction states the finding bluntly: "Distillation from advanced teacher models significantly outperforms reinforcement learning in performance and training efficiency"1 (§1, §4). Module 21 derives the objective. The report does not say which teacher serves which student, the KL direction, the estimator, the number of prompts or steps, or whether the off-policy phase filters teacher outputs for correctness.

One consequence deserves stating. The six students never see an RL reward. Their thinking behavior, non-thinking behavior and mode switching all come from teacher signals, so the Stage 3 and Stage 4 trade-offs of Section 5.4 are not measured for them; the report has no stage-wise table for any small model.

6.2 What the "1/10" figure measures

The report writes a GPU-hour ratio of about one tenth twice, for two different comparisons. In the §4 introduction, distillation needs "only 1/10 of the GPU hours compared to the four-stage training method". In §4.7, on-policy distillation reaches higher scores than direct RL "while requiring approximately only 1/10 of the GPU hours", and Table 21 gives the numbers: starting from the same off-policy-distilled Qwen3-8B checkpoint, on math and code queries only, the RL arm used 17,920 GPU-hours and the on-policy-distillation arm 1,8001 (Table 21, p. 21; the two figures appear only in the table's GPU Hours column, and no sentence of the text states them). Module 21 §4.3 has the arithmetic (a ratio of 9.96 from the printed numbers), the observation that the passage gives no hardware and does not say whether the teacher is included in the 1,800, and the per-point costs. Module 20 covers how pass@64 should be estimated; the report does not say how it did, and the pass@64 gain for on-policy distillation is exactly one question on each AIME set (Module 21).

Three further points concern what the ratio is a ratio of.

  • The introduction's 1/10 has no printed support. The report gives no GPU-hours for the four-stage route, for any of its stages, or for the off-policy phase. Only the §4.7 comparison has numbers.
  • The §4.7 ratio compares two continuations. Both arms start from the off-policy-distilled checkpoint, so that phase's cost, and the cost of generating teacher outputs for it, sits in neither 17,920 nor 1,800. The table's off-policy row has a dash in the GPU-hours column. It is not a measurement of the whole small-model recipe against the whole flagship recipe.
  • Table 21's final row is not the shipped model. The released Qwen3-8B scores 76.0 on AIME'24 and 67.3 on AIME'25 in thinking mode1 (Table 17), against 74.4 and 65.5 for the Table 21 distillation row. Table 21 restricts to "math and code-related queries" for simplicity; the report does not say how the experiment relates to the released checkpoint.

The Thinking Machines post builds on this table. It takes the RL cost from the report and starts from its own SFT baseline of 60% on AIME'24, against 55.0 for the off-policy row here10; Module 21 compares the two accountings.

Four cautions bound any attribution to the on-policy phase. First, the baselines are SFT-only models, since R1's authors "apply only SFT and do not include an RL stage"4 (Supp. F), so the comparison is partly SFT-only against SFT plus on-policy distillation; but bases, teachers, data and sizes all differ too. Second, the baselines' AIME'24 and GPQA-Diamond scores in Tables 15, 17 and 19 (AIME'24 28.9, 50.4, 69.7, 72.6; GPQA-Diamond 33.8, 49.0, 59.1, 62.1) equal, to the decimal, those DeepSeek printed for the same models4 (Supp. Table 15), which suggests they were carried over rather than re-run. Their LiveCodeBench values were not: the report's 54.5 for R1-Distill-Qwen-32B against DeepSeek's 57.2, and 45.5 against 53.1 for the 14B, are different measurements on a different window (Qwen's 2024.10 to 2025.02 against DeepSeek's 2024-08 to 2025-01), and DeepSeek's table has no AIME'25 column at all. The Qwen3 report does not say which baseline cells were taken and which were run, so a mixed protocol is possible. Third, the Qwen3 students are scored at 32,768 maximum output tokens (38,912 for AIME), and the baselines' settings are not stated1 (§4.6). Fourth, every cell is one run.

6.4 The parity with QwQ-32B

The first row of Table 1 is the result the report highlights: Qwen3-30B-A3B "achieves comparable performance to QwQ-32B with a smaller model size and less than 1/10 activated parameters"1 (§4.6). QwQ-32B is an RL-trained model3, and the distilled MoE matches it within 1.4 points on all four benchmarks. For decode, the arithmetic is direct: weights read per generated token at batch 1 in BF16 are about \(3\times10^9 \times 2\ \mathrm{B} = 6\) GB for 3B activated parameters against \(32\times10^9\times 2\ \mathrm{B} = 64\) GB for 32B (derived; this ignores the KV cache and the larger set of experts touched at bigger batch sizes). It is not a controlled RL-versus-distillation experiment, since base model, data, size and architecture all differ. It does show what a distilled MoE can reach against an RL-trained dense model of the previous generation, at about a tenth of the activated weight traffic.

7. Qwen3 beside R1: one skeleton, different choices

Module 27 reads R1 in full. Table 2 lists only what the comparison needs. The skeleton is shared: a small long-CoT cold start, a verifier-driven reasoning RL stage, an SFT step built by rejection sampling from that RL model, a broader final RL stage that admits learned rewards, and distilled small models. Three further statements from the Qwen3-8B card matter to a runtime. "DO NOT use greedy decoding, as it can lead to performance degradation and endless repetitions". In multi-turn conversations "the historical model output should only include the final output part", with the provided chat template handling that stripping. And enable_thinking defaults to true; with it set to false the model emits no think block and the /think and /no_think soft switches "are not valid"11.

Three consequences are the writer's reading, not claims of the sources. One set of weights now serves two output-length profiles, so a scheduler that mixes the modes sees a bimodal length distribution, the situation Module 1's static-batching waste arithmetic describes. Stripping earlier thinking from the history means the prompt for turn \(n+1\) no longer contains the thinking tokens whose KV entries were computed while generating turn \(n\), so prefix-cache reuse across turns stops where the previous assistant message begins. And the budget, which the report implements by halting the thinking at a threshold and appending a stop string1 (§4.3), is a serving-time action: the runtime must inject text mid-decode and continue from the extended prefix. The 2507 releases ship one mode per checkpoint, which turns the first issue from a scheduling problem into a deployment choice between two models (last section).

Where experts disagree

Qwen3 sits inside four live disputes. Each is stated below as its own set of positions, with the Qwen3 evidence placed where it belongs and no more weight than it can bear.

Disagreement 1: RL or distillation for strong small reasoning models?

Distill: a teacher signal beats RL for the student

Qwen3 team1; DeepSeek-R1 authors4; Thinking Machines10

Qwen3 reports on-policy distillation ahead of RL on all six Table 21 benchmarks at about a tenth of the GPU-hours. R1 found that 10K+ RL steps on Qwen2.5-32B only matched QwQ-32B-Preview while distilling R1 did "significantly better". Thinking Machines reproduces the direction with its own accounting.

  • Three groups, one direction (R1's comparison is off-policy SFT distillation, not on-policy), and Table 21 starts both arms from the same checkpoint.
  • The cost is paid once per teacher and reused across six students.
  • The 17,920 and 1,800 GPU-hours come with no hardware and no statement on whether the teacher is counted (Module 21).
  • The teacher is itself the product of a four-stage RL pipeline, so distillation depends on RL rather than replacing it.
  • The RL arm's recipe is not described; it may not be a tuned run.

RL remains necessary at the frontier and may close the gap with enough steps

DeepSeek-R1 authors, same appendix4; ProRL authors14

R1's authors add that "advancing beyond the boundaries of human intelligence may still require more powerful base models and larger-scale reinforcement learning". ProRL reports that prolonged RL from a distilled 1.5B model, with KL control, reference resets and diverse tasks, finds strategies the base model cannot reach.

  • Distillation cannot exceed the teacher; only RL has a path past it.
  • ProRL's 2,000-plus steps is a different regime from a short RL arm.
  • Needs a long run: ProRL used four nodes of 8 H100 for about 16K GPU-hours14 (§3.2), the same order as Qwen3's RL arm of 17,920 (hardware unstated), so cost alone does not separate the two.
  • No source compares a long RL run with on-policy distillation at matched total compute.
Assessment

For a small student with an existing strong teacher that shares the tokenizer and an SFT start, distillation is the cheaper route in every comparison available, and Qwen3 shows it at product scale (six models). That is a statement about copying a capability, not creating one: the teachers cost four RL-shaped stages that Qwen3 never prices. Whether RL given Qwen3-sized compute and ProRL-style stabilizers would match OPD is untested. The evidence that would settle it is one starting checkpoint, teacher cost counted, matched total GPU-hours, several seeds, and pass@k over more than 30 problems.

Disagreement 2: does RLVR create reasoning capability or sharpen what exists?

RL sharpens; distillation expands

Yue et al.15; DeepSeekMath's Maj@K versus Pass@K observation5; Qwen3's Table 211

RLVR models beat their base at small \(k\) and lose at large \(k\), and the reasoning paths of RLVR models were already inside the base model's samples. Distillation, unlike RLVR, can introduce new patterns from a teacher. Qwen3's Table 21 has the same shape: RL left AIME pass@64 at 90.0 and 83.3, on-policy distillation raised it to 93.3 and 86.7.

  • Yue et al. report the pattern across six RLVR algorithms.
  • Gives a mechanism: a teacher's logits carry information RL's one-bit reward cannot.
  • Table 21's expansion is one question out of 30 on each AIME set, from one run, with no stated estimator (Modules 20 and 21).
  • The RL arm's length and recipe are not given, and the report's RL pass@64 is for a model that started from an already distilled checkpoint.

RL can expand, in a second phase or with prolonged training

ProRL14; the two-stage dynamics view16

ProRL reports gains "inaccessible to base models, even under extensive sampling" and ties them to base-model weakness on the task and to training duration. The two-stage view argues both camps are partly right: an exploitation phase that sharpens, then an exploration phase in which rare good tokens gain probability.

  • Explains why short runs (Qwen3's RL stage ran 170 steps at 235B) look like sharpening.
  • Results are on a 1.5B model; the two-stage paper is a theoretical reconciliation by its own authors' account.

Much of the gain is prior amplification

Spurious Rewards authors17

On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, randomly assigned rewards gave a 21.4-point MATH-500 gain against 29.1 for true rewards; the effect is model-dependent and absent for other families. The paper's mechanism is a clipping bias that amplifies high-prior behavior.

  • A control any pass@k claim should survive.
  • The paper's gains are specific to Qwen2.5-Math and minimal or negative for Qwen2.5, Olmo 2 and Llama 3; it tests no Qwen3 model.
Assessment

Qwen3's own contribution is thin and should be filed as a consistent data point, not a test: a one-question margin from a single run cannot distinguish a real expansion from noise, and the RL arm never had the long horizon where ProRL sees expansion. What the report does support is narrower. Starting from the same checkpoint, a teacher-driven signal gave higher pass@1 at a tenth of the stated cost (hardware and teacher cost not stated). I found no spurious-rewards control on a Qwen3 checkpoint in the sources, and it is among the cheapest experiments that would reduce the uncertainty.

Disagreement 3: verifiers, learned reward models, or judges?

Verifiers only for reasoning; keep learned rewards on a short leash

DeepSeek-R1 authors4

R1 abstains from neural reward models on reasoning tasks because they are susceptible to hacking at scale, and confines the preference reward to the last 400 of 1,700 second-stage steps.

  • Hacking was observed: reward rose while reasoning fell (Supp. B.5).
  • Silent on open-ended tasks where no verifier exists.

Route by task: rules where possible, a reference judge or an RM elsewhere

Qwen3 team1

Qwen3 keeps rules for precision, adds a 72B judge with a reference "avoiding false negatives", and a preference RM for queries with no reference, over more than 20 tasks.

  • Covers instruction following, format, agent and open-ended preference in one stage; ThinkFollow rises to 98.9.
  • No hacking analysis, no exposure cap, no task-to-reward mapping; the judge is fixed and older than the policy.

Rubric judges, trained in the loop

DeepSeek-V3.218; DeepSeek-V419

V3.2 uses a generative reward model with per-prompt rubrics for general tasks. V4 uses no scalar reward models and applies RL to the generative judge, which is the actor itself.

  • Removes the fixed-judge ceiling, and needs no separate judge model.
  • Shared weights mean shared failure modes; the report cannot audit them from inside (Module 16).
Assessment

Qwen3 is the middle position, and the evidence is not there to rank it. Where a checker exists, all three agree on using it. For the rest, each design trades a different risk: R1 limits exposure, Qwen3 relies on a fixed judge that is a generation older than the policies it scores, V4 gives up independence for scale. The measurement that separates them is judge accuracy on labelled pairs tracked through training, which none of the three reports prints.

Disagreement 4: one fused model, or separate thinking and non-thinking models?

Fuse the modes in one set of weights

Qwen3 report, April and May 20251

One model saves the cost and complexity of deploying separate models and gets budget control as a side effect. At 32B, thinking-mode AIME'24 goes from 83.8 (Stage 2, before fusion) to 81.4 (Stage 4, fused), and the fused model's non-thinking mode scores 31.0.

  • One deployment; budgets from a stop string without extra training.
  • Table 22 shows a 2.4-point AIME'24 and 2.7-point LiveCodeBench drop in thinking mode, accepted "to enhance the model's overall versatility".

Train Instruct and Thinking models separately

Qwen team's July 2025 decision, as quoted by The Register20; the 2507 model cards1213

The team's post, as quoted, says: "After talking with the community and thinking it through, we decided to stop using hybrid thinking mode." It adds: "We believe that providing better-quality performance is more important than the unification at this moment." The Instruct-2507 card says the model "supports only non-thinking mode"; the Thinking-2507 card says it "supports only thinking mode".

  • Each checkpoint is tuned for one length profile; the Thinking-2507 card prints AIME25 92.3 against 81.5 for the original and a longer thinking length.
  • Two deployments. The comparison is confounded: three months of reasoning work, longer thinking and possibly other changes came with the split, so the card's gap is not the cost of fusion.
Assessment

Qwen's own reversal is the strongest evidence, and it is a statement of priorities, not a measurement. The team says it keeps researching hybrid thinking20. DeepSeek's V4 trains three reasoning-effort modes per model19, so multi-mode single-model designs are still being built, and Module 23 compares the mechanisms. What would settle it is the same model trained both ways with equal data and compute. The primary Qwen post was not retrievable here (the X page returned HTTP 402 to the fetch tool, and I did not work around it), so the quotes are taken from The Register's labelled secondary account.
