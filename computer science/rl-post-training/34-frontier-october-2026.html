1. How to read the map

1.1 What the date covers

The evidence base is the course's source map (60 entries, built 2026-10-26) plus the additions in the references. Most 2026 reports were read at the sections that bear on RL, not end to end, and several have moved since first posting: Kimi K2.5 and K3 are at v2 of 7 August 202623, Olmo 3 at v2 of 14 April 20264, and DeepSeek-R1 changed two headline numbers between v1 and v2 (Table 9). Every benchmark number below was reported by the lab that trained the model.

1.2 Three states, defined by evidence

  • Settled, within a stated scope. Every report read for that regime agrees, and either one measurement or at least three adopters from separate labs support it. The scope is part of the claim: "no KL term" is settled for rule-verified rewards in open recipes, not for judge rewards or distilled starts.
  • Contested. At least two choices, each carried by a lab's own run or a measured result, and no matched comparison between them. The right choice currently depends on stack, cost and task, so Module 33's decision framework applies.
  • Open. The decisive experiment is absent from every source read, or a report states the problem is unsolved.

The tables tag each row's evidence as measured (a controlled comparison or a theorem), concordant (three or more independent reports, no controlled comparison), single report, or claim only. A row can be contested in one regime and settled in another; the figure places each mechanism by the regime its row names.

Common error

Reading the settled column as "best known". A settled row says that no report read dissents within its scope. It says nothing about whether the shared choice is optimal. Module 9 makes the same point about critic-free methods: adoption shows what teams made work on a deadline, not where the ceiling is.

1.3 What the map cannot see

Open reports come from labs that chose to publish, so a convergence among them is evidence about open-weight practice, not about the field. The closed labs are nearly absent as recipe sources; Section 8 treats what they have said, in a bounded way. Nothing below reconstructs a closed recipe.

4.1 The one controlled comparison, and what it licenses

C4 has the most adoption and the least independent evidence. The MOPD paper compares five integration methods from one SFT checkpoint on Qwen3-30B-A3B across three domains, normalizing each score so the SFT student is 0 and the per-domain RL teachers are 1.

Three things limit it. The setting is a 30B model with three domains, where Ultra and V4 use more than ten teachers197. There is no repeat, so the 5.5-point gap has no stated interval. And "nearly all of each teacher's capability" holds for skills the student can sample, which Ultra's Table 5 qualifies (Section 5.1). None of the frontier reports publishes the comparison against its own prior mixed-RL stage at equal compute (Module 22).

4.2 Worked example: how much of the 9.96 times survives a teacher's cost

Qwen3's Table 21 starts two arms from the same off-policy-distilled 8B checkpoint. RL took 17,920 GPU-hours and reached AIME'24 67.6 with pass@64 unchanged at 90.0; on-policy distillation took 1,800 GPU-hours and reached 74.4 with pass@64 up to 93.36. The ratio is $$ \frac{17{,}920}{1{,}800} = 9.96 $$ The report states neither the hardware nor whether the teacher's cost is inside the 1,800. Suppose the teacher cost \(T\) GPU-hours on the same hardware and serves \(N\) students of this size. The distillation arm then costs \(1{,}800 + T/N\) per student, and stays cheaper than RL while $$ \frac{T}{N} < 17{,}920 - 1{,}800 = 16{,}120 \text{ GPU-hours.} $$ The break-even is arithmetic, not a Qwen3 figure; the report prints no \(T\). It shows where the claim is fragile: a teacher whose cost divided by the students it serves exceeds 16,120 GPU-hours reverses the result. Qwen3's teachers serve six small models, which lowers the per-student share, and are themselves the output of a four-stage pipeline the report never prices6. The Thinking Machines post's 9, 18 and 30 times compare on-policy distillation with SFT, not with RL, and differ in what they charge for the teacher (Module 21)15, so none is an RL-versus-distillation ratio.

4.3 Efficiency versus asymptote as a tie-breaker

ScaleRL says loss aggregation, advantage normalization, curriculum and the off-policy algorithm "primarily modulate compute efficiency without materially shifting the asymptote"14. If that holds, C2's and Table 3's spreads are questions of cost, and a team should pick the cheaper. The paper's Appendix A.11 qualifies its abstract for one item: the off-policy algorithm "affects the asymptotic performance c of the scaling curve, not just the efficiency exponent b", one of "the most consequential design decisions in RL post-training"14. An FP32 LM head moves the fitted asymptote from 0.52 to 0.61, and GSPO runs diverged mid-training, with restarts recovering the 8B runs but not the 17B-by-16 mixture-of-experts model14. So the paper supports "most loss details move cost" and also "numerics, staleness regime and ratio form can move the ceiling". It is one lab's framework on one 8B dense model and one MoE, unreplicated.

5.1 O3 in numbers: what distillation does not import

Nemotron 3 Ultra defines recovery as \((\text{MOPD}_2-\text{RLVR})/(\text{Teacher}-\text{RLVR})\), the fraction of the teacher-student gap MOPD closes. Its Table 5 lists recoveries from 172.7% down to 16.9%, and the report ties the low end, Humanity's Last Exam, to a teacher whose advantage came from extra SFT and RL on data the student never saw: "When the missing capability requires reasoning paths that the student rarely samples, student rollouts become effectively out-of-distribution for the teacher"19. A recovery of 16.9% leaves \(100-16.9=83.1\%\) of the gap open (derived). This is the Module 22 limit in the lab's own words, and it makes the agentic item and the HLE item the same question from two sides: what the student never samples, the teacher cannot grade. The report's RLVR batch figure, a global batch of 8,192 with 16 rollouts per sample, has no stated unit, and this module does not multiply it out (Modules 22 and 32).

5.2 O2: judges are now trained in the loop, and nothing audits them

Between R1 (January 2025) and V4 the reward for unverifiable tasks moved from a scalar model used for the last 400 steps11 to rubric-guided generative reward models, and in V4 to a policy that is its own judge, with RL applied "directly to the GRM itself"7. K3 describes its group reward as "tournament-style" with binary comparisons "as in Kimi K2.5"; the K2.5 text read does not use the word for a reward, and K2's equivalent is pairwise ranking (Module 16)3236. None of these reports publishes judge accuracy against human labels through training, the monitor that would separate an improving judge from an exploited one (Modules 16 and 19). Exploitation is already visible in the reports: Ultra's remark above, K3's guard under which a candidate over the length limit "automatically loses the binary comparison"3, and R1's reward-up, accuracy-down curve11.

5.3 O1 and O5 are less in conflict than they look

If RLVR mostly sharpens, pass@1 should saturate and pass@k at large k should fall, while RL compute keeps buying pass@1 until the base's distribution is exhausted. ScaleRL fits a saturating pass-rate curve, and Module 15 (Section 8) sets out what such a curve does not say. A pass-rate curve that keeps rising with compute is compatible with a coverage curve that keeps falling. That is my inference from the two modules, not a result in any one source; the test that separates them is O1's, run on the same runs that produce the compute fit.

6.1 What varies inside "MOPD"

The label hides differences that Module 22 tabulates. The teachers' relation to the student: the MOPD paper reports that same-origin teachers are "critical for stable optimization" and that a larger external teacher failed20; Nemotron's warmup SFT raises the overlap between student rollouts and teacher-supported trajectories and helps most in agentic domains19. The signal: the MOPD paper scores each sampled token with a per-token reverse KL20, while V4 says that kind of estimate "leads to high variance in gradient estimation and often causes training instability" and distills over the full vocabulary7. The name: V4 calls its stage OPD, GLM-5 calls its stage on-policy cross-stage distillation, and Lambert groups both under MOPD7181. The reference for the method is still a blog post whose original models were retired in a June 2026 update15. Qwen3's strong-to-weak distillation for six small models cost about a tenth of the four-stage pipeline's GPU-hours6; that is distillation as size reduction, a different use from consolidation.

6.2 Reasoning effort as a product surface

Effort levels moved from a prompt trick to a trained product surface in the same window. gpt-oss ships three trained levels, set by a system-prompt keyword such as "Reasoning: low", and the model card says a higher level lengthens the average chain of thought37 (§2.5.2). DeepSeek-V4 supports three modes per model with distinct length penalties and context windows7. Kimi K3 crosses three domains with three effort levels to make nine experts, then distills them into one conditioned student3. Training details are thin everywhere: the gpt-oss card gives one sentence, and V4 and K3 give no penalty or budget values (Module 23). For a reader on the serving side, the output-length distribution a deployment sees is now a function of a trained, user-selectable input, which Module 23's serving section treats as a capacity-planning variable.

7. Calibration as a post-training target, and the claim that carries the same letters

7.1 What the public record supports

Three public sources carry the evidence. RLCR adds a Brier term on a verbalized confidence to the correctness reward, \(R=\mathbb{1}[y=y^*]-(q-\mathbb{1}[y=y^*])^2\), and reports expected calibration error falling from 0.37 to 0.03 on HotpotQA and from 0.26 to 0.10 on math, with accuracy matched to RLVR24 (Eq. 1, §1). Behaviorally Calibrated RL optimizes strictly proper scoring rules so a model can output a calibrated probability of correctness; its headline comparison against GPT-5 on BeyondAIME is the authors' own evaluation39. Kalai et al. argue that the incentive sits in the grading: under 0-1 scoring an "I don't know" is maximally penalized and a best guess is optimal, so the repair is to put explicit confidence targets in benchmark instructions25 (§4). Two printed slips matter for anyone reusing these: RLCR's Theorem 1 has a sign slip in its appendix, and Kalai et al.'s key section has a slip in a worked penalty value (Module 24); this module uses neither. GPT-4's technical report supplies the earliest closed-lab data point: the pre-trained model "is highly calibrated" and after post-training "the calibration is reduced"26 (§5, Fig. 8).

No recipe report read for the course describes a calibration reward in its RL stage, and Kimi K2 names the gap: its rubric-style rewards can penalize hedging (Module 24)36. So calibration is a diagnosis that is settled (S4) and a repair that is research on 4B to 8B models with short verifiable tasks, not a stage in any published frontier recipe. That is why it sits in Table 4 and not in Table 2's practice rows.

7.3 What can and cannot be said about TypeSafe's RLCD

The company states that RLCD is a third post-training route beside RLHF and RLVR, which its launch-post comparison table describes as optimizing for human preference and for verifiable rewards; RLCD is described as training a model "to return decisions and calibrated probabilities instead of generated text"458. The founder says in an interview that the method is not yet published and that the training data is synthetic46. The only press coverage writes "from" where the company writes "for", reports that the architecture was not detailed, and relays one developer's remark that the model "delegates the hallucination problem a little bit to the user"48. A third party has built a method and labeled it a reconstruction, stating that TypeSafe discloses no training algorithm49.

That is the whole of the record. Everything the course says about calibration training is about the public methods in 7.1, and none of it should be read as TypeSafe's reward or algorithm. Whether RLCD is a new regime or the interface and packaging of known proper-scoring training is the fifth dispute below, and Module 25 holds the claims ledger, the output contract and the evidence that outside groups have tested the released model's behavior and not its training.

8.2 Where the boundary falls

The closed labs have disclosed research and failures in detail: reward-model overoptimization in 202254, the monitoring study, the reward-hacking study, the grading theory. They have disclosed recipes only for the era before verifiable-reward reasoning RL: InstructGPT and Constitutional AI in 2022, and the GPT-4 system card's RLHF outline in 2023. For the era after, the on-record material for OpenAI is a few paragraphs in an open-weight model card, and for Anthropic a statement that a document is used "at various stages". No document read gives a reward function, a loss, a KL treatment, a batch size, a compute figure or an environment count for a closed reasoning model.

The one closed-lab sentence about reasoning RL carries less than it appears to. "Similar CoT RL techniques as OpenAI o3" says the open-weight models were trained with techniques that resemble o3's; it does not say which, and it does not say they match anything in Tables 2 to 5. Read it as an existence statement: chain-of-thought RL with tool-use training on a coding, math and science dataset is how this lab made a competitive open-weight reasoning model, and its effort levels are trained and not merely prompted.

Common error

Importing open-lab findings into closed-lab claims. "Frontier labs do not use a KL term" and "Claude uses MOPD" are statements this course cannot make: the evidence behind Tables 3 and 6 is DeepSeek, Moonshot, Zhipu, NVIDIA, Ai2, Alibaba, ByteDance and Xiaomi. Where a closed lab matches an open recipe, the match is unobserved.

8.3 Consequences for the rest of the module

Every settled row is settled among labs that publish. The strongest evidence on failure modes at frontier scale is closed-lab research, and it is the best-sourced reason to treat O6 as a live decision. A UK AI Security Institute research note, not peer reviewed, reproduced the Anthropic setup with open models and found hacking consistent and misalignment inconsistent29; it is the only outside check, and it does not say how the production pipeline behaves.

Where experts disagree

The five disputes below are the ones the map leaves open; each has two positions, the strongest cited evidence for each, and an assessment that names the experiment that would settle it. Disputes that Modules 9 to 15 treat as contested (critic, ratio, normalization) are in Table 4 and not repeated.

Dispute 1: Does RLVR create capability, or sharpen what the base has?

Sharpening: the base sets the boundary

Yue et al.42; Qwen3's Table 216

At large k the base model solves more problems than its RL-trained version, and RL left Qwen3-8B's pass@64 unchanged.

  • Two reports, one direction; a testable claim.
  • Short runs, strong math bases, answer-only checking.

Expansion: long, diverse, KL-controlled training

ProRL21; a phase-based reconciliation43

Over 2,000 steps, gains were largest where the base started weak, and a held-out task the base could not solve at all was solved (Module 20).

  • Answers the "stopped too early" objection directly.
  • Starts from a distilled 1.5B model; the phase theory is the authors' own assessment.
Assessment

For strong math bases and runs of hundreds of steps, sharpening is best supported, and Qwen2.5-Math gains stay unconfirmed until a random-reward control agrees (+21.4 against +29.1 points)44. Expansion is plausible for weak starts but rests on one small model. The deciding run is pass@k to large k at every checkpoint on a decontaminated base with a random-reward arm (Module 20).

Dispute 2: How should many skills end up in one model?

One mixed RL stage

DeepSeek-V3.216; Ultra's first stage, before it moves to MOPD19

One on-policy run over all domains avoids the forgetting of multi-stage pipelines.

  • One trainer; within 5.5 points of MOPD in the controlled test20.
  • Per-domain signal dilutes as environments multiply19; DeepSeek replaced it in V47.

Specialists, then MOPD

MiMo-V2-Flash, Ultra, K3, V4; the MOPD paper17193720

Train each skill to its peak in parallel, then distill on the student's own rollouts.

  • Best score in the one controlled comparison; teams work in parallel.
  • Needs same-origin teachers; HLE recovery of 16.9% in Ultra's Table 519.
Assessment

With few, similar domains mixed RL is within reach and cheaper; with many environments and long agentic rollouts, four labs chose specialists and the dilution argument is sound. GLM-5's staged RL with cross-stage distillation is a hedge18. The missing run is one SFT checkpoint through both routes at matched GPU-hours with ten or more domains (Module 22).

Dispute 3: RL or distillation for a strong small reasoner?

Distill first

DeepSeek-R1 Supp. F.111; Qwen3 Table 216

R1 found 10K+ RL steps on a 32B base only matched QwQ-32B-Preview; Qwen3 got higher scores at about a tenth of the GPU-hours.

  • Two reports, one direction; pass@64 rose.
  • Teacher cost and hardware unstated; the teacher is itself an RL product.

RL to pass the teacher

R1's second conclusion11; ProRL21

R1 adds that "advancing beyond the boundaries of human intelligence may still require more powerful base models and larger-scale reinforcement learning".

  • The only route past a teacher.
  • No matched-compute comparison; ProRL's baseline is its own start.
Assessment

Below the frontier, with a close teacher, distillation is the cheaper route in every comparison here; at the frontier there is no teacher above you. Both statements hold, and the comparison that would join them, with teacher cost counted (Section 4.2), has not been published.

Dispute 4: Calibration: train it into the policy, or fix how benchmarks grade?

Train it in

RLCR24; Behaviorally Calibrated RL39

A bounded proper scoring rule next to the correctness reward removes the incentive to guess.

  • ECE 0.37 to 0.03 on HotpotQA with matched accuracy.
  • 4B to 8B models, short tasks, authors' evaluation.

Fix the grading

Kalai et al., OpenAI and Georgia Tech25

Models guess because leaderboards reward guessing; add explicit confidence targets to the scoring.

  • Acts on the cause for every lab at once.
  • No training experiment; depends on leaderboard owners.
Assessment

Complements: they act on two layers of one incentive. If you train, a Brier term costs little accuracy at modest scale; if you publish numbers, the grading argument applies regardless (Module 24). A frontier-scale test on agentic or noisy-verifier tasks is missing.

Dispute 5: TypeSafe's RLCD: a new regime, or known calibration-aware RL under a new name?

A new regime

TypeSafe AI: launch post, primer, founder (company and interview claims)84546

RLHF and RLVR optimize the wrong target for software that needs a trustworthy probability; RLCD trains calibrated typed decisions.

  • The output contract is public, and the motive matches the literature24.
  • No mechanism, data, ablation or calibration table is public.

Known training, new packaging

No source states this about TypeSafe; it is what the public record implies243949

Proper-scoring training exists for text models, and a third party rebuilt a version from standard parts.

  • Parsimonious: needs no hidden method.
  • Assumes the undisclosed method is no more than the public ones; no source can show that.
Assessment

Neither can be established. The record documents an output interface that outside groups have tested and says nothing about the training, so "regime" and "rebranding" are both claims about a method nobody has seen. The decider is a statement of the reward or loss plus an ablation only the company can run (Module 25). Until then use the public machinery of Module 24 for anything you build.

What is changing

As of 2026-10-27 the fastest-moving items are the documents the course reads least stably. The Nemotron and GLM-5 READMEs already list successors (a 3.5 Super recipe with two-phase MOPD, and GLM-5.1 to 5.3)5657, TRL's defaults sit on a main branch32, and the K3 and K2.5 reports were revised on 7 August 202632. Any value quoted in this course from those pages should be re-read first.

One forecast, labeled as my own and not found in any source: the first controlled MOPD-versus-mixed-RL comparison at frontier scale is the result most likely to change Table 4, since five labs adopted the step while the only controlled comparison is at 30B scale. Module 23 notes that no shared accuracy-per-token metric exists, so C6 cannot be compared across labs until one does. And TypeSafe's situation can change in a day: the founder says RLCD is unpublished46, so one post stating the reward would supersede Section 7 and Module 25 at once.
