Reinforcement Learning for LLM Post-Training · Module 32

Case Study: GLM-5 and Nemotron 3 Ultra: Asynchronous Agentic RL, a Deterministic Indexer Top-k, Unified RLVR over Environments the Report Calls Open, and the Limits of MOPD

Two 2026 reports describe agent-era post-training from opposite ends. GLM-5 runs reasoning, agentic and general RL in sequence, closes with on-policy distillation from its own earlier checkpoints, and found that a non-deterministic top-k kernel in its sparse-attention indexer broke RL within a few steps. Nemotron 3 Ultra runs one unified RLVR stage over fifteen environment groups, merges more than ten specialists by on-policy distillation, and states where that merge stops working. Afterwards you can say what each lab found that the other did not, which of their knobs are disclosed and which are not, and what to audit in an RL rollout path.

1. Two recipes, one frame

1.1 The reports and what they disclose

GLM-5 is a 744B-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 40B active, trained on 28.5T tokens and built on DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA)1 (§1, §2.1). Its report is dated 17 Feb 2026, with v2 on 24 Feb 2026. Nemotron 3 Ultra is a 550B total, 55B active hybrid Mamba-attention mixture-of-experts model pre-trained on 20T tokens and posted 12 Jun 20262 (abstract). Ultra says its RL training "largely follows the asynchronous GRPO algorithm" with the stability optimizations of the Nemotron 3 Super report2 (§3.2), so the algorithm's details are in Super3 (§3.2.4), posted 14 Apr 2026. As of 2026-10-27 arXiv lists v2 as the latest GLM-5 version and v1 for both NVIDIA reports4.

Neither report states a total RL budget in GPU-hours or FLOPs in the text searched, and neither tabulates its RL recipe against alternatives. GLM-5's one ablation section compares attention variants (§2.1.2); Ultra's ablations cover MOPD design (the warmup in Table 4, rollouts per prompt in §3.3.1, logit matching in §3.3.5) and the MTP draft length (Figure 12). That sets the evidential ceiling for everything below: two labs describing what they did and what they say worked.

2. GLM-5: three RL stages and the distillation that closes them

2.1 The SFT starting point

The RL stages start from a multi-task SFT model with a 202,752-token context and three thinking behaviors: interleaved thinking (reason before every response and tool call), preserved thinking (keep thinking blocks across turns in coding-agent use) and turn-level control of whether to think1 (§3.1). One SFT detail anticipates the RL rules of Section 4: in coding and agent trajectories, "erroneous segments" are kept but masked out of the loss, which lets the model "learn error correction behaviors without reinforcing incorrect actions"1 (§3.1).

2.2 Reasoning RL: GRPO, IcePop, no KL

Reasoning RL is GRPO with IcePop to handle the gap between the training policy \(\pi^{train}\) and the inference policy \(\pi^{infer}\), with the KL term removed "to accelerate RL improvement"1 (§3.2). Per token:

$$\mathcal L(\theta)=-\,\mathbb E\Big[\tfrac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\tfrac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\mathrm{pop}\big(\rho_{i,t},\tfrac1\beta,\beta\big)\cdot\min\big(r_{i,t}\hat A_{i,t},\ \mathrm{clip}(r_{i,t},1-\epsilon_{low},1+\epsilon_{high})\hat A_{i,t}\big)\Big]$$

where \(\rho_{i,t}=\pi^{train}_{\theta_{old}}/\pi^{infer}_{\theta_{old}}\) is the train-to-inference ratio, \(r_{i,t}=\pi^{train}_{\theta}/\pi^{train}_{\theta_{old}}\) is the usual PPO ratio, \(\mathrm{pop}(\rho,1/\beta,\beta)\) equals \(\rho\) inside the band and \(0\) outside it, and \(\hat A_{i,t}\) is the group-normalized advantage with the standard-deviation division kept1 (Eq. 1). The stated values are \(\beta=2\), \(\epsilon_{low}=0.2\), \(\epsilon_{high}=0.28\), group size 32 and batch size 32, "entirely on-policy". Module 12 (Section 5.2) places this mask among the other log-ratio gates; Module 14 covers where \(\rho\) comes from.

Two readings of the numbers. With \(\beta=2\), a token receives no gradient when its train-to-inference probability ratio leaves \([0.5,\,2]\), that is when \(|\ln\rho|>\ln 2=0.693\) (derived). And if each rollout batch feeds a single update, \(r_{i,t}=1\) exactly and the clip at 0.2 and 0.28 is idle, so the pop mask is the only guard against drift (my reading; the report does not give updates per batch or the unit of "batch size 32").

The data side is prose only. Four domains are mixed, roughly balanced: mathematics, science, code and tool-integrated reasoning. Problems are those GLM-4.7 solves "only rarely or fails consistently" and stronger teachers can solve; domain-specific judges or evaluation systems return binary outcome rewards1 (§3.2). The report claims "stable and significant gains in each domain" without printing them, and prints no run with the KL term kept (the KL question is Modules 6 and 13).

2.3 General RL: three reward types and a human anchor

The third stage targets foundational correctness, emotional intelligence and task-specific quality with a hybrid of rule-based rewards, outcome reward models (ORMs) and generative reward models (GRMs). The report says ORMs give "low-variance signals and high training efficiency" but are "more susceptible to reward hacking", while GRMs are "more robust to such exploitation" but "exhibit higher variance"1 (§3.4). It also adds expert human-written responses as "stylistic and qualitative anchors", on the stated observation that purely model-generated optimization converges toward "recognizably 'model-like' patterns". No reward weights, data sizes or hacking measurements appear in the section (judges are Module 16; the hacking taxonomy is Module 19).

2.4 Cross-stage distillation: the loss and what the report leaves out

GLM-5 ends with "on-policy cross-stage distillation as the final stage", because "sequentially optimizing for distinct objectives can lead to the cumulative degradation of previously acquired capabilities". The teachers are "the final checkpoints from the preceding training stages"; prompts are "sampled from the corresponding teachers' RL training sets and mixed in appropriate proportions"1 (§3.5). The loss reuses Eq. 1 with the advantage replaced by a stop-gradient log-ratio (Eq. 2):

$$\hat A_{i,t}=\mathrm{sg}\Big[\log\frac{\pi^{infer}_{teacher}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi^{train}_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Big]$$

Group size is set to 1 and batch size to 1,024 because "it is no longer necessary to maintain a large group of samples per prompt to estimate advantages; the advantage is computed directly from the gap with the teacher models instead". Teacher logits come from the inference engine; the authors plan to move the inference backend into the training engine and use the MQA mode of MLA for inference, so that \(\pi^{infer}\rightarrow\pi^{train}\)1 (§3.5). The labels in Eq. 2 show why: the numerator is scored in the inference engine and the denominator in the training engine, so any engine mismatch enters the advantage as a bias indistinguishable from teacher-student gap (my reading; Module 14 gives the size of such gaps). Module 22 (Section 6.3) treats the objective itself.

What the report leaves out is the measurement. Its stated motive is cumulative degradation, yet the sections read print no before-and-after scores for this stage, no teacher count, and no statement of whether Agentic RL's skills reach the final model by distillation or only through General RL starting from the agentic checkpoint, which the "sequential" description implies (my reading). Ultra's analogous stage prints scores for every stage on eleven benchmarks (Section 6.3). Any comparison with GLM-5's distillation rests on the report's reasoning, not on a table.

3. A top-k kernel as an RL hyperparameter

3.1 A discrete branch inside attention

DSA adds a lightning indexer to attention. For a query token \(t\) it scores every preceding token \(s\) as \(I_{t,s}=\sum_{j=1}^{H^I} w^I_{t,j}\,\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf q^I_{t,j}\cdot\mathbf k^I_s)\), chosen for throughput and implementable in FP8; a selection step keeps the key-value entries with the top-\(k\) scores and attention runs over that set only5 (§2.1, Eqs. 1-2). GLM-5 uses \(k=2{,}048\), with 32 indexer heads of dimension 1281 (§3.2, Table 10). Selection is a discrete function of continuous scores: the attended set changes when two scores swap order. It is the same kind of branch as MoE routing and top-p truncation. If the rollout engine and the trainer disagree on the set, they compute different distributions for the same sampled tokens, and the mismatch of Module 14 appears with no optimizer update at all.

DeepSeek-V3.2's answer to the two discrete branches it met was replay: Keep Routing reuses the sampling-time expert paths in training, and Keep Sampling Mask reuses the top-p and top-k truncation masks5 (§3.1; Module 30 covers both). GLM-5 reports that for the indexer, replay is not the answer.

3.3 Two candidate mechanisms (my inference)

Two mechanisms would explain a non-deterministic top-k hurting RL, and the report does not choose between them. First, a call-to-call difference inside one engine: when scores tie at the cut, a parallel selection kernel may return either tied token on different runs with identical inputs. The trainer's two forward passes at the same weights, the one that fixes \(\pi_{old}\) and the one that computes \(\pi_\theta\), can then disagree, so the ratio \(r\) that should be exactly 1 on the first update is not, and the clip and IcePop masks act at random. Second, a cross-engine difference: each engine may break ties, or round scores, differently, which is the \(\rho\) of Section 2.2 acting through the selected set. Ties are plausible after a ReLU with low-precision scores. For scale, DeepSeek-V4 (a different model and indexer) quantized its index scores from FP32 to BF16 and kept a 99.7% recall of the selected KV entries6 (§5.2.1), so a small perturbation changes a fraction of a percent of entries. Figure 3 draws the tie case. A fixed batch scored twice under each operator, with the spread of \(\ln\rho\) and \(\ln r\) logged, would separate the mechanisms; the report shows none.

3.4 Why replay was judged impractical: the arithmetic

The report calls replay impractical without giving sizes. They follow from printed quantities. Per token and per layer, MoE routing replay stores 8 expert ids (8 routed experts of 256, Table 10), while indexer replay stores 2,048 indices:

$$\frac{k}{k_{\mathrm{MoE}}}=\frac{2048}{8}=256\times$$

For a sequence of \(L=10^5\) tokens, with \(N_{idx}\) layers that each carry an indexer and \(b\) bytes per index,

$$S_{\mathrm{idx}}=L\,N_{idx}\,k\,b=10^{5}\cdot 78\cdot 2048\cdot 4\ \mathrm{B}\approx 63.9\ \mathrm{GB},\qquad S_{\mathrm{MoE}}=10^{5}\cdot 75\cdot 8\cdot 1\ \mathrm{B}=60\ \mathrm{MB}.$$

Packing each index into 18 bits (derived: \(2^{18}=262{,}144\) exceeds the 202,752-token context) gives 35.9 GB. The ratios to routing replay are about 1,065 times for int32 and 599 times packed. The assumptions: \(N_{idx}=78\), the 3 dense and 75 MoE layers of Table 10 (the text of §2.1 says 80 layers, while Table 10 lists those 78 plus one MTP layer), with an indexer in each. The second assumption is supported by the GLM-5 repository, whose README says GLM-5.2 introduces IndexShare, which "reuses the same indexer across every four sparse attention layers"7; there would be nothing to share if GLM-5 had one indexer in total. For scale, a 576-dimension latent KV entry (the MLA cache size §2.1 discusses) in BF16 is 1,152 B per token per layer, so the latent cache of the same sequence is about 9.0 GB (derived; the dtype is assumed), and the replay payload would be 4 to 7 times larger than the cache it describes. Sharing one selection across four layers would cut it about fourfold, to roughly 16 GB, still about 266 times the routing payload (my extrapolation from the README line).

In practice: a checklist for every discrete branch in the rollout path

My checklist, built from this section and Module 14, not a procedure from either report. (1) List each data-dependent discrete choice between the weights and the sampled token: the indexer's top-k, the router's top-k, top-p and top-k truncation, speculative-decoding acceptance. (2) Test each for call-to-call determinism on identical inputs, in both engines. (3) Compare tie-breaking and score precision across engines. (4) Log the spread of \(\ln\rho\) and \(\ln r\) on a fixed batch before and after any kernel swap. (5) Where replay costs more than the data it protects, as here, fix the operator instead of the data.

4. GLM-5 agentic RL: asynchrony as a set of hygiene rules

4.1 The objective and what it ignores

For each problem \(x\), GLM-5 samples \(K\) agent traces from the previous policy and optimizes a group-wise objective whose advantage is the trace reward minus the group mean reward, \(r(x,y_i)-\bar r(x)\); "only model-generated tokens are used for optimization, and the environment feedback is ignored in loss computation"1 (§4.1). Unlike the reasoning loss of Section 2.2, the printed agentic advantage has no standard-deviation division (Module 11 covers the choice), and the printed objective carries only the advantage term; the policy term arrives in Eq. 3 below. The report uses \(K\) both for traces per problem and for the weight-push interval below; neither value is given. Module 18 covers the masking of environment tokens and the multi-turn credit problem; this section is about what the report builds around the objective so that a learner can be fed by long, uneven rollouts.

4.2 The loop, one stage at a time

Synchronous RL "suffers from severe GPU idle time during long-horizon agent rollouts"1 (§3.3), so the report decouples the engines onto different GPUs. The inference engine generates continuously; once the number of trajectories reaches a threshold, the batch goes to the trainer. Figure 5 steps through it.

  1. Register and balance. Each task is a microservice with its own rollout and reward logic, registered with the orchestrator, which sets the per-task rollout ratio and generation speed and standardizes every trajectory into one message-list format. The report cites more than 1,000 concurrent rollouts.
  2. Record the tokens. The TITO gateway intercepts every generation request and stores token IDs and metadata, so the learner never re-tokenizes text. Requests from one rollout are hashed to one data-parallel rank so the shared prefix hits the KV cache.
  3. Filter the batch. Once enough trajectories exist the batch moves on. A sample is dropped if its oldest weight version is more than \(\tau\) behind the current one, or if its sandbox failed; a group with more than half valid samples is padded by repeating them, otherwise dropped.
  4. Update with a double-sided mask. The ratio is the current policy over the logged rollout probability; tokens outside \([1-\epsilon_\ell,\,1+\epsilon_h]\) get no gradient; no old-policy checkpoints are tracked.
  5. Push the weights. Every \(K\) gradient updates the weights return to the inference engines and the optimizer is reset, because the new weights pose a different optimization problem.

Module 14 (Section 2.5) explains why the token-in-token-out gateway matters, and Module 18 (Section 4) why a trajectory spanning several weight versions needs per-token behavior probabilities. The point of this section is the combination, written as a ledger of the knobs and which of them the report gives.

4.3 What Eq. 3 optimizes

The report writes the token-level objective as \(L(\theta)=\mathbb E_t[f(r_t(\theta),\epsilon_\ell,\epsilon_h)\,\hat A_t\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)]\) with \(r_t=\exp(\log\pi_\theta-\log\pi_{rollout})\) and \(f(x)=x\) for \(1-\epsilon_\ell<x<1+\epsilon_h\), else 01 (§4.1.2, Eqs. 3-5). It prints no stop-gradient on \(f\). Read as a constant weight, as Module 12 does, the gradient is

$$\nabla_\theta L=\mathbb E_t\Big[\mathbb 1\{1-\epsilon_\ell<r_t<1+\epsilon_h\}\;r_t\,\hat A_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\Big]=\mathbb E_t\Big[\mathbb 1\{\cdot\}\,\hat A_t\,\frac{\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{rollout}(a_t\mid s_t)}\Big],$$

the off-policy policy gradient with the logged rollout probability as behavior policy, restricted to in-band tokens. Because \(\pi_{rollout}\) is whatever the engine held when it sampled each token, one stored number per token serves however many weight versions a trajectory crossed. The report says reusing rollout log-probabilities "accepts a controlled degree of off-policy bias" to avoid historical tracking and that this improves stability; it prints no comparison against tracking \(\pi_{old}\).

4.4 Environments, in four lines

Module 17 covers construction and validity checks; the sizes the report prints are these. SWE: more than 10,000 verifiable environments across thousands of repositories and nine languages, with fail-to-pass and pass-to-pass tests extracted by LLM-written log parsers (§4.2.1). Terminal: thousands of Harbor-format tasks with Docker construction accuracy "exceeding 90%" (§4.2.2). Search: a web knowledge graph over more than two million pages, and a filter that removes any question a tool-free reasoning model answers in at least one of eight attempts (§4.2.3). Slides: a three-level HTML reward whose renderer was patched after reward hacking, with 16:9 compliance rising from 40% to 92% (§4.2.5; Module 19, Section 4.3)1. The report also describes a keep-recent-\(k\) context rule for search agents (\(k=5\), BrowseComp from 55.3% to 62.0%) in its inference section (§4.2.4); whether training rollouts used it is not stated.

5. Nemotron 3 Ultra: one unified RLVR stage

5.1 From Super to Ultra: two RL stages become teacher recipes

Ultra's post-training is "substantially redesigned from the pipeline used for Nemotron 3 Super"2 (§3). Super ran three RL stages after SFT, multi-environment RLVR, a separate end-to-end SWE-RL stage and RLHF, then healed its MTP heads3 (§3.2). It split SWE-RL out because "SWE rollouts are substantially slower to generate and typically require longer context lengths, creating a throughput bottleneck when co-trained with shorter-horizon environments". Ultra keeps the unified first stage. The other two stages survive as teacher recipes: its SWE teacher ends in end-to-end SWE-RL, and its chat teacher is trained by RLHF against a generative reward model2 (§3.3.2). The mapping is my reading; the report does not draw it. The rollout-heterogeneity problem behind Super's separate stage returns as Ultra's stated limit in Section 6.4.

5.2 The algorithm, and a batch whose unit is not stated

Super's algorithm section is the specification Ultra points to. Training and inference run on separate GPUs; workers fill a rollout buffer; a batch trains once enough trajectories exist, "a single gradient update per rollout"; updated weights are pushed "as soon as a new model version is available", so one trajectory can hold tokens from different versions and the KV cache is not recomputed after an update; workers stay "at most one step behind"; and "we mask the importance sampling ratio computed from the training and inference logprobs"3 (§3.2.4, §3.2.5). Ultra's MOPD loss names the same masking "IcePop strategy"2 (§3.3.1), which is what GLM-5's reasoning stage uses (Section 2.2). Two labs and three reports therefore converge on masking tokens whose train-to-inference ratio leaves a band (Module 14 covers the design space). Neither NVIDIA report prints the band in the sections read.

The unit of "global batch 8192"

Ultra's §3.2 says "a global batch size of 8192, with each sample generating 16 rollouts". It does not say whether 8,192 counts prompts or rollouts, and this module derives no per-step prompt or rollout count from it. Nearby text suggests a convention without stating it for this run. Super prints 256 prompts per step, 16 responses per prompt and a batch size of 4,096, and 256 × 16 = 4,096 (derived)3. Ultra's STEM-teacher RL "use[s] a smaller number of prompts per batch (128) and a resultant smaller global batch size (2048)", and 128 × 16 = 2,048 (derived)2 (§3.3.2). In both, the batch figure equals prompts times 16, which reads as a global batch that counts rollouts. That is a pattern across two printed pairs, not a statement about §3.2. GLM-5's reasoning RL has the mirror problem: "a group size of 32 and a batch size of 32", with no unit1 (§3.2).

5.3 Fifteen environment groups and several harnesses

The unified stage spans "terminal usage, office and productivity workflows, software engineering, search, general tool-calling, math, code, STEM, safety, chat, instruction following, long-context QA, inductive and transductive reasoning, structured outputs, and general model usability": fifteen groups by my count2 (§3.2). For harness-based environments the training data use "a diverse collection of harness implementations and interaction formats", to reduce "overfitting to any particular harness design". The report profiles rewards before training and takes its mixture and curriculum from "the Gaussian-based approach introduced in NVIDIA (2025b)", an earlier report I did not read; Super describes dropping prompts the SFT model answers correctly "consistently" and sorting the rest by difficulty3 (§3.2.1). Appendix A.2 sorts the task distributions into five verticals (terminal use and software engineering, bug fixing in existing repositories, office productivity, general knowledge, search) and trains each under at least two of Stirrup, OpenHands, OpenCode, Terminus, Droid and internal harnesses2; which harnesses serve which vertical is not stated. 6. Nemotron 3 Ultra: from unified RLVR to MOPD, and where it stops

6.1 Why a second stage, and who the teachers are

Ultra's reason for MOPD is dilution: "as the number of environments continues to grow, each domain contributes only a relatively small number of samples to any given training batch, diluting the per-domain learning signal and making it increasingly difficult to balance training across domains". The remedy is "more than ten specialized teacher models, each optimized through its own domain-specific training pipeline"; the student, "obtained from RLVR", "generates rollouts across all domains and receives dense reward signals from the corresponding teacher models"2 (§3.3). Figure 10 of the report draws ten first-round teachers, then five new or refreshed second-round teachers beside five reused ones (my count), with the RLVR student as self-teacher where no specialist covers a domain. Module 22 (Sections 4 and 6) covers the objective, the warmup SFT and the same-origin condition. Table 6 lists how each teacher is trained.

6.4 The reported limit: rollouts that differ in length

Ultra lists three unresolved MOPD problems. Two are about teachers (logit matching, and giving teacher and student a shared SFT stage first). The third is about rollouts: "When mixing end-to-end agentic environments with reasoning environments in MOPD, we observed substantial training inefficiency because rollout times can differ dramatically. Balancing efficiency and accuracy requires sophisticated training infrastructure together with careful asynchronous algorithm design. In practice, we use single-turn rollouts, similar to PivotRL (Yi et al., 2026), for most of the agentic tasks. This approach performs relatively well, but whether end-to-end rollouts can yield further gains, and how to make it robust to potential distribution mismatch (Wang et al., 2026a), remains an open area for exploration"2 (§3.3.5). The sentence states a cost and a choice, not a comparison: the sections read contain no run of end-to-end agentic MOPD against the single-turn version.

The cost is a straggler cost, and the report's own infrastructure numbers show how it enters. During RL and MOPD the pipeline overlaps rollout generation with the policy update, so "the step time is bounded by whichever stage is slower", typically generation, "dominated by a small fraction of straggler generations"2 (§3.6.1). With \(T_{step}=\max(T_{gen},T_{train})\) and \(a=T_{gen}/T_{train}\ge1\), a generator made 1.46 times faster (the measured MTP gain at \(k=5\), concentrated on the slowest trajectories) shortens the step to \(\max(T_{gen}/1.46,\,T_{train})\):

$$\text{speedup}_{\text{overlapped}}=\min(1.46,\;a),\qquad \text{speedup}_{\text{sequential}}=\frac{a+1}{a/1.46+1}.$$

At \(a=1.2\) the overlapped pipeline gains 1.2 times, not 1.46; at \(a=3\) it gains the full 1.46 (derived; Figure 8). Two consequences. In a generation-bound pipeline every added second of straggler time lands in the step time one for one, which is how a few 200-turn, 192K-token SWE rollouts in a batch of single-turn reasoning rollouts can idle a trainer (my reading of the report's statement). And rollout-side speedups (MTP, FP8, prefill-decode splitting, as GLM-5 also uses1 (§3.6.2)) are worth their full value only while \(a\) stays above the speedup. The remedy Ultra chose changes the work, not the engine: shorter, turn-local rollouts (my reading).

Kimi K3 keeps long-horizon rollouts inside distillation by pausing generation once a fraction of trajectories has finished and resuming the rest next iteration8 (§4.1.2, §4.1.3), trading the straggler cost for staleness (Module 18, Section 4); GLM-5 states no agentic distillation design. The agentic rows of Ultra's Table 5 recover 67.0% to 172.7% of the teacher gap, with single-turn rollouts for most agentic tasks, and the report reads MOPD as most effective "when the teacher's advantage can be expressed as token-level preferences over trajectories that the student is already able to sample, such as tool-use decisions, environment interactions, abstention behavior, and multi-step execution patterns"2 (§3.3.4). That supports "good enough" on these benchmarks, not "no gain left".

Results will differ from the tech report benchmarks, which used additional proprietary data"10. The RLVR and MOPD stages this module is about are named there but not in that recipe; the report's "RL environments" may live in the NeMo Gym and NeMo RL repositories, which I did not inspect. Super's recipe entry lists "3× RLVR + 2× SWE-RL + RLHF across 21 reward environments"10. Where experts disagree

Neither report is an experiment. Each states a design and its authors' judgment that it worked, so the disagreements are between designs, and each block ends with what would settle it.

A. How should separately trained skills end up in one model?

A1. Sequential stages, healed by distilling from earlier checkpoints

GLM-51 (§3.5)

Run reasoning, agentic and general RL in order, then distill from the final checkpoints of earlier stages to undo "cumulative degradation".

  • Needs only checkpoints the run already produced; teachers are same-origin, so no warmup.
  • Stages stay serial; no scores printed for the healing step; teacher count and prompt mix not given.

A2. Unified RL first, specialists as teachers second

Nemotron 3 Ultra2 (§3.2, §3.3)

Train one student on all environments, then close per-domain gaps with more than ten teachers trained in parallel.

  • Measured: 67.0% to 172.7% recovery on the agentic rows of Table 5.
  • Teachers train in parallel and can be refreshed.
  • Pays for both stages; HLE recovers only 16.9%, which the report attributes to capabilities the student rarely samples.

A3. One mixed stage, or distillation replacing it

DeepSeek-V3.2 mixed GRPO5 (§3); DeepSeek-V4 replaced it with OPD6 (§5.1); Module 30

V3.2 merged reasoning, agent and alignment RL into one stage to avoid forgetting; V4 dropped that stage for distillation.

  • One trainer, one recipe.
  • Ultra's dilution argument applies; V4 gives no ablation for its reversal.
Assessment

The reports do not measure the same thing on the same base, so neither "A1 is enough" nor "A2 is better" is established. Under few domains with a natural order, A1 costs least. Under many environments of different rollout shape, Ultra's dilution argument is sound and its Table 5 is the only measurement of a merge stage in these two reports. What would settle it is one SFT checkpoint taken through both routes at matched GPU-hours over the same domains, scored per domain (Module 22 reaches the same verdict).

B. What closes the training-inference gap?

B1. Mask or correct in the loss

GLM-5 reasoning RL1; Nemotron 3 Super3; Ultra's MOPD2

Drop or reweight tokens whose train-to-inference ratio is out of band.

  • Engine-agnostic; one band to tune.
  • Discards tokens; the NVIDIA bands are not printed.

B2. Make the two engines agree

GLM-5's deterministic top-k1 (§3.2); Kimi K3's shared quantization scheme8 (§4.1.4)

Remove the cause: same operator, same numerics on both sides.

  • Fixes what a mask cannot: GLM-5 reports collapse with non-deterministic top-k.
  • Costs speed ("slightly slower"); per-operator engineering; no measured comparison.

B3. Replay the discrete state

DeepSeek-V3.2's Keep Routing and Keep Sampling Mask5 (§3.1)

Reuse the sampling-time choice in training.

  • Exact for small state such as 8 expert ids per layer.
  • For the indexer the derived payload is hundreds of times larger (Section 3.4); GLM-5 rejects it.
Assessment

They are layers, not rivals. GLM-5 runs B1 and B2 together; B3 fits only where the state is small. If IcePop was active in GLM-5's failed top-k runs (the report does not say), the finding shows masking alone cannot absorb this failure. A run that logs \(\ln\rho\) and \(\ln r\) under both operators would settle which mechanism of Section 3.3 operates.

C. Which rollouts should agentic training consume?

C1. Turn-local, single-turn rollouts

Nemotron 3 Ultra2 (§3.3.5); Super3 (§3.2.4)

Use PivotRL-style turns for most agentic tasks.

  • No stragglers; the agentic rows still recover 67.0% to 172.7%.
  • Untested against end-to-end; distribution mismatch is listed as open.

C2. End-to-end, balanced by an orchestrator

GLM-51 (§4.1)

Train on whole trajectories, with per-task rollout ratio and speed control and staleness hygiene.

  • Trains the distribution that is deployed.
  • Needs the Table 3 rules; no throughput figures printed.

C3. Partial rollouts and a staleness-tolerant loss

Kimi K38 (§4.1.2, §4.1.3)

Pause at a fraction of finished trajectories and resume the rest later.

  • Keeps the long tail in the batch.
  • Trajectories span policy versions; tolerance is asserted.
Assessment

Unsettled: no report compares the three. Figure 8 gives the way to choose: if generation is the slower stage, stragglers lengthen every step, which favors C1 or C3; if training dominates, they are hidden and C2's fidelity is cheaper. Measuring \(T_{gen}/T_{train}\) on your own rollout mix comes before choosing. The experiment that would settle the quality question is Ultra's own open item: end-to-end against single-turn distillation on the same teachers.

D. Should the policy loss keep a KL term to a reference?

D1. Drop it

GLM-5 reasoning RL1 (§3.2); no KL term appears in Super's algorithm text or Ultra's RLVR description3

GLM-5 removes it "to accelerate RL improvement".

  • Faster movement; no reference-model forward pass.
  • No printed ablation; no restraint where rewards are soft.

D2. Keep a small, corrected term, tuned by domain

DeepSeek-V3.25 (§3.1)

V3.2 corrects the estimator with an importance ratio and says that for mathematics "a relatively weak KL penalty or even omitting it entirely can yield improved performance".

  • A leash where a judge can be exploited.
  • One more knob per domain; no values printed.
Assessment

The reports split by reward type, and V3.2's own sentence agrees. GLM-5 states the removal for reasoning RL only; whether its General RL stage, whose rewards partly come from reward models the report itself calls hackable (§3.4), keeps a KL term is not stated. The run that would settle it is General RL with and without the term, scored for reward hacking; neither report has it (Modules 6 and 13).

What is changing

Both reports have successors. The GLM-5 repository's README now lists GLM-5.1, 5.2 and 5.3, and says GLM-5.2 shares one indexer across every four sparse-attention layers7; if that carries to the selections, the replay problem of Section 3.4 shrinks about fourfold without vanishing. I did not read those reports. Ultra's own figures already compare against GLM-5.1 and Kimi K2.62. The README of the Nemotron repository lists a 3.5 Super recipe with two-phase MOPD10, which I also did not read. Every number here is from a v1 or v2 report that may be revised; MOPD is a family of recipes, not a specification (Module 22).
