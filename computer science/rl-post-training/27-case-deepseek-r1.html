Case Study: DeepSeek-R1: R1-Zero, the Four-Stage Pipeline, Its Compute, and What the Nature Version Changed

DeepSeek-R1 is a GRPO case, not a TRPO one: GRPO entered through DeepSeekMath in February 2024, and TRPO is the 2015 ancestor of PPO. This module reads the report stage by stage (R1-Zero's rule-based RL, the four-stage R1 pipeline, its GPU-hours, what failed), flags every place where two versions or two sections of the report disagree, and sets later recipes against it. Afterwards you can reproduce R1's schedule arithmetic and say which of its choices later reports kept, changed or dropped.

1. Lineage: what R1 inherited and what it originated

The request behind this course says R1 introduced TRPO. It did not. R1's Section 2.1 names its algorithm and its source: GRPO "was originally proposed to simplify the training process and reduce the resource consumption of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)", credited to Shao et al., 20241. That paper is DeepSeekMath, which describes GRPO as a PPO variant that "foregoes the critic model, instead estimating the baseline from group scores"4. TRPO is Schulman et al.'s 2015 trust-region method2, the thing PPO simplified3. Module 3 derives both, Module 9 derives GRPO, and Module 1 counts R1's GPU-hours; this module reads what R1 did.

Common error

"R1 introduced TRPO" and "R1 was first" both fail on dates: TRPO is from 2015 and GRPO from February 2024. R1's own claim is narrower. Version 1 calls R1-Zero "the first open research to validate that reasoning capabilities of LLMs can be incentivized purely through RL, without the need for SFT"9.

The o1 row needs a caveat. OpenAI's announcement page returned HTTP 403 when this course's source map was built, and I did not work around it. The month rests on two second-hand statements: R1's Appendix F.1 says its Qwen2-Math-7B experiment ran before "the first reasoning model, OpenAI-o1 (September 2024)"1, and Lambert's bibliography lists "Introducing OpenAI o1-preview", September 202410.

R1 and Kimi k1.5 appeared on the same day: Lambert's Table 4 dates both 2025-01-2210, and the first version of k1.5 is also 22 January 202511. k1.5 claimed o1-matching numbers with a different objective and no GRPO11 (Module 28), so January 2025 was not a one-recipe result.

What R1 did contribute is narrower and easier to defend. It showed, on a 671B-parameter mixture-of-experts base with 37B parameters active per token and 14.8T pretraining tokens1 (App. A.1), that rule-based rewards and GRPO elicit long reasoning with no SFT stage first. It then published a multi-stage recipe that turns that behavior into a usable assistant, released weights, and, in v2, supplied enough hyperparameters to do the arithmetic. Its conclusion states the claim as a recipe: "the key to unlocking this potential lies not in large-scale human annotation but in the provision of hard reasoning questions, a reliable verifier, and sufficient computational resources for reinforcement learning"1 (§6). Smaller verifiable-domain RL existed earlier. Lambert's survey lists STaR (2022-2023) and VinePPO, and notes that Tulu 3 ran PPO against correctness rewards without losing general performance; what those methods lacked was R1's scale, or they "made sacrifices in overall performance"10 (§7.3.1).

2. R1-Zero: RL on a base model

2.1 What was trained, and where the detail lives

R1-Zero starts from DeepSeek-V3-Base and applies GRPO with no SFT stage1. The reward is a rule-based accuracy term plus a format term (Eq. 4), "combined with the same weight", and the report says it abstains "from applying neural reward models—whether outcome-based or process-based—to reasoning tasks" because they are "susceptible to reward hacking during large-scale reinforcement learning"1 (§2.2). Module 10 reads that reward, the template and the prompt data line by line, and Module 4 §4.5 covers why process reward models did not survive RL at scale; Section 6 below gives R1's own reasons. The optimizer settings are the ones Module 9's Table 4 sets beside DeepSeekMath and later recipes: learning rate 3e-6, KL coefficient 0.001, rollout temperature 1, 16 samples per question, and a maximum length of 32,768 tokens until step 8.2k and 65,536 after it1 (§2.1).

One comparison in the report is about the algorithm itself. Supplementary A.3 trains DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite (16B mixture-of-experts, 2.4B active) on MATH with PPO at GAE \(\lambda = 0.95\), PPO at \(\lambda = 1.0\), and GRPO. At the open-source default of 0.95, PPO "performs considerably worse than GRPO"; at 1.0 it is "nearing that of GRPO"1 (A.3, Figure 4). At the last plotted step the three curves sit at about 50.1, 53.5 and 55.4 percent MATH accuracy (read off the plot, accurate to about ±0.3; the same reading as Module 9's Figure 6). The report's argument is about tuning cost and memory, not that a tuned critic loses: "PPO can achieve comparable performance when appropriately tuned", but it demands extra hyperparameter search and a second policy-sized model1. Module 3 sets this against the RLOO and VAPO evidence.

One more thing to carry from the equations. R1 prints its objective (Eq. 1, repeated as Eq. 11) with the ratio \(\pi_\theta(o_i\mid q)/\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_i\mid q)\) at sequence level and no \(1/|o_i|\) factor, whereas DeepSeekMath's Eq. 3 is per-token with \(1/|o_i|\)4. The report does not say which form ran. Treat it as a source-level conflict (Section 7.3); Module 12 §2.4 works through both readings.

2.2 The schedule, as arithmetic

Read the run-size sentences of §2.1 as a ledger. A training step is "a single policy update operation" (caption of the report's Figure 1)1. Each step uses 32 unique questions with 16 samples each, a batch of 512 outputs. Each rollout generates 8,192 outputs, "randomly split into 16 mini-batches and trained for only a single inner epoch". So 8,192 is 512 questions times 16 samples, one rollout feeds 16 steps, and 10,400 steps are 650 rollouts and 5,324,800 sampled outputs (derived). The reference model is replaced by the latest policy "every 400 steps": 26 multiples of 400 up to 10,400, or every 25 rollouts. The run draws 332,800 questions (10,400 times 32); at the stated 1.6 epochs that implies about 208,000 distinct prompts (derived). Module 10 §2.5 checks that against the 80K reasoning prompts in R1's Table 4, which describe R1; the report does not give R1-Zero's prompt count.

Defaults are R1-Zero's1. With \(Q\) questions, \(G\) samples, \(K\) minibatches and \(T\) steps: outputs per rollout \(QGK\), rollouts \(T/K\), distinct prompts \(TQ/E\), mean staleness \((K-1)/2\).

Two consequences follow. The schedule is off-policy by construction: minibatch \(j\) is trained after \(j-1\) updates on outputs sampled before the first of them, so staleness runs from 0 to 15 and one minibatch in sixteen is on-policy (derived). That is where the clip begins to act; DeepSeekMath took a single update per rollout, where the clip is inert (Module 12 §1.1). Second, the report does not say whether the random split keeps a question's 16 outputs together. The advantage comes from a question's 16 rewards and is fixed when the group is scored, so the split changes which outputs share an optimizer step, not their advantages (my reading of the equations).

2.3 The rollout side

Supplementary B.1 describes four modules: rollout, inference, rule-based reward and training. Rollout runs vLLM workers holding the actor, with expert parallelism across nodes and "redundant copies of hotspot experts" to balance expert load. The multi-token-prediction head is used "for self-speculative decoding, significantly accelerating the decoding speed and effectively minimizing the completion time for the longest samples"1. The rule-based reward module (code executors, answer matchers, format checkers) needs no GPU memory but "tends to be time-consuming", so it runs asynchronously and overlaps rollout and inference. Training packs sequences into fixed-length chunks with a best-fit strategy, reuses DualPipe, and each module's models are offloaded from VRAM to host memory or disk when its phase ends.

For an inference engineer the omissions matter more than the list. The report gives no tokens per second, no split of GPUs between rollout and training, and no per-phase seconds, so the 68.5 s average per optimizer step on 512 GPUs that Module 1 derives from the 198 hours (derived) is the only timing the text supports. System Design Modules 36 (post-training pipelines), 37 (RL training infrastructure) and 38 (the open-source RL frameworks) cover that side.

Version 2 reports AIME 2024 pass@1 rising "from an initial 15.6% to 77.9%", and 86.7% with self-consistency decoding1 (§2.3). Version 1 reports 15.6% to 71.0%, and 86.7% with majority voting9 (§2.2.4). It gives no step count, only "thousands of RL steps"; its Figure 2, read by eye, ends near step 8,400, while version 2's curve runs to 10,400.

3. R1: four stages around the same RL core

3.1 Why R1-Zero was not the product

The report says R1-Zero "struggles with challenges like poor readability, and language mixing", which it ties to a base trained mostly on Chinese and English, and that its RL stage "is narrowly focused on reasoning tasks, resulting in limited performance in broader areas such as writing and open-domain question answering"1 (§1, §3). The cold start was also a product decision. The report says its motivation "is primarily product-driven", because users find first-person reasoning more intuitive, and cautions that such patterns "may elicit unwarranted trust" and reflect "DeepSeek-engineered heuristics" rather than human-like intelligence1 (B.3.2).

3.2 Stage by stage

  1. Cold-start SFT. Thousands of long chains of thought: R1-Zero outputs sampled at temperature 1.0, kept if the answer is correct and the format readable, rewritten by DeepSeek-V3 and checked by humans. Output: Dev1.
  2. Reasoning RL. The R1-Zero recipe started from Dev1, with a language-consistency reward and a printed clip of 10 (the report's "first stage of RL"). The report states no step count. Output: Dev2.
  3. Rejection-sampling SFT. About 600K reasoning and 200K other samples; this SFT restarts from V3-Base, so Dev2 supplies data rather than weights. Output: Dev3.
  4. RL for all scenarios. The report's "second stage of RL": 1,700 steps at temperature 0.7, with reward models active only in the last 400. Output: R1.
  5. Distillation. About 800K samples fine-tune Qwen2.5 and Llama students with SFT only; no RL on the students.

Step 1, the cold start. Both versions say "thousands" of cold-start samples19. Lambert's textbook summary of the recipe says "100K+"10 (§3.2.3); the report does not support that figure and I follow the report.

Step 2. The first RL stage, started from Dev1, reuses R1-Zero's sampler (16 outputs per question, 32,768 tokens) with two changes. One is the language-consistency reward, "the proportion of target language words in the CoT" (Eq. 7). The other is the printed clip, "the GRPO clip ratio ε to 10"1 (§3.2.1). The report says "a lower value can lead to the truncation of gradients for a significant number of tokens ... while a higher value may cause instability", and gives neither an ablation nor a step count; v1 says only "until it achieves convergence"9. The nearest sentence is a credit line in Section 7: "Zhibin Gou proposed a large PPO clipping strategy to enhance GRPO performance, demonstrating its significance alongside Zhihong Shao and Junxiao Song"1. That is a lead, not an explanation: it does not say the strategy is ε = 10. Module 12 §2.4 works out what 10 means under token-level and sequence-level readings and marks its own reading as unverified. The language reward has a measured cost: in the ablation on a distilled 7B model, math stays comparable and coding shows "a slight degradation"1 (B.6).

Step 3. The second SFT goes back to DeepSeek-V3-Base: B.4.2 says both SFT stages fine-tune V3-Base for 2 to 3 epochs1, and v1 says the data are used to "retrain the DeepSeek-V3-Base model"9. Reasoning samples come from rejection sampling on the step-2 checkpoint, correct answers only; some prompts added at this stage are judged by DeepSeek-V3 against the ground truth, and chains with mixed languages, long paragraphs or code blocks are filtered out1 (B.3.3). The non-reasoning samples reuse DeepSeek-V3's SFT data, with generated chain of thought for some and none for queries such as "hello". Table 5 of the report splits the 804,745 samples into 395,285 math, 211,129 code, 10,124 STEM, 10,395 logic and 177,812 general, averaging 5,355.3 tokens: about 4.31 billion tokens per epoch, or 8.6 to 12.9 billion over two or three epochs (derived).

Step 4. The second RL stage "retains most of the parameters from the first stage", with the temperature lowered to 0.7 because "higher temperatures in this stage lead to incoherent generation". Its reward is a sum (Eqs. 8 to 10): the rule reward for reasoning, a reward-model score plus a format reward for general data, and the language term. Of the 1,700 steps, general data and preference rewards enter "exclusively in the final 400", steps 1,301 to 1,700, because "more training steps with the model based preference reward signal may lead to reward hacking"1 (§3.2.2). Module 16 §1.2 reads the mixture and Module 19 §3 the hacking curve in Supplementary Figure 6, whose pass@1 values (about 0.346 at the peak, about 0.289 at the end) were read by eye from the PDF there. The helpful reward model trains on 66,000 pairs judged by DeepSeek-V3 and the safety model on 106,000 point-wise labels (Module 4).

Key insight

R1-Zero already held most of the math score. Pure RL got AIME to 77.9 (v2) and MATH-500 to 95.9; the four stages add 1.9 and 1.4 points there and lose 9.3 on CNMO and 4.3 on GPQA Diamond (derived from Table 4). What they buy is code (+15.9 LiveCodeBench, +585 Codeforces rating), instruction following (+36.7 IF-Eval), chat quality (+62.9 AlpacaEval) and readability, which the table does not measure.

The cold start cost reasoning before RL repaired it. At Dev1, AIME fell from 77.9 to 59.0 and CNMO from 88.1 to 58.0, and the report attributes the Dev1 degradation to "the limited size of the cold-start dataset"; step 2 then recovered 15.0 AIME points, to 74.01 (§4). On LiveCodeBench the gains come from medium and hard problems: the hard-problem pass rate runs 17.09, 23.21, 30.36, 33.16, 34.44 across the five checkpoints, while easy problems sit at 98 to 1001 (Table 14). The last stage mostly moves chat metrics: from Dev3 to R1, AlpacaEval 2.0 rises 25.5 points and ArenaHard 16.7. The report writes "25%" and "17%", which are points on the win-rate scale (derived).

For serving cost, Supplementary E.3 and E.4 report that on 366 problems from 93 competitions held in 2024, R1 averages 8,793 thinking tokens per problem at 61.8% pass@1, fewer than 7,000 on simple problems and more than 18,000 on the hardest, against 711 output tokens at 24.7% for GPT-4o-05131.

5. Distillation versus RL on a small model

Supplementary F trains one base two ways. Qwen2.5-32B-Base gets "large-scale RL training" on math, code and STEM "for over 10K steps", giving Qwen2.5-32B-Zero (named DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B in v1)19. Its settings (B.4.1) are R1-Zero's with a learning rate of 2e-6: KL 0.001, temperature 1, 16 samples of up to 32,768 tokens, 32 questions and 512 outputs per step, reference replaced every 400 steps. The other arm is SFT of the same base on the 800K R1-generated samples, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B.

The report draws two conclusions: distilling stronger models "yields excellent results, whereas smaller models relying on the large-scale RL ... require enormous computational power and may not even achieve the performance of distillation", and, second, "advancing beyond the boundaries of human intelligence may still require more powerful base models and larger-scale reinforcement learning"1 (F.1). The ladder of students shows how far the first goes: AIME 2024 pass@1 of 28.9 for the 1.5B Qwen student (GPT-4o-0513 scores 9.3, Claude-3.5-Sonnet-1022 16.0), 55.5 at 7B, 69.7 at 14B, 72.6 at 32B, and 50.4 and 70.0 for the Llama 8B and 70B students1 (Table 15).

The comparison controls less than its table suggests. The RL arm samples at least \(10{,}000 \times 512 = 5.12\) million completions of up to 32,768 tokens (derived), and the SFT arm reads 804,745 sequences per epoch for two or three epochs, but the distilled student inherits its teacher: 147K GPU-hours of R1 and the generation of the samples. "Economical" is the student's marginal cost. The arms also see different data, since the RL prompts are math, code and STEM while the SFT set includes 177,812 general samples (derived from Table 5). Qwen3 later ran the comparison with GPU-hours at one starting checkpoint: on Qwen3-8B, RL reached 67.6 on AIME'24 with 17,920 GPU-hours and left pass@64 unchanged, while on-policy distillation reached 74.4 with 1,800 GPU-hours13 (§4.7, Table 21). Module 21 sets those rows beside R1's.

A second small-model result sits in Table 17: Qwen2-Math-7B-Zero, trained with "approximately 10,000 policy gradient update steps" on a base released in August 2024, "prior to the launch of the first reasoning model, OpenAI-o1 (September 2024), to ensure the base model was not exposed to any reasoning trajectory data", reaches 22.3% on AIME 2024 and 18.1% on AIME 2025, against 7.9% and 4.6% for Qwen2-Math-7B-Instruct1.

Later evidence points the same way without settling it. Kimi k1.5 set out to avoid "Monte Carlo tree search, value functions, and process reward models"11. Llama 3 still uses outcome and stepwise reward models, but to filter SFT reasoning traces14 (§4.3.3), which is the reranking use R1 concedes. Lightman et al. found that process supervision "significantly outperforms outcome supervision" for training reward models on MATH15. The three are compatible: process labels rank and filter well, and R1's finding concerns using them as the RL reward. Module 4 §4.5 covers the mechanics.

G.1 widens the verifier in one direction: besides rules, an LLM that checks an answer against a ground truth "demonstrates particular effectiveness for tasks with well-defined, concise answers" but "exhibits limited generalizability to more complex tasks, including open-ended generation and long-form writing"1. That is the same boundary R1 draws in its limitations (§6): for tasks "that cannot be effectively evaluated by a reliable reward model, scaling up pure RL methods remains an open challenge", so it used human-annotated SFT data there and "only conduct[ed] RL for hundreds of steps"1.

7. The report across versions, and where it disagrees with itself

7.1 From v1 to v2

Version 1 is 22 pages and version 2 is 8691. The additions, by location: hyperparameters and run length (§2.1, §3.2); the RL data recipe and SFT statistics (Tables 4 and 5, B.3); the PPO-versus-GRPO study (A.3) and the RL framework (B.1); the cost table (Table 7); the reward-hacking and language-consistency ablations (B.5, B.6); reasoning-behavior counts over training (C.1, C.2); Qwen2-Math-7B-Zero (Table 17); a safety report (D.3); and results on problems released after training, 75% pass@1 on AIME 2025 against o1's 80% (E.2). 8. After R1: critiques, and what later recipes kept, changed or dropped

8.1 Four checks on R1's claims

The papers below differ from R1 in base model, data and scale, so each tests a claim or fills a gap and none is a replication. Dr. GRPO hosted DeepSeek-V3-Base and found that it "already exhibit[s] 'Aha moment'", that Qwen2.5 base models "demonstrate strong reasoning capabilities even without prompt templates", and that GRPO's two normalizers bias the update: dividing by response length favors short correct and long wrong responses, and dividing by the group's reward standard deviation up-weights questions that are too easy or too hard17 (abstract, §3.1). Its minimalist recipe reached 43.3% on AIME 2024 from Qwen2.5-Math-7B17. R1's own appendix is closer to that reading than the "aha" framing is: pretraining exposure to reasoning traces "equips the model with the capability to generate plausible solution candidates, from which reinforcement learning can effectively identify and optimize high-quality outputs"1 (A.1; Module 10 §6.3).

Spurious Rewards found that on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, randomly assigned rewards raised MATH-500 by 21.4 points against 29.1 for ground-truth rewards, traced it to a clipping bias that amplifies behaviors with high prior probability, and reported that such rewards "often fail to produce gains for other model families, such as Llama3 or OLMo2"12. It did not test DeepSeek-V3-Base, so it says nothing direct about R1-Zero. It does make an R1-Zero-style gain on a Qwen math base weak evidence about reward quality, which bears on replications built that way, including the 43.3% above. R1 itself flags contamination: n-gram decontamination "cannot prevent the paraphrase of testset", so benchmarks "released before 2024 may suffer from contamination issues"1 (D.1); its answer is the AIME 2025 result of Section 7.1. Olmo 3 argues that open-weight bases with undisclosed mid-training data can contaminate RLVR benchmarks, and releases an RL-Zero run from a fully open base18 (Module 31).

Yue et al. find RLVR-trained models ahead of their base models at small k and behind at large k, with the RLVR reasoning paths already inside the base model's sampling distribution, whereas distillation "can introduce new reasoning patterns from the teacher"19. DeepSeekMath saw the same split between Maj@K and Pass@K in 20244. R1's E.4 gives pass@64 of 90.0 against pass@1 of 79.8 for R1 itself, which is not a base-model comparison1. Module 20 weighs this evidence.

DAPO ran GRPO on Qwen2.5-32B and reached "only 30 points on AIME — a performance significantly below DeepSeek's RL (47 points)", adding that "critical training details may have been omitted in the R1 paper"20. Version 2's added hyperparameters answer part of that. The step count of the first RL stage and the reason for ε = 10 remain absent.

Where experts disagree

R1 was a method claim (verifiers, GRPO, pure RL from a base) and a set of artifacts (a pipeline, a cost, a failure list). Three disputes it started or sharpened are still open, and each can be read against a table in the report.

What did RL do in R1-Zero?

RL elicited new reasoning behavior

R1's own framing: "emergent development of advanced reasoning patterns", the "aha moment"1

R1-Zero starts from a base with no SFT, and the supplement counts behaviors over training: reflective words rise 5- to 7-fold, and "wait" is nearly absent early, occasional between steps 4,000 and 7,000, and spikes after step 8,000 (C.2).

  • A measured trajectory: AIME 15.6 to 77.9 (v2) with response length growing alongside.
  • No human reasoning traces in the loop.
  • No base-model control for the counted behaviors.
  • The same report says pretraining supplies "plausible solution candidates" and that checkpoints "inherently possess substantial potential"1.

RL selects and sharpens what the base can already produce

Yue et al.19; DeepSeekMath's Maj@K versus Pass@K4; Qwen3's pass@6413; Dr. GRPO on V3-Base17

RLVR models win at small k and lose at large k, their reasoning paths lie inside the base distribution, and Dr. GRPO finds the "aha" behavior in V3-Base itself.

  • Independent groups, different bases and methods, one direction.
  • Fits R1's own appendix on candidates.
  • ProRL argues such studies used math-heavy settings and stopped RL after "no more than hundreds of steps"21.
  • Large-k comparisons on a 30-problem benchmark are coarse.

On some bases the gain is prior amplification

Spurious Rewards12

Random rewards give +21.4 MATH-500 points on Qwen2.5-Math-7B against +29.1 for ground truth; GRPO's clip amplifies high-prior behaviors; other families mostly do not move.

  • A direct control: the reward carries no task signal.
  • Explains why a Qwen math base can mislead.
  • Does not test V3-Base, so it bears on R1-Zero only by analogy.

Prolonged RL expands the boundary

ProRL21; a two-stage reconciliation22

With KL control, reference resets and diverse tasks, over 2,000 steps on a 1.5B model found strategies "inaccessible to base models, even under extensive sampling". The reconciliation proposes sharpening first, exploration later.

  • Addresses the "too short" objection head-on.
  • Starts from a distilled checkpoint and changes several things at once, so the cause is not isolated.
  • The reconciliation is the authors' own assessment.
Assessment

The first two are less opposed than they look: R1's appendix already describes selection among candidates, so the live question is whether more compute adds anything beyond selection. Quote R1's gains as pass@1 gains and do not read "emergent" as "absent in the base". What would settle it: pass@k at large k on one contamination-free base, matched data, runs as long as R1-Zero's 10,400 steps, and a base-model control for the counted behaviors. Module 20 holds the full ledger.

Process or outcome supervision for the RL reward?

Outcome and rules only

R1 Supplementary G.21; Kimi k1.511

Step boundaries, step labels and hackability make a model-based PRM a poor RL reward; a verifier is cheaper to trust.

  • Simplest pipeline; scaled to 10,400 steps without a learned scorer.
  • Two independent reports on the same day reached the same design.
  • G.2 gives no numbers or ablation, and frames the attempts as early failures.

Step-level labels help where they exist

Lightman et al.15; Llama 3's stepwise reward models14

Process supervision "significantly outperforms outcome supervision" for MATH reward models; Llama 3 filters SFT traces with stepwise models.

  • A measured ranking advantage.
  • Used to rank or filter, not as the reward of a long-chain RL run.
  • Needs step labels, which cost human effort.
Assessment

Both hold. R1 concedes that PRMs rerank well, and the two positions describe different uses. None of the reports read for this module trains a long-chain RL run on a learned PRM reward, so the question is untested rather than answered; a matched-compute run with a PRM reward against an outcome reward would settle it.

RL or distillation for a strong small reasoning model?

Distill from a stronger teacher

R1 Supplementary F.11; Qwen313

On one 32B base, SFT on R1 samples beats 10K+ RL steps by 25.6 AIME points; Qwen3-8B reaches 74.4 against 67.6 at 1/10 of the GPU-hours.

  • Two reports, same direction.
  • Cheap for the student.
  • Capped by the teacher, whose cost is not counted.
  • R1's arms differ in data as well as method.

RL remains the way past the teacher

R1's second conclusion1; ProRL21

"Advancing beyond the boundaries of human intelligence may still require more powerful base models and larger-scale reinforcement learning"; ProRL reports further gains over a distilled 1.5B start.

  • Only route to a model stronger than every available teacher.
  • No matched-compute comparison exists in the sources read.
Assessment

When a stronger teacher exists and the student is small, distillation wins on both quality and cost in the two measured cases, and R1 itself says so. RL is the only route past the teacher, but I found no source that compares it with distillation at matched compute from the same start with the teacher's cost counted; that comparison would settle it.

What is changing

R1's own list of open problems has moved. For tasks without a reliable verifier, where the report says scaling pure RL "remains an open challenge"1, 2026 recipes train rubric-based generative judges inside the RL loop (in V4 the actor itself is the judge), and V4 replaces mixed RL with on-policy distillation of specialists6, a step Lambert says labs are "far from unanimous" about10 (§3.2.4). Three things in R1's own record stay open: the step count and the reason for ε = 10 in the first RL stage, which training steps the 80 hours cover, and what the Nature review changed, which I could not read. I cite V4 without a date because the source map records an unexplained mismatch between its arXiv identifier and the date the arXiv API reports. V3.2 and V4 are the reports in this module most likely to be superseded.
