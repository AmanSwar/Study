# Module 1.4 — Product Measures and the Fubini-Tonelli Theorem

> *"The integral over a product space equals the iterated integral."* — Fubini's declaration (1907), valid for $\sigma$-finite measures under the right hypotheses.

## Prerequisites

- **Module 1.1** — $\sigma$-algebras, measures, Carathéodory extension, uniqueness of $\sigma$-finite extensions.
- **Module 1.2** — measurable functions, modes of convergence, measurable pre-images of product-constructed sets.
- **Module 1.3** — Lebesgue integral, MCT, Fatou, DCT, layer cake formula.
- **Module 0.4** — Basic iterated Riemann integrals (for contrast).

## Overview

A probabilistic event often depends on two or more inputs: a Brownian path at time $s$ *and* at time $t$; a loss $L(X, Y)$ as a function of a systematic factor $X$ and idiosyncratic $Y$; an FFT integral against a two-dimensional Fourier kernel. Whenever we write an integral $\int f(x, y) \, d\mu(x) \, d\nu(y)$ we silently invoke a **product measure** $\mu \times \nu$ on a **product $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$, and we swap iterated integrals appealing to **Fubini's theorem** or **Tonelli's theorem**.

This module does three things:

1. **Construction.** Given two $\sigma$-finite measure spaces $(X, \mathcal F, \mu)$ and $(Y, \mathcal G, \nu)$, define the product $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$ and the product measure $\mu \times \nu$. Uniqueness follows from the $\sigma$-finite case of the Carathéodory extension theorem.
2. **Fubini-Tonelli.** Tonelli: for $f \ge 0$ measurable on $(X \times Y, \mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G)$, the two iterated integrals $\iint f \, d\mu \, d\nu$ and $\iint f \, d\nu \, d\mu$ both equal the double integral $\int f \, d(\mu \times \nu)$. Fubini: for $f$ integrable on the product, the same holds but the iterated integrals only need exist for $\mu$-a.e. $x$ (resp. $\nu$-a.e. $y$).
3. **Applications.** Computation of convolutions, multivariate densities, characteristic functions of sums, moments of Gaussians, Black-Scholes volatility smiles by integration against implied-density kernels, copulas, iterated expectations for martingales, and more.

The punchline of the module is: "if $f \ge 0$ is measurable, iterated integrals work; if $f$ is signed but $\int |f| < \infty$, they still work; otherwise, you can get any answer depending on the order of integration."

## Topic 1.4.1 — Product σ-algebras

### Definition 1.4.1 (Measurable rectangle, product σ-algebra)

Let $(X, \mathcal F)$ and $(Y, \mathcal G)$ be measurable spaces. A **measurable rectangle** is a set of the form $A \times B$ with $A \in \mathcal F$ and $B \in \mathcal G$. The **product $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$ is the $\sigma$-algebra on $X \times Y$ generated by measurable rectangles:
$$\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G := \sigma\!\left(\{A \times B : A \in \mathcal F, B \in \mathcal G\}\right).$$

**Warning.** $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$ is *generated by* — not equal to — the family of measurable rectangles, which is not closed under intersections of infinitely many rectangles, nor arbitrary unions. Many sets in $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$ are *not* rectangles (e.g., the diagonal $\{(x, x) : x \in X\}$ in a sensible setting).

### Lemma 1.4.2 (Sections of measurable sets are measurable)

Let $E \in \mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$. For each $x \in X$ define the **$x$-section** $E_x := \{y \in Y : (x, y) \in E\}$; for each $y \in Y$ define the **$y$-section** $E^y := \{x \in X : (x, y) \in E\}$. Then $E_x \in \mathcal G$ for every $x$ and $E^y \in \mathcal F$ for every $y$.

**Proof.** Let $\mathcal E := \{E \subset X \times Y : E_x \in \mathcal G \text{ for all } x, E^y \in \mathcal F \text{ for all } y\}$. We show $\mathcal E$ is a $\sigma$-algebra containing all measurable rectangles; then $\mathcal E \supset \mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$.

- *Rectangles.* For $R = A \times B$ with $A \in \mathcal F, B \in \mathcal G$: $R_x = B$ if $x \in A$, $R_x = \emptyset$ otherwise; both are in $\mathcal G$. Similarly for $R^y$.
- *Complement.* $(E^c)_x = Y \setminus E_x$ and $(E^c)^y = X \setminus E^y$; complement in $\mathcal G, \mathcal F$ respectively.
- *Countable union.* $(\bigcup_n E_n)_x = \bigcup_n (E_n)_x$ and analogously for the other section; closed under countable unions.

Hence $\mathcal E$ is a $\sigma$-algebra containing the generating set, so $\mathcal E \supset \mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$. $\blacksquare$

### Lemma 1.4.3 (Sections of measurable functions are measurable)

Let $f: (X \times Y, \mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G) \to (Z, \mathcal H)$ be measurable. For each $x$, the map $y \mapsto f(x, y)$ is $(\mathcal G, \mathcal H)$-measurable; for each $y$, the map $x \mapsto f(x, y)$ is $(\mathcal F, \mathcal H)$-measurable.

**Proof.** For any $H \in \mathcal H$, $\{y : f(x, y) \in H\} = (f^{-1}(H))_x \in \mathcal G$ by Lemma 1.4.2. Similarly for the other variable. $\blacksquare$

### Example 1.4.4 (Borel vs. product Borel)

When $(X, Y) = (\mathbb R, \mathbb R)$ with Borel $\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$, we can form $\mathcal B(\mathbb R) \otimes \mathcal B(\mathbb R)$ and compare it with $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^2)$ (the Borel $\sigma$-algebra on $\mathbb R^2$ under the product topology).

**Fact.** $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^2) = \mathcal B(\mathbb R) \otimes \mathcal B(\mathbb R)$. More generally, for separable metric spaces $X, Y$, $\mathcal B(X \times Y) = \mathcal B(X) \otimes \mathcal B(Y)$.

*Proof.* Every open set $U \subset \mathbb R^2$ is a countable union of open rectangles $I \times J$ (open intervals with rational endpoints), hence lies in $\mathcal B(\mathbb R) \otimes \mathcal B(\mathbb R)$. Conversely, each $A \times B$ with $A, B$ Borel in $\mathbb R$ lies in $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^2)$: $A \times Y = \pi_1^{-1}(A)$ is Borel in $\mathbb R^2$ (preimage of Borel under continuous projection), and $A \times B = (A \times \mathbb R) \cap (\mathbb R \times B)$.

**Warning for non-separable spaces.** For two copies of $[0,1]$ with its Borel $\sigma$-algebra, things work (the space is separable); but in general Banach spaces you must be careful. For Lebesgue-completed $\sigma$-algebras, the identification fails: $\mathcal L(\mathbb R) \otimes \mathcal L(\mathbb R) \subsetneq \mathcal L(\mathbb R^2)$. We normally construct product measures *first* on the uncompleted product and then complete.

### Lemma 1.4.5 (Generators of product σ-algebras)

Let $\mathcal C \subset 2^X$ and $\mathcal D \subset 2^Y$ be collections of sets with $\sigma(\mathcal C) = \mathcal F$ and $\sigma(\mathcal D) = \mathcal G$, and assume there exist sequences $X_n \in \mathcal C$, $Y_n \in \mathcal D$ with $X_n \uparrow X$ and $Y_n \uparrow Y$. Then
$$\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G = \sigma(\{C \times D : C \in \mathcal C, D \in \mathcal D\}).$$

**Proof.** The right side is contained in $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$ obviously. For the reverse, fix $A \in \mathcal F$ and consider $\{B \in \mathcal G : A \times B \in \text{RHS}\}$. This is a $\sigma$-algebra. It contains $\mathcal D$ (because for $D \in \mathcal D$, $A \times D$ may not be in RHS directly, but we use another argument): ... The clean way is via a $\pi$-$\lambda$ argument. Set $\mathcal M := \{A \in \mathcal F : A \times D \in \text{RHS for all } D \in \mathcal D\}$. We show $\mathcal M$ is a $\sigma$-algebra containing $\mathcal C$, hence $\mathcal M = \mathcal F$. Then the symmetric argument using $\mathcal Y$'s sequence $Y_n$ gives the result. (Details in Folland §2.5.) $\blacksquare$

This lemma is why we don't have to use all Borel sets — a generating collection (like half-lines or rectangles) suffices to generate the product $\sigma$-algebra.

## Topic 1.4.2 — Product Measures

### Theorem 1.4.6 (Existence and uniqueness of product measure)

Let $(X, \mathcal F, \mu)$ and $(Y, \mathcal G, \nu)$ be $\sigma$-finite measure spaces. Then there exists a unique measure $\mu \times \nu$ on $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$ such that
$$(\mu \times \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A) \nu(B) \qquad \text{for all } A \in \mathcal F, B \in \mathcal G.$$
The measure $\mu \times \nu$ is called the **product measure**.

**Proof. Step 1 (Sections of measurable sets have measurable "size" functions).** Let $E \in \mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$. By Lemma 1.4.2, each section $E_x \in \mathcal G$, so $\nu(E_x)$ is well-defined; each $E^y \in \mathcal F$, so $\mu(E^y)$ is well-defined. We claim the map $x \mapsto \nu(E_x)$ is $\mathcal F$-measurable and $y \mapsto \mu(E^y)$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable.

*Proof (via monotone class).* Let $\mathcal L := \{E \in \mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G : x \mapsto \nu(E_x) \text{ is measurable}\}$. We show:

- $\mathcal L$ contains all rectangles: for $E = A \times B$, $\nu(E_x) = \nu(B) \mathbf 1_A(x)$, a measurable function.
- $\mathcal L$ is closed under finite disjoint unions: if $E = E_1 \sqcup E_2$, $\nu(E_x) = \nu((E_1)_x) + \nu((E_2)_x)$.
- $\mathcal L$ is closed under increasing unions: if $E_n \uparrow E$, then $(E_n)_x \uparrow E_x$, so $\nu((E_n)_x) \uparrow \nu(E_x)$ by continuity of measures; each $\nu((E_n)_x)$ is measurable, so the limit is measurable.
- *Closure under decreasing unions with finiteness.* If $E_n \downarrow E$ and $\nu((E_1)_x) < \infty$ for a.e. $x$ (use $\sigma$-finiteness to first restrict to a finite-measure part of $X \times Y$), then $\nu((E_n)_x) \downarrow \nu(E_x)$, hence measurable.

Hence $\mathcal L$ is a monotone class containing the algebra generated by rectangles (finite disjoint unions of rectangles form an algebra; see below). By the monotone class theorem, $\mathcal L$ contains $\sigma(\text{rectangles}) = \mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$.

For the full $\sigma$-finite case without assumed finiteness: cover $X \times Y$ by a countable increasing family $X_n \times Y_n$ with $\mu(X_n), \nu(Y_n) < \infty$. Apply the above to $E \cap (X_n \times Y_n)$ and take the limit. This works because $\sigma$-finiteness allows us to write everything as an increasing union of finite-measure pieces.

**Step 2 (Construction).** Define, for $E \in \mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$,
$$\rho_1(E) := \int_X \nu(E_x) \, d\mu(x), \qquad \rho_2(E) := \int_Y \mu(E^y) \, d\nu(y).$$
Both are well-defined by Step 1, non-negative, and countably additive (MCT applied to countable disjoint unions: $\nu((E_n)_x)$ for disjoint $E_n$ gives $\nu(\bigsqcup_n (E_n)_x)$ by countable additivity of $\nu$, and then MCT on the integral over $X$).

Both $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ agree on rectangles: $\rho_1(A \times B) = \int \nu(B) \mathbf 1_A(x) \, d\mu(x) = \nu(B) \mu(A)$ and similarly $\rho_2(A \times B) = \mu(A)\nu(B)$.

**Step 3 (Uniqueness).** The collection of finite disjoint unions of measurable rectangles forms an algebra $\mathcal A$ in $X \times Y$ (finite union, intersection, and complement of a rectangle lies in $\mathcal A$: a little algebra-of-rectangles exercise). Then $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G = \sigma(\mathcal A)$. Both $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ are measures on $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$ agreeing on $\mathcal A$; by $\sigma$-finiteness of $\mu$ and $\nu$ (so of $\rho_i$ on $\mathcal A$), the uniqueness part of the Carathéodory extension theorem (proven via $\pi$-$\lambda$ in Module 1.1) gives $\rho_1 = \rho_2$ on $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$. Call this common measure $\mu \times \nu$. $\blacksquare$

### Remark. We have already proved something close to Tonelli

Note that Step 2 proved
$$(\mu \times \nu)(E) = \int_X \nu(E_x) \, d\mu(x) = \int_Y \mu(E^y) \, d\nu(y),$$
which is exactly Tonelli's theorem applied to $f = \mathbf 1_E$. The next step is to promote this to general non-negative measurable $f$.

### Proposition 1.4.7 (Counting measure on $\mathbb N$ is self-product)

Let $X = Y = \mathbb N$ with counting measure. Then $\mu \times \nu$ is counting measure on $\mathbb N^2$. For any non-negative double sequence $a_{ij}$,
$$\int_{\mathbb N^2} a \, d(\mu \times \nu) = \sum_{i, j} a_{ij} = \sum_i \sum_j a_{ij} = \sum_j \sum_i a_{ij}.$$
So Tonelli for counting measure is the statement "for non-negative double series, iterated sums agree." This is the first "proof" of the infamous rearrangement fact.

## Topic 1.4.3 — Tonelli's Theorem

### Theorem 1.4.8 (Tonelli's theorem)

Let $(X, \mathcal F, \mu)$ and $(Y, \mathcal G, \nu)$ be $\sigma$-finite measure spaces. Let $f: X \times Y \to [0, \infty]$ be $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$-measurable. Then:

1. For $\mu$-a.e. $x$, the function $y \mapsto f(x, y)$ is $\mathcal G$-measurable.
2. The map $x \mapsto \int_Y f(x, y) \, d\nu(y)$ is $\mathcal F$-measurable.
3. Symmetrically for $y$.
4. The iterated integrals equal the product integral:
$$\int_{X \times Y} f \, d(\mu \times \nu) = \int_X \left(\int_Y f(x, y) \, d\nu(y)\right) d\mu(x) = \int_Y \left(\int_X f(x, y) \, d\mu(x)\right) d\nu(y).$$

**Proof.** Item 1 follows from Lemma 1.4.3 (sections of measurables are measurable).

For items 2, 3, 4, use the **standard machine**: prove the statement for $f = \mathbf 1_E$ (indicator), extend to non-negative simple functions by linearity, and to non-negative measurable functions by the approximation theorem and MCT.

**Step 1: Indicator $f = \mathbf 1_E$, $E \in \mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$.**
$\int_Y f(x, y) \, d\nu(y) = \int_Y \mathbf 1_E(x, y) \, d\nu(y) = \nu(E_x)$, which is $\mathcal F$-measurable by Step 1 of Theorem 1.4.6.
The full equality $(\mu \times \nu)(E) = \int \nu(E_x) \, d\mu(x) = \int \mu(E^y) \, d\nu(y)$ was proved in Theorem 1.4.6, Step 3.

**Step 2: Simple function $\varphi = \sum a_i \mathbf 1_{E_i}$.**
By linearity of the integral (Module 1.3), all three integrals in (4) extend linearly from indicators to simple functions, preserving equality.

**Step 3: Non-negative measurable $f$.**
By the approximation theorem (Module 1.2), there exist simple $0 \le \varphi_n \uparrow f$ pointwise. Fix $x$: $\varphi_n(x, \cdot) \uparrow f(x, \cdot)$ in $y$, so by MCT,
$$\int_Y \varphi_n(x, y) \, d\nu(y) \;\uparrow\; \int_Y f(x, y) \, d\nu(y).$$
The left side is measurable in $x$ (item 2 for simple functions), so as a pointwise limit, the right side is measurable. Now apply MCT again to the integrating variable $x$:
$$\int_X \int_Y \varphi_n(x, y) \, d\nu(y) \, d\mu(x) \;\uparrow\; \int_X \int_Y f(x, y) \, d\nu(y) \, d\mu(x).$$
And directly $\int_{X \times Y} \varphi_n \, d(\mu \times \nu) \uparrow \int_{X \times Y} f \, d(\mu \times \nu)$ by MCT. Since the equality holds for each simple $\varphi_n$ (Step 2), it holds in the limit for $f$. The symmetric iterated integral is handled identically. $\blacksquare$

### Remark. No integrability assumption

Tonelli requires only non-negativity. The iterated integrals may be $+\infty$, but they are equal. In particular, even if both iterated integrals equal $+\infty$, that is the same value.

### Example 1.4.9 (Dini-style formula via Tonelli)

Let $f: X \to [0, \infty]$ measurable. Using the layer cake and Tonelli:
$$\int_X f \, d\mu = \int_X \int_0^{f(x)} dt \, d\mu(x) = \int_0^\infty \mu(\{f > t\}) \, dt,$$
the layer cake formula (1.3.30) restated. The Tonelli swap is legitimate because the integrand $(x, t) \mapsto \mathbf 1_{\{0 \le t < f(x)\}}$ is non-negative and measurable (the set $\{(x, t) : 0 \le t < f(x)\}$ is in $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal B([0, \infty))$; check by writing it as $\bigcup_{r \in \mathbb Q_{> 0}}\{f > r\} \times [0, r)$).

### Worked Example 1.4.10 (A classical trick: double integration to compute an improper integral)

Compute $I := \int_0^\infty \frac{\sin x}{x} dx$ (as an improper Riemann integral).

Write $\frac{1}{x} = \int_0^\infty e^{-xt} \, dt$ for $x > 0$. Then formally
$$I = \int_0^\infty \sin(x) \int_0^\infty e^{-xt} \, dt \, dx = \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty \sin(x) e^{-xt} \, dx \, dt = \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{1 + t^2} dt = \frac{\pi}{2}.$$
**However**, the Lebesgue theory cannot directly justify this: $\int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty |\sin x| e^{-xt} \, dx \, dt = \int_0^\infty \int_0^\infty |\sin x| e^{-xt} \, dt \, dx = \int_0^\infty |\sin x|/x \, dx = \infty$, so Fubini does not apply. The rigorous argument uses a cutoff: consider $I_R := \int_0^R \sin(x)/x \, dx$, represent $1/x$ as the $t$-integral, swap Tonelli for *the absolute value on the bounded cutoff* (which is integrable on $[0, R] \times [0, \infty)$), and then let $R \to \infty$. This is carried out as an exercise.

### Theorem 1.4.11 (Gauss' integral via Tonelli)

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} \, dx = \sqrt{\pi}.$$

**Proof.** Let $I = \int e^{-x^2} dx$. By Tonelli applied to $e^{-(x^2 + y^2)} \ge 0$:
$$I^2 = \iint e^{-x^2} e^{-y^2} \, dx \, dy = \iint e^{-(x^2+y^2)} \, dx \, dy.$$
Switch to polar coordinates $(x, y) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$; by the change of variables theorem (Module 0.2 / classical), $dx \, dy = r \, dr \, d\theta$.
$$I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2} r \, dr \, d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \pi.$$
Hence $I = \sqrt\pi$. $\blacksquare$

This is the first indispensable use of Tonelli in probability: it verifies that the Gaussian $(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2} e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ is indeed a probability density.

## Topic 1.4.4 — Fubini's Theorem

### Theorem 1.4.12 (Fubini's theorem)

Let $(X, \mathcal F, \mu)$ and $(Y, \mathcal G, \nu)$ be $\sigma$-finite. Let $f: X \times Y \to \mathbb R$ (or $\mathbb C$) be $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$-measurable and $\mu \times \nu$-integrable (i.e. $\int |f| \, d(\mu \times \nu) < \infty$). Then:

1. For $\mu$-a.e. $x$, the function $y \mapsto f(x, y)$ is in $L^1(\nu)$.
2. The function $F(x) := \int_Y f(x, y) \, d\nu(y)$ is defined $\mu$-a.e. and is in $L^1(\mu)$.
3. Symmetrically for $y$.
4.
$$\int_{X \times Y} f \, d(\mu \times \nu) = \int_X F(x) \, d\mu(x) = \int_Y \left(\int_X f(x, y) \, d\mu(x)\right) d\nu(y).$$

**Proof.** Apply Tonelli to $|f|$: by hypothesis, $\int \int |f(x, y)| \, d\nu(y) \, d\mu(x) = \int |f| \, d(\mu \times \nu) < \infty$. Hence the inner integral $\int |f(x, \cdot)| \, d\nu$ is finite for $\mu$-a.e. $x$ (since a non-negative measurable function with finite integral is finite a.e., Proposition 1.3.20). On this full-measure set, $y \mapsto f(x, y)$ is in $L^1(\nu)$; off of it, set $F(x) := 0$ (conventionally).

Decompose $f = f^+ - f^-$. Apply Tonelli to each non-negative part separately:
$$\int \int f^\pm(x, y) \, d\nu(y) \, d\mu(x) = \int f^\pm \, d(\mu \times \nu),$$
each of which is finite (since $f^\pm \le |f|$). Subtract:
$$\int F \, d\mu = \int \int f^+ \, d\nu \, d\mu - \int \int f^- \, d\nu \, d\mu = \int f^+ \, d(\mu \times \nu) - \int f^- \, d(\mu \times \nu) = \int f \, d(\mu \times \nu).$$
The symmetric integral is the same. $\blacksquare$

### Remark. The order "check integrability of $|f|$, then swap"

The standard Fubini invocation is a two-step protocol:

1. Compute $\iint |f| \, d\nu \, d\mu$ (or $\iint |f| \, d\mu \, d\nu$) using Tonelli. If finite, proceed.
2. Invoke Fubini to swap iterated integrals of $f$ itself.

If the $|f|$-integral is infinite, **do not swap** — the iterated integrals of $f$ may disagree.

### Example 1.4.13 (A canonical non-Fubini example)

Let $X = Y = [0, 1]$ with Lebesgue measure. Define
$$f(x, y) = \begin{cases} \frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}, & (x, y) \ne (0, 0) \\ 0, & (x, y) = (0, 0). \end{cases}$$

*Iterated integrals.* Note that $\frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left(\frac{-y}{x^2 + y^2}\right)$ (check). So
$$\int_0^1 f(x, y) \, dy = \left[\frac{-y}{x^2 + y^2}\right]_{y=0}^{y=1} = \frac{-1}{x^2 + 1}.$$
Then $\int_0^1 \int_0^1 f \, dy \, dx = \int_0^1 \frac{-1}{x^2 + 1} dx = -\frac{\pi}{4}$.

By antisymmetry in $(x, y)$: $f(x, y) = -f(y, x)$, so $\int_0^1 \int_0^1 f \, dx \, dy = +\frac{\pi}{4}$.

*Conclusion.* The two iterated integrals differ: $-\pi/4 \ne +\pi/4$. Fubini does not apply because
$$\int\int |f| \, dx \, dy = \infty.$$
(One can check by polar coordinates that $|f| \sim 1/r^2$ near origin, whose integral diverges.)

### Example 1.4.14 (Fubini with counting measure: the rearrangement warning)

Let $X = Y = \mathbb N$ with counting measure. Let $a_{ij} = +1$ if $j = i$, $-1$ if $j = i + 1$, $0$ otherwise. Then
$$\sum_i \sum_j a_{ij} = \sum_i 0 = 0 \qquad \text{but} \qquad \sum_j \sum_i a_{ij} = 1 \cdot 1 + 0 + 0 + \ldots = 1$$
(working through the matrix gives, in fact: $\sum_j$ first picks the column $j$: for $j = 1$, only $a_{1,1} = 1$ contributes (there's no "before $a_{1,1}$" as $a_{i, 2}$ with $i+1 = 2$ means $i=1$, giving $a_{1,2} = -1$, so $\sum_i a_{i, 2} = 1 + (-1) = 0$). Let me recompute:

$a_{ij}$: nonzero only at $(i, i)$ where it is $+1$ and $(i, i+1)$ where it is $-1$.
Row $i$: $a_{i,i} = 1, a_{i, i+1} = -1$, all others $0$, sum = $0$. So $\sum_i \sum_j = \sum_i 0 = 0$.
Column $j$: $a_{i, j}$ is nonzero when $i = j$ (value $+1$) or $i = j-1$ (value $-1$). For $j = 1$: only $i = 1$ ($+1$), so column sum $= 1$. For $j \ge 2$: $i = j$ ($+1$) and $i = j - 1$ ($-1$), sum $= 0$. So $\sum_j \sum_i = 1 + 0 + 0 + \ldots = 1$.

So $\sum_i \sum_j = 0 \ne 1 = \sum_j \sum_i$.

Again, Fubini fails because $\sum_{ij} |a_{ij}|$ is the counting-measure integral of $|a|$, and $|a_{i, i}| + |a_{i, i+1}| = 2$ for each $i$, so $\sum_{ij} |a_{ij}| = \infty$. Tonelli is vacuous (both iterated $|a|$-sums are $\infty$), and Fubini does not apply.

### Worked Example 1.4.15 (Fubini for convolutions)

For $f, g \in L^1(\mathbb R, \lambda)$, define the **convolution**
$$(f * g)(x) := \int_\mathbb R f(x - y) g(y) \, dy.$$

**Claim.** $f * g \in L^1(\mathbb R)$ with $\|f * g\|_1 \le \|f\|_1 \|g\|_1$.

**Proof.** Apply Tonelli to $|f(x - y) g(y)|$:
$$\int\int |f(x-y)||g(y)| \, dy \, dx = \int |g(y)| \int |f(x - y)| \, dx \, dy = \int |g(y)| \|f\|_1 \, dy = \|f\|_1 \|g\|_1 < \infty,$$
using translation invariance of Lebesgue measure to move the inner $x$-integral.

Hence $(x, y) \mapsto f(x - y) g(y)$ is in $L^1(\mathbb R^2, \lambda \times \lambda)$. Fubini says the iterated integrals agree and are finite: in particular $(f * g)(x) = \int f(x - y) g(y) dy$ is defined for a.e. $x$ and is in $L^1$, with
$$\int |f * g| \, dx \le \int\int |f(x-y) g(y)| \, dy \, dx = \|f\|_1 \|g\|_1. \qquad \blacksquare$$

This turns $L^1(\mathbb R)$ into a *Banach algebra* under convolution, the basis of Fourier analysis (Module 0.5's $\widehat{f*g} = \widehat f \cdot \widehat g$ formula).

## Topic 1.4.5 — Completeness and Iteration

### Completion and its subtleties

If $\mu, \nu$ are **complete** (every subset of a null set is measurable), the product $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$ may not be complete, and $\mu \times \nu$ must be **completed** to $\overline{\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G}$. For Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb R$, the product of Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebras is strictly smaller than Lebesgue on $\mathbb R^2$. The practical upshot:

- On $\mathbb R^n$ with the Borel $\sigma$-algebra, $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n) = \mathcal B(\mathbb R)^{\otimes n}$, and product Lebesgue measure on Borel sets agrees with $n$-dim Lebesgue measure on Borel sets. Great.
- The Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra on $\mathbb R^n$ is the completion of $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n)$ with respect to Lebesgue measure. Equivalently it is the completion of $\mathcal L(\mathbb R) \otimes \mathcal L(\mathbb R) \otimes \ldots$ (but not this product directly).
- **Fubini for complete measures:** Tonelli and Fubini hold if we use the Borel product and then complete, or equivalently if we measure $f$ with respect to the completed $\sigma$-algebra. The only "gotcha" is that a Lebesgue-measurable function on $\mathbb R^2$ may have some sections $y \mapsto f(x, y)$ that are non-measurable for certain $x$ in a null set — which is fine for the purposes of integration.

### Theorem 1.4.16 (Iterated Fubini for $n$-fold products)

Let $(X_i, \mathcal F_i, \mu_i)$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$ be $\sigma$-finite. Define $\mu_1 \times \ldots \times \mu_n$ iteratively. Then on the product $\mathcal F_1 \otimes \ldots \otimes \mathcal F_n$, for any non-negative measurable $f: X_1 \times \ldots \times X_n \to [0, \infty]$:
$$\int f \, d(\mu_1 \times \ldots \times \mu_n) = \int_{X_{\sigma(1)}} \int_{X_{\sigma(2)}} \cdots \int_{X_{\sigma(n)}} f \, d\mu_{\sigma(n)} \cdots d\mu_{\sigma(1)}$$
for any permutation $\sigma$. For integrable $f$, the same holds provided $\int |f| \, d(\mu_1 \times \ldots \times \mu_n) < \infty$.

**Proof.** Induct on $n$ using Tonelli/Fubini pairwise, grouping the last two variables together. $\blacksquare$

### Corollary 1.4.17 (Exchange of integral and series via Fubini)

For measurable $f_n: X \to \overline{\mathbb R}$ with $\sum_n \int |f_n| \, d\mu < \infty$ (equivalently $\int \sum_n |f_n| \, d\mu < \infty$ by Tonelli):
$$\int_X \sum_n f_n \, d\mu = \sum_n \int_X f_n \, d\mu.$$

**Proof.** Apply Fubini on $X \times \mathbb N$ (with $\mu$ and counting measure) to $g(x, n) := f_n(x)$. Condition: $\int |g| = \sum_n \int |f_n| < \infty$. $\blacksquare$

This is the *swap-sum-integral* lemma we've been using throughout, now rigorously justified.

## Topic 1.4.6 — Python: Computational Verification

```python
"""
Numerical verification of Tonelli/Fubini via numerical double integration
and comparison to single-variable transforms.
"""
import numpy as np
from scipy import integrate

# 1. Gaussian integral squared via Tonelli
def gaussian_squared():
    # int e^{-x^2 - y^2} dx dy = pi
    double, _ = integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: np.exp(-(x**2 + y**2)),
                                  -10, 10, lambda x: -10, lambda x: 10)
    single, _ = integrate.quad(lambda x: np.exp(-x**2), -10, 10)
    print(f"Double integral of e^(-x^2-y^2) ≈ {double:.6f}, should be pi = {np.pi:.6f}")
    print(f"Single integral^2 of e^(-x^2) ≈ {single**2:.6f}, should be pi = {np.pi:.6f}")

gaussian_squared()

# 2. Convolution of two Gaussians is a Gaussian (verify via Fubini-justified integral)
def gauss_conv_check(sig1, sig2, x_test):
    # (f*g)(x) where f, g are N(0, sig1^2), N(0, sig2^2)
    # should give N(0, sig1^2 + sig2^2)
    f = lambda u: np.exp(-u**2 / (2*sig1**2)) / (sig1 * np.sqrt(2*np.pi))
    g = lambda v: np.exp(-v**2 / (2*sig2**2)) / (sig2 * np.sqrt(2*np.pi))
    conv_val, _ = integrate.quad(lambda y: f(x_test - y) * g(y), -20, 20)
    expected = np.exp(-x_test**2 / (2*(sig1**2+sig2**2))) / (np.sqrt(sig1**2+sig2**2) * np.sqrt(2*np.pi))
    print(f"Conv at x={x_test}: numerical={conv_val:.6f}, theoretical={expected:.6f}")
gauss_conv_check(1.0, 1.5, 0.7)

# 3. Demonstrate failure of Fubini for non-integrable f
def fubini_failure():
    # f(x, y) = (x^2 - y^2) / (x^2 + y^2)^2 on [0, 1]^2
    def f(x, y):
        r2 = x**2 + y**2
        if r2 < 1e-20:
            return 0.0
        return (x**2 - y**2) / r2**2
    iter_xy, _ = integrate.dblquad(
        lambda y, x: f(x, y),
        1e-6, 1.0, lambda x: 1e-6, lambda x: 1.0  # avoid singularity at origin
    )
    # Flip order of integration by swapping x and y in the lambda
    iter_yx, _ = integrate.dblquad(
        lambda x, y: f(x, y),
        1e-6, 1.0, lambda y: 1e-6, lambda y: 1.0
    )
    print(f"int int f dy dx = {iter_xy:.6f}")
    print(f"int int f dx dy = {iter_yx:.6f}")
    # Should be close to -pi/4 and pi/4 (Example 1.4.13).
    # Numerical error near origin can be significant.
fubini_failure()

# 4. Verify exchange of sum and integral: sum_n int |f_n| < infty => sum-int swap works
def sum_integral_swap():
    # f_n(x) = (-1)^n / n * sin(n*x) / (1 + x^2), x in [0, pi]
    # sum f_n converges absolutely only because of the 1/(1+x^2) decay
    xs = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 10000)
    def f_n(n, x):
        return (-1)**n / n * np.sin(n * x) / (1 + x**2)
    total_int = 0
    for n in range(1, 100):
        integrand = f_n(n, xs)
        total_int += np.trapz(integrand, xs)
    # Alternatively compute sum first then integrate
    sum_vals = np.sum([f_n(n, xs) for n in range(1, 100)], axis=0)
    swap_int = np.trapz(sum_vals, xs)
    print(f"sum of integrals: {total_int:.6f}")
    print(f"integral of sum:  {swap_int:.6f}")
sum_integral_swap()
```

Expected output: (1) both give $\pi \approx 3.1416$, (2) numerical and theoretical convolution agree, (3) iterated integrals differ (showing Fubini fails), (4) sum of integrals $\approx$ integral of sum (Fubini applies).

## Topic 1.4.7 — [QUANT APPLICATION] Joint Distributions, Convolutions, and Fubini in Finance

### Application 1. Joint distributions via product measures

Let $X, Y$ be random variables on $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)$. The **joint distribution** is the pushforward $\mu_{X, Y} := (X, Y)_* \mathbb P$ on $\mathbb R^2$. If $X$ and $Y$ are **independent**, $\mu_{X, Y} = \mu_X \times \mu_Y$. Iterated expectation then becomes iterated integration:
$$\mathbb E g(X, Y) = \int g \, d(\mu_X \times \mu_Y) = \int_\mathbb R \int_\mathbb R g(x, y) \, d\mu_X(x) \, d\mu_Y(y).$$
Tonelli applies for $g \ge 0$, Fubini for $g(X, Y) \in L^1$.

### Application 2. Sum of independents has a convolution density

If $X, Y$ are independent with densities $f_X, f_Y$, the density of $X + Y$ is the convolution $f_X * f_Y$. The proof uses Fubini: for any Borel $B \subset \mathbb R$,
$$\mathbb P(X + Y \in B) = \mathbb E \mathbf 1_B(X + Y) = \iint \mathbf 1_B(x + y) f_X(x) f_Y(y) \, dx \, dy = \int_B (f_X * f_Y)(z) \, dz,$$
where we made the change of variable $(x, y) \to (x, z = x + y)$ with Jacobian $1$.

This is the **law of convolution** for sums of independents, a cornerstone of Fourier methods in probability (multiplication of characteristic functions).

### Application 3. Characteristic function of a sum

From Module 0.5, the characteristic function is $\varphi_X(t) = \mathbb E e^{itX}$. For independent $X, Y$:
$$\varphi_{X+Y}(t) = \mathbb E e^{it(X+Y)} = \iint e^{it(x+y)} \, d\mu_X \, d\mu_Y = \int e^{itx} d\mu_X \cdot \int e^{ity} d\mu_Y = \varphi_X(t) \varphi_Y(t),$$
using Fubini (the integrand has $|e^{it(x+y)}| = 1$ and product measure is finite when $\mu_X, \mu_Y$ are probability measures). This is the computational heart of the CLT proof.

### Application 4. Carr-Madan option pricing via Fubini

Recall from Module 0.5 the Carr-Madan formula for an out-of-the-money call:
$$C(k) := \mathbb E \left[(S_T - e^k)^+\right], \qquad \widehat C(v) = \int e^{ivk} C(k) \, dk.$$
Writing $C(k) = \int (s - e^k)^+ \, d\mu_{S_T}(s)$ and using Fubini,
$$\widehat C(v) = \iint e^{ivk} (s - e^k)^+ \, dk \, d\mu_{S_T}(s) = \int K(v, s) \, d\mu_{S_T}(s),$$
for an explicit kernel $K(v, s)$. The Fourier transform of the payoff is computed pointwise and *then* integrated against the risk-neutral distribution of $S_T$. Fubini is legal because we first verify $\int \int |\cdot| < \infty$ via a damping factor $e^{\alpha k}$ with $\alpha > 0$ large enough to control the $k \to -\infty$ tail of the payoff.

### Application 5. Copulas as 2D joint distributions

A **copula** is a joint distribution on $[0, 1]^2$ with uniform marginals. Sklar's theorem says any joint distribution $\mathbb P(X \le x, Y \le y) = F_{X, Y}(x, y)$ can be written as $C(F_X(x), F_Y(y))$ for a copula $C$. Fubini is implicit whenever we integrate a function of $(X, Y)$ against this joint distribution, decomposing into marginal and copula contributions.

### Application 6. Fubini for iterated conditional expectation

The tower property $\mathbb E[\mathbb E[X | \mathcal G]] = \mathbb E[X]$ is, in essence, Fubini applied to the joint law $\mu_{X, \mathcal G}$. Regular conditional distributions (Module 2.3) make this precise.

### Application 7. Stochastic integrals and Itô isometry (preview)

In Itô calculus (Module 3.2), the Itô isometry $\mathbb E (\int_0^T H_s \, dW_s)^2 = \mathbb E \int_0^T H_s^2 \, ds$ is proved for simple integrands and then extended. The proof uses Fubini on $\Omega \times [0, T]$ with product measure $\mathbb P \times ds$.

### Application 8. Moment generating function of multivariate normal

If $X \sim N(\mu, \Sigma)$ in $\mathbb R^n$ with density $(2\pi)^{-n/2} |\Sigma|^{-1/2} e^{-(x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)/2}$, then
$$M_X(t) = \mathbb E e^{t^\top X} = e^{t^\top \mu + t^\top \Sigma t / 2}.$$
The computation uses Tonelli to reduce the $n$-dim Gaussian integral to $n$ 1-dim Gaussian integrals (after diagonalizing $\Sigma$).

## Module Summary

- Product σ-algebras $\mathcal F \otimes \mathcal G$ are generated by rectangles; on separable metric spaces they agree with the Borel σ-algebra of the product topology.
- For $\sigma$-finite measures, product measure $\mu \times \nu$ exists and is unique.
- **Tonelli's theorem**: for $f \ge 0$ measurable, iterated integrals equal double integral (possibly $+\infty$).
- **Fubini's theorem**: for $f$ with $\int |f| \, d(\mu \times \nu) < \infty$, iterated integrals are finite, agree, and equal the double integral.
- **Protocol**: check $\int |f|$ with Tonelli; if finite, proceed with Fubini. Never swap without this check.
- Classical failures: $\frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}$ (gives $\pm\pi/4$), conditionally convergent double series, non-$\sigma$-finite product measures (counting × Lebesgue).
- Applications: convolution makes $L^1$ a Banach algebra; sum of independents has convolved density; multivariate Gaussian integrals reduce to 1-dim; Carr-Madan option pricing splits payoff and distribution.
