## Overview

A **measurable function** is the structure-preserving map between measurable spaces — the analogue of a continuous function between topological spaces. In probability, a measurable function is a **random variable**. This module develops:

1. The definition of measurability and basic stability properties (sums, products, composition).
2. **Simple functions** and the approximation theorem: every non-negative measurable function is an increasing pointwise limit of simple functions.
3. Modes of convergence: pointwise, a.e., uniform, in measure.
4. **Egorov's theorem** (a.e. ⇒ uniform off a small set).
5. **Lusin's theorem** (measurable ⇒ continuous on a large set).

These results are the bridge between the "structural" machinery of Module 1.1 and the "integration" machinery of Module 1.3.

## Topic 1.2.1 — Measurable Functions

### 1.2.1.1 Definition

**Definition 1.2.1.1 (Measurable function).** Let $(X, \mathcal F), (Y, \mathcal G)$ be measurable spaces. A function $f: X \to Y$ is **$(\mathcal F, \mathcal G)$-measurable** if $f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal F$ for every $B \in \mathcal G$.

When the context is clear (e.g., $Y = \mathbb R$ with the Borel σ-algebra), we just say "measurable."

**Proposition 1.2.1.2 (Generator criterion).** $f$ is measurable iff $f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal F$ for every $B$ in a generating collection $\mathcal E$ of $\mathcal G$ (i.e., $\sigma(\mathcal E) = \mathcal G$).

*Proof.* Let $\mathcal H := \{B \subseteq Y : f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal F\}$. $\mathcal H$ is a σ-algebra (preimages commute with set operations: $f^{-1}(B^c) = f^{-1}(B)^c$, $f^{-1}(\bigcup B_n) = \bigcup f^{-1}(B_n)$). If $\mathcal E \subseteq \mathcal H$, then $\sigma(\mathcal E) = \mathcal G \subseteq \mathcal H$, so $f$ is measurable. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 1.2.1.3.** A function $f: X \to \mathbb R$ is (Borel) measurable iff any one of the following holds for every $\alpha \in \mathbb R$:

(i) $\{x : f(x) > \alpha\} \in \mathcal F$.
(ii) $\{x : f(x) \geq \alpha\} \in \mathcal F$.
(iii) $\{x : f(x) < \alpha\} \in \mathcal F$.
(iv) $\{x : f(x) \leq \alpha\} \in \mathcal F$.

These equivalent checks are often easier than working with general Borel sets.

### 1.2.1.2 Examples

**Example 1: Constants.** $f(x) \equiv c$ is measurable — $f^{-1}(B) = X$ or $\emptyset$ depending on whether $c \in B$.

**Example 2: Indicator.** $\mathbf 1_A$ is measurable iff $A \in \mathcal F$.

**Example 3: Continuous functions.** Every continuous $f: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$ is Borel measurable. ($f^{-1}(\text{open}) = \text{open} \in \mathcal B$.)

**Example 4: Monotone functions.** Every monotone $f: \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is Borel measurable. ($\{f \leq \alpha\}$ is a half-line.)

**Example 5: Coordinate projections.** $\pi_i: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R$, $\pi_i(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = x_i$, is Borel measurable. In fact, measurability of $f: X \to \mathbb R^n$ is equivalent to each coordinate $\pi_i \circ f$ being measurable.

**Example 6: Step functions.** On $[0, 1]$, a function taking finitely many values on a partition into intervals is Borel measurable.

**Example 7: Dirichlet function.** $\mathbf 1_{\mathbb Q} : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is Borel measurable ($\mathbb Q$ is countable, hence Borel).

### 1.2.1.3 Complex- and Extended-Real-Valued Functions

**Complex-valued.** $f: X \to \mathbb C$ is measurable iff its real and imaginary parts are (equivalently, iff $f^{-1}(B)$ is measurable for every open $B \subseteq \mathbb C$).

**Extended real.** Often we consider $f: X \to [-\infty, \infty]$, where the Borel σ-algebra on $[-\infty, \infty]$ is generated by sets of the form $\{[-\infty, a]\}$ (equivalently, by the ordinary Borel sets plus $\{-\infty\}$ and $\{\infty\}$). Measurability is checked by $\{x : f(x) \leq a\} \in \mathcal F$ for all $a$, with $\{f = \pm\infty\}$ obtained as countable intersections/unions.

### 1.2.1.4 Pushforward σ-Algebra and Measure

**Definition 1.2.1.4 (Pushforward).** If $f: X \to Y$ and $\mathcal F$ is a σ-algebra on $X$, the **pushforward σ-algebra** on $Y$ is $\{B \subseteq Y : f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal F\}$. It's the largest σ-algebra on $Y$ for which $f$ is measurable.

If $\mu$ is a measure on $(X, \mathcal F)$, the **pushforward measure** $f_* \mu$ on $(Y, \mathcal G)$ (assuming $f$ is measurable) is
$$
(f_* \mu)(B) := \mu(f^{-1}(B)).
$$
In probability, this is the **distribution** or **law** of the random variable $f$: for $X \sim \mu$, the law of $f(X)$ is $f_* \mu$.

## Topic 1.2.2 — Operations on Measurable Functions

### 1.2.2.1 Arithmetic

**Theorem 1.2.2.1.** Let $f, g: X \to \mathbb R$ be measurable, $c \in \mathbb R$. Then $cf$, $f + g$, $fg$, $\max(f, g)$, $\min(f, g)$ are measurable; and $f/g$ is measurable on $\{g \neq 0\}$.

*Proof.*
**$cf$:** $\{cf \leq \alpha\}$ is $\{f \leq \alpha/c\}$ (or $\{f \geq \alpha/c\}$ if $c < 0$, or $\{f \in \mathbb R\}$ or $\emptyset$ if $c = 0$). Measurable.

**$f + g$:** For $\alpha \in \mathbb R$,
$$
\{f + g > \alpha\} = \bigcup_{r \in \mathbb Q} [\{f > r\} \cap \{g > \alpha - r\}].
$$
This is a countable union of measurable sets, hence measurable.

[The key point is $f(x) + g(x) > \alpha \iff \exists r \in \mathbb Q: f(x) > r, g(x) > \alpha - r$. Necessary: given $f + g > \alpha$, pick $r$ rational in $(\alpha - g(x), f(x))$, possible by density. Sufficient: if $f > r$ and $g > \alpha - r$, then $f + g > \alpha$.]

**$fg$:** Use $fg = \tfrac{1}{4}[(f + g)^2 - (f - g)^2]$, reducing to squares. For $f^2$: $\{f^2 > \alpha\} = \{f > \sqrt\alpha\} \cup \{f < -\sqrt\alpha\}$ for $\alpha \geq 0$, measurable; for $\alpha < 0$, $\{f^2 > \alpha\} = X$. So $f^2$ is measurable, hence $fg = \tfrac{1}{2}[(f+g)^2 - f^2 - g^2]$.

**$\max(f, g)$:** $\{\max(f, g) > \alpha\} = \{f > \alpha\} \cup \{g > \alpha\}$. Measurable.

**$\min(f, g) = -\max(-f, -g)$.**

**$f/g$ on $\{g \neq 0\}$:** Reduces to $1/g$ being measurable on $\{g > 0\} \cup \{g < 0\}$; $\{1/g > \alpha\}$ is a union of half-lines, measurable. $\blacksquare$

### 1.2.2.2 Composition

**Theorem 1.2.2.2.** If $f: X \to Y$ is $(\mathcal F, \mathcal G)$-measurable and $g: Y \to Z$ is $(\mathcal G, \mathcal H)$-measurable, then $g \circ f: X \to Z$ is $(\mathcal F, \mathcal H)$-measurable.

*Proof.* $(g \circ f)^{-1}(C) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(C))$. $g^{-1}(C) \in \mathcal G$ by measurability of $g$, then $f^{-1}(\cdot) \in \mathcal F$ by measurability of $f$. $\blacksquare$

**Caution.** If $g: \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is Lebesgue measurable (not Borel) and $f: \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is continuous, then $g \circ f$ may not be Lebesgue measurable. (A Lebesgue measurable but non-Borel $g$ can be pulled back by continuous $f$ to a non-measurable set.) The workaround: use **Borel** measurability on the codomain.

### 1.2.2.3 Limits

The star property of measurable functions: limits of measurable functions are measurable. This fails for continuous functions and is a major reason to prefer measurability.

**Theorem 1.2.2.3.** Let $f_n: X \to [-\infty, \infty]$ be a sequence of measurable functions. Then the following are measurable:

(a) $\sup_n f_n$, $\inf_n f_n$.

(b) $\limsup_n f_n$, $\liminf_n f_n$.

(c) On the set where the pointwise limit exists, $\lim_n f_n$.

*Proof.*
**(a):** $\{\sup_n f_n \leq \alpha\} = \bigcap_n \{f_n \leq \alpha\}$ — countable intersection of measurable sets. $\inf$ similar.

**(b):** $\limsup f_n = \inf_N \sup_{n \geq N} f_n$. Apply (a) twice. Similarly $\liminf$.

**(c):** $\{\lim_n f_n \text{ exists}\} = \{\liminf = \limsup\} = \bigcap_q \{\liminf f_n > q\} \triangle \{\limsup f_n > q\}$... actually cleaner: $\{\omega : \liminf f_n(\omega) = \limsup f_n(\omega)\}$ is $\{\liminf - \limsup \geq 0\} \cap \{\liminf - \limsup \leq 0\}$. The $\liminf$ and $\limsup$ are both measurable by (b), so their difference is measurable, and we can compare it to $0$. Hence the set where the limit exists is measurable, and $\lim f_n = \liminf f_n$ (or $\limsup f_n$) on this set, which is measurable by (b) restricted to this measurable subset. $\blacksquare$

### 1.2.2.4 Positive and Negative Parts

**Definition 1.2.2.4.** For $f: X \to [-\infty, \infty]$,
$$
f^+(x) := \max(f(x), 0), \quad f^-(x) := \max(-f(x), 0) = -\min(f(x), 0).
$$
Then $f = f^+ - f^-$ and $|f| = f^+ + f^-$.

If $f$ is measurable, so are $f^+, f^-, |f|$. (Follows from $\max$ being measurable.)

This decomposition will be crucial for defining the Lebesgue integral of general (possibly sign-changing) functions in terms of integrals of non-negative functions.

## Topic 1.2.3 — Simple Functions and Approximation

### 1.2.3.1 Simple Functions

**Definition 1.2.3.1 (Simple function).** A function $s: X \to \mathbb R$ is **simple** if it is measurable and takes only finitely many values. Equivalently, $s = \sum_{k=1}^n c_k \mathbf 1_{A_k}$ where $A_k \in \mathcal F$ and $c_k \in \mathbb R$.

The **standard representation** of a simple function has the $A_k$ disjoint and the $c_k$ distinct — made explicit by $A_k := s^{-1}(\{c_k\})$.

**Operations.** Linear combinations and products of simple functions are simple. The set of simple functions is closed under $\max, \min, |\cdot|$.

### 1.2.3.2 Approximation Theorem

The foundational theorem for Lebesgue integration:

**Theorem 1.2.3.2 (Simple function approximation).** Let $f: X \to [0, \infty]$ be measurable. There exist simple functions $s_n: X \to [0, \infty)$ with $0 \leq s_1 \leq s_2 \leq \cdots$ and $s_n(x) \to f(x)$ pointwise for every $x \in X$. Moreover, if $f$ is bounded, the convergence is uniform.

*Proof.* For each $n \geq 1$, partition $[0, n)$ into $n 2^n$ subintervals of length $2^{-n}$:
$$
I_{n,k} := [(k-1)2^{-n}, k \cdot 2^{-n}), \quad k = 1, 2, \ldots, n 2^n.
$$
Define
$$
s_n(x) := \sum_{k=1}^{n 2^n} (k-1) 2^{-n} \mathbf 1_{f^{-1}(I_{n,k})}(x) + n \mathbf 1_{f^{-1}([n, \infty])}(x).
$$

- $s_n$ is simple (finitely many values $\{0, 2^{-n}, 2 \cdot 2^{-n}, \ldots, n\}$, each on a measurable set $f^{-1}(I_{n,k})$).

- $s_n \leq f$: on $f^{-1}(I_{n,k})$, $s_n = (k-1) \cdot 2^{-n}$ and $f \geq (k-1) \cdot 2^{-n}$; on $f^{-1}([n, \infty])$, $s_n = n \leq f$.

- $s_n \leq s_{n+1}$: refine the partition. At level $n+1$, $I_{n, k}$ is split into $I_{n+1, 2k-1}$ and $I_{n+1, 2k}$. On the first, $s_{n+1}$ takes value $(2k - 2) 2^{-(n+1)} = (k-1) 2^{-n} = s_n$; on the second, $s_{n+1}$ takes value $(2k - 1) 2^{-(n+1)} = (k - 1/2) 2^{-n} \geq s_n$. Also for $n \leq f < n+1$: $s_n = n$ and $s_{n+1} \geq n$. And for $f \geq n+1$: $s_n = n < n+1 = s_{n+1}$.

- **Pointwise convergence:** Fix $x$. If $f(x) < \infty$, pick $n > f(x)$. Then $f(x) \in I_{n, k}$ for some $k$, so $s_n(x) = (k-1)2^{-n}$ and $f(x) - s_n(x) < 2^{-n} \to 0$. If $f(x) = \infty$, $s_n(x) = n \to \infty$.

- **Uniform convergence for bounded $f$:** if $\|f\|_\infty \leq M$, then for $n > M$, $f(x) < n$ everywhere, so $|f - s_n| \leq 2^{-n}$ uniformly. $\blacksquare$

**General $f$ (possibly sign-changing).** Apply the theorem to $f^+$ and $f^-$ separately, obtaining $s_n^+, s_n^-$; set $s_n := s_n^+ - s_n^-$. Then $s_n$ are simple and $s_n \to f$ pointwise (pointwise convergence might not be monotone).

### 1.2.3.3 Role of Simple Functions

Simple functions are the "finite-dimensional" objects in measure theory: every quantity we compute (integrals, probabilities, expectations) is first defined for simple functions by an explicit finite sum, then extended via the approximation theorem. Examples (Module 1.3):

- $\int s \, d\mu := \sum_k c_k \mu(A_k)$ for simple $s$.
- $\int f \, d\mu := \sup \{\int s \, d\mu : 0 \leq s \leq f, s \text{ simple}\}$ for non-negative $f$.
- $\int f \, d\mu := \int f^+ \, d\mu - \int f^- \, d\mu$ for general $f$.

Every convergence theorem (MCT, DCT) leans on the simple-function approximation.

## Topic 1.2.4 — Modes of Convergence

There are several distinct ways in which a sequence of measurable functions $f_n$ can converge to $f$:

**Definition 1.2.4.1.** For measurable $f_n, f: X \to \mathbb R$ on a measure space $(X, \mathcal F, \mu)$:

1. **Pointwise:** $f_n(x) \to f(x)$ for every $x$.

2. **Almost everywhere (a.e. or μ-a.e.):** $f_n(x) \to f(x)$ for $\mu$-almost every $x$, i.e., $\mu(\{x : f_n(x) \not\to f(x)\}) = 0$.

3. **Uniform:** $\sup_x |f_n(x) - f(x)| \to 0$.

4. **Almost uniform (a.u.):** For every $\epsilon > 0$ there is $E \in \mathcal F$ with $\mu(E) < \epsilon$ and $f_n \to f$ uniformly on $E^c$.

5. **In measure (μ-in-measure):** For every $\epsilon > 0$, $\mu(\{x : |f_n(x) - f(x)| > \epsilon\}) \to 0$.

6. **$L^p$ convergence:** $\int |f_n - f|^p \, d\mu \to 0$ (defined in Module 1.5).

### 1.2.4.1 Implications

The general implications on a finite measure space $\mu(X) < \infty$:

$$
\text{uniform} \Rightarrow \text{a.u.} \Rightarrow \text{a.e.} \Rightarrow \text{in measure}.
$$

Also:

$$
\text{a.u.} \Rightarrow \text{in measure}.
$$

In infinite measure spaces, "a.e. ⇒ in measure" can fail: $f_n = \mathbf 1_{[n, n+1]}$ on $\mathbb R$ converges pointwise (and a.e.) to $0$, but $\mu(\{|f_n| > 1/2\}) = 1 \not\to 0$.

### 1.2.4.2 Counterexamples

**Example 1: Pointwise ⇏ uniform.** $f_n(x) = x^n$ on $[0, 1]$; pointwise limit is $\mathbf 1_{\{1\}}$, but $\sup |f_n - f| = 1$ for all $n$.

**Example 2: Pointwise ⇏ a.e. uniform (i.e., a.u.) — fails on infinite measure.** $f_n = \mathbf 1_{[n, n+1]}$; pointwise $\to 0$ everywhere, but for any finite-measure set $E^c$, $f_n = 1$ on $[n, n+1] \cap E^c$ for large $n$, which is still "large."

**Example 3: In measure ⇏ a.e.** (The "typewriter" or "moving bump" example.) On $[0, 1]$ with Lebesgue measure, enumerate dyadic subintervals:
$$
f_1 = \mathbf 1_{[0, 1]}, \; f_2 = \mathbf 1_{[0, 1/2]}, \; f_3 = \mathbf 1_{[1/2, 1]}, \; f_4 = \mathbf 1_{[0, 1/4]}, \; f_5 = \mathbf 1_{[1/4, 1/2]}, \ldots
$$
Lengths of support decrease → $f_n \to 0$ in measure. But at every $x \in [0, 1]$, $f_n(x) = 1$ infinitely often (for $n$ such that the bump covers $x$), so $f_n$ does *not* converge at any $x$.

**Example 4: A.e. ⇒ in measure on finite measure spaces.** (Theorem, not counterexample.) Proof: $\{|f_n - f| > \epsilon\} \subseteq \{|f_n - f| > \epsilon \text{ infinitely often}\} \cup \text{[set of non-converging points]}$. The latter is null. For the first, $A_n := \{|f_n - f| > \epsilon\}$, $\limsup A_n \subseteq \{\text{non-converging}\}$, null. By continuity of measure on finite spaces, $\mu(\limsup A_n) = 0$, hence $\mu(A_n) \to 0$.

### 1.2.4.3 Subsequences Converging A.E.

**Theorem 1.2.4.2.** If $f_n \to f$ in measure, there is a subsequence $f_{n_k} \to f$ a.e.

*Proof.* Since $f_n \to f$ in measure, for each $k$, pick $n_k$ such that $\mu(\{|f_{n_k} - f| > 2^{-k}\}) < 2^{-k}$. Define $E_k := \{|f_{n_k} - f| > 2^{-k}\}$; $\sum \mu(E_k) < \infty$. By Borel-Cantelli (proven in Module 2.X, but follows from continuity of measure), $\mu(\limsup E_k) = 0$.

For $x \notin \limsup E_k$: $x \in E_k$ only finitely often, so for large $k$, $|f_{n_k}(x) - f(x)| \leq 2^{-k} \to 0$. Hence $f_{n_k}(x) \to f(x)$ except on the null set $\limsup E_k$. $\blacksquare$

This is an important theorem: "in measure" convergence + "pick a subsequence" = a.e. convergence. The converse fails.

## Topic 1.2.5 — Egorov's and Lusin's Theorems

These two theorems connect measurable functions with the "smooth" worlds of uniform convergence and continuous functions — on large sets, measurable functions are "almost" uniform / continuous.

### 1.2.5.1 Egorov's Theorem

**Theorem 1.2.5.1 (Egorov).** Let $(X, \mathcal F, \mu)$ be a finite measure space and $f_n \to f$ a.e. with $f_n, f$ measurable. Then for every $\epsilon > 0$ there is $E \in \mathcal F$ with $\mu(E) < \epsilon$ and $f_n \to f$ uniformly on $X \setminus E$.

In other words, a.e. convergence + finite measure ⇒ a.u. convergence (using Definition 1.2.4.1 item 4).

*Proof.* WLOG (discarding a null set) assume $f_n \to f$ everywhere. For integers $n, k \geq 1$, define
$$
A_{n, k} := \bigcup_{m \geq n} \{|f_m - f| \geq 1/k\}.
$$
Since $f_n \to f$ everywhere, for fixed $k$ and each $x$, $|f_n(x) - f(x)| < 1/k$ eventually, so $x \notin A_{n,k}$ for large $n$. Hence $A_{n, k} \downarrow \emptyset$ as $n \to \infty$ (for each $k$).

By continuity from above (using $\mu(X) < \infty$), $\mu(A_{n, k}) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. So for each $k$, pick $n_k$ with $\mu(A_{n_k, k}) < \epsilon/2^k$.

Set $E := \bigcup_k A_{n_k, k}$. By σ-subadditivity, $\mu(E) \leq \sum_k \epsilon/2^k = \epsilon$.

On $X \setminus E$: for each $k$, $x \notin A_{n_k, k}$, i.e., $|f_m(x) - f(x)| < 1/k$ for all $m \geq n_k$. So $\sup_{m \geq n_k} \sup_{x \notin E} |f_m(x) - f(x)| \leq 1/k$. Letting $k \to \infty$, uniform convergence on $X \setminus E$. $\blacksquare$

**Caveat.** Egorov's theorem fails for infinite measure spaces: $f_n = \mathbf 1_{[n, n+1]}$ on $\mathbb R$ converges a.e. to $0$, but for any $E$ with $\mu(E) < \infty$ (say $\mu(E) \leq 10$), $[n, n+1] \cap E^c \neq \emptyset$ for most $n$, so $f_n$ does not converge uniformly on $E^c$.

### 1.2.5.2 Lusin's Theorem

**Theorem 1.2.5.2 (Lusin).** Let $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb R$ be Lebesgue measurable (and finite a.e.). For every $\epsilon > 0$ there exists a closed set $K \subseteq [a, b]$ with $\mu([a, b] \setminus K) < \epsilon$ such that $f|_K$ is continuous.

**Slogan:** measurable functions are *continuous on most of their domain*.

*Proof sketch.*
**Step 1.** By simple function approximation, there are simple functions $s_n \to f$ a.e. Each simple function is piecewise constant on measurable sets.

**Step 2.** A simple function $s = \sum c_k \mathbf 1_{A_k}$ with disjoint measurable $A_k$. By inner regularity of Lebesgue measure (Module 1.1 Theorem 1.1.4.4), for each $A_k$ find a closed set $K_k \subseteq A_k$ with $\mu(A_k \setminus K_k) < \epsilon / n$. On $K := \bigcup K_k$, $s$ is constant on each $K_k$, hence continuous (since $K_k$'s are separated closed sets, a function constant on each is continuous on the union).

**Step 3.** Apply Egorov: on a set where $s_n \to f$ uniformly (off a small exception set), $f$ inherits continuity from uniform limits of continuous functions.

Combining: find simple $s_n \to f$ uniformly on $[a, b] \setminus E$ with $\mu(E) < \epsilon/2$ (Egorov). On each simple function's "continuous part" $K_n$ with $\mu([a, b] \setminus K_n) < \epsilon/2^{n+1}$: intersect all of these to get $K := (\bigcap K_n) \setminus E$, closed, with complement of measure $< \epsilon$, on which each $s_n$ is continuous and $s_n \to f$ uniformly, so $f$ is continuous on $K$. $\blacksquare$

**Extension.** Lusin's theorem extends to locally compact Hausdorff spaces with Radon measures — the general statement in modern functional analysis. Tietze's extension theorem can extend $f|_K$ to a continuous function on all of $\mathbb R$ (matching $f$ on $K$, possibly differing off $K$), which is the usual packaging of Lusin's theorem: every Lebesgue measurable function is a pointwise a.e. limit of continuous functions.

## Topic 1.2.6 — Random Variables and Distributions

### 1.2.6.1 Random Variables

**Definition 1.2.6.1 (Random variable).** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)$ be a probability space. A **random variable** is a measurable function $X: \Omega \to \mathbb R$. A **random vector** is a measurable function $X: \Omega \to \mathbb R^d$.

In probability, we almost never care about the underlying probability space $\Omega$ — we only care about the **distribution** of $X$.

### 1.2.6.2 Distribution

**Definition 1.2.6.2 (Distribution / law).** The **distribution** of a random variable $X$ is the pushforward measure $\mu_X := X_* \mathbb P$ on $(\mathbb R, \mathcal B(\mathbb R))$: $\mu_X(B) = \mathbb P(X \in B)$.

**Distribution function.** $F_X(x) := \mathbb P(X \leq x) = \mu_X((-\infty, x])$. Properties:

- Non-decreasing.
- Right-continuous.
- $\lim_{x \to -\infty} F_X(x) = 0$, $\lim_{x \to \infty} F_X(x) = 1$.

Conversely, any $F$ with these properties determines a unique probability measure on $(\mathbb R, \mathcal B)$ via $\mu((-\infty, x]) = F(x)$ and extension.

**Density.** If $\mu_X$ is absolutely continuous w.r.t. Lebesgue, its Radon-Nikodym derivative $p_X := d\mu_X/d\lambda$ is the **probability density**. Not all distributions have densities (e.g., Dirac $\delta$-measures, Cantor distributions).

### 1.2.6.3 Independence

**Definition 1.2.6.3.** Random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are **independent** if for all Borel $B_1, \ldots, B_n$,
$$
\mathbb P(X_1 \in B_1, \ldots, X_n \in B_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n \mathbb P(X_i \in B_i).
$$

Equivalently, the joint distribution $\mu_{(X_1, \ldots, X_n)}$ on $\mathbb R^n$ is the product measure $\mu_{X_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \mu_{X_n}$.

### 1.2.6.4 Expectation (Preview)

For a simple random variable $X = \sum c_k \mathbf 1_{A_k}$ (with disjoint $A_k$), $\mathbb E[X] := \sum c_k \mathbb P(A_k)$. For general $X \geq 0$, $\mathbb E[X] := \sup \mathbb E[S]$ over simple $0 \leq S \leq X$. For general $X$, $\mathbb E[X] := \mathbb E[X^+] - \mathbb E[X^-]$ when at least one of $\mathbb E[X^+], \mathbb E[X^-]$ is finite.

This is the Lebesgue integral $\int X \, d\mathbb P$, developed rigorously in Module 1.3.

## 1.2.7 Python: Measurable Functions in Practice

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Illustrate simple function approximation of f(x) = sin(x)^2 on [0, π].
def simple_approx(f, x_grid, n_levels):
    """Approximate f by a simple function taking values k/2^n (k=0,...,n*2^n)."""
    f_vals = f(x_grid)
    # Discretize f into levels [(k-1)/2^n, k/2^n)
    step = 1.0 / (2**n_levels)
    levels = np.floor(f_vals / step) * step
    return np.minimum(levels, n_levels)  # cap at n

x = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 1000)
f = lambda x: np.sin(x)**2

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(12, 8))
for ax, n in zip(axes.flat, [1, 2, 4, 6]):
    s_n = simple_approx(f, x, n)
    ax.plot(x, f(x), 'k-', linewidth=2, label='f(x) = sin²(x)')
    ax.plot(x, s_n, 'r-', linewidth=1, label=f's_{n} (n={n} levels)')
    ax.fill_between(x, s_n, f(x), alpha=0.3)
    ax.set_title(f'Approximation level n = {n}')
    ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()

# Simulation: modes of convergence
# Example 1: pointwise but not uniform
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
xs = np.linspace(0, 1, 200)
for n in [1, 5, 20, 100]:
    ax.plot(xs, xs**n, label=f'x^{n}')
ax.plot(xs, np.where(xs == 1, 1, 0), 'k--', label='pointwise limit')
ax.set_title('x^n → 𝟙_{x=1} pointwise but not uniformly')
ax.legend()

# Example 2: in-measure but not a.e.
# Typewriter sequence: 𝟙_{[k/2^n, (k+1)/2^n]} enumerated by (n, k)
N = 100
def typewriter(i, x):
    # Index i → (n, k) pair: n = log2(roughly), k = remainder
    n = int(np.floor(np.log2(i + 1)))
    k = i + 1 - 2**n
    return (x >= k / 2**n) & (x < (k + 1) / 2**n)

# Measure of {|f_i| > 0.5} = 1/2^n → 0, but every x is hit infinitely often.
print("Typewriter: μ(|f_i| > 0.5) for i=1..10:")
for i in range(1, 11):
    n = int(np.floor(np.log2(i)))
    print(f"  i={i}: bump width = 1/2^{n} = {1/2**n:.3f}")
print("Each x ∈ [0,1] is hit for some bump at every level n, so f_i(x) = 1 infinitely often.")
print("Hence no pointwise convergence, but convergence in measure.")

# Example 3: Egorov verification
# On [0,1]: f_n(x) = x^n → 0 a.e. (0 on [0,1), 1 at x=1).
# Off any small neighborhood of x=1, convergence is uniform.
for eps in [0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    # Remove the set [1-eps, 1] of measure eps.
    # On [0, 1-eps], |x^n| ≤ (1-eps)^n → 0 uniformly.
    n_for_unif_0_01 = int(np.ceil(np.log(0.01) / np.log(1 - eps)))
    print(f"Egorov: with exception set [1-{eps}, 1], need n ≥ {n_for_unif_0_01} for uniform |x^n| < 0.01 on [0, 1-{eps}].")

# Random variable: construction as a measurable function Ω → ℝ
# Example: X ~ Uniform[0,1] via Ω = [0,1], 𝒫 = Borel, P = Lebesgue, X(ω) = ω.
# The distribution function is F_X(x) = P({ω : X(ω) ≤ x}) = P([0, min(x, 1)]) = min(x, 1) for x ≥ 0.
# The density is p_X(x) = 𝟙_{[0,1]}(x).
# For Y = X^2, the distribution is:
#   F_Y(y) = P(X^2 ≤ y) = P(-√y ≤ X ≤ √y) = P(0 ≤ X ≤ √y) = √y for y ∈ [0,1].
#   p_Y(y) = 1/(2√y).
xs = np.linspace(0.01, 1, 100)
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(xs, np.ones_like(xs), label='p_X(x) = 𝟙_{[0,1]}(x)')
ax.plot(xs, 1/(2*np.sqrt(xs)), label='p_Y(y) = 1/(2√y)')
ax.set_title('Pushforward of Lebesgue on [0,1] under X and Y = X²')
ax.legend()
```

## 1.2.8 [QUANT APPLICATION]

**1. Random variables are measurable functions.** A stock price $S_T$ at time $T$, the log-return $\log S_T / S_0$, the Black-Scholes option price $C$ — all are measurable functions on the probability space. Measurability is what allows us to talk about their expectations, distributions, and conditional versions.

**2. Filtrations and measurability hierarchy.** A filtration $\{\mathcal F_t\}$ encodes information revealed over time. A random variable $X$ is **$\mathcal F_t$-measurable** iff its value at time $t$ is determined by the information $\mathcal F_t$. E.g., "the price at time $t$" is $\mathcal F_t$-measurable, but "the maximum price on $[0, T]$" for $T > t$ is **not** $\mathcal F_t$-measurable — a clean mathematical formulation of "no cheating by looking at future data."

**3. Adapted and predictable processes.** An adapted process is one where $X_t$ is $\mathcal F_t$-measurable for each $t$. Predictable processes (for jump processes) are even more restrictive: $X_t$ is $\mathcal F_{t-}$-measurable. These are the correct processes against which stochastic integrals are defined (Module 6.X).

**4. Risk-neutral pricing and pullback.** The risk-neutral expectation $\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[e^{-rT} f(S_T)]$ prices derivatives. The "pullback" interpretation: $f(S_T)$ is the composition of the measurable function $f$ with the random variable $S_T$, hence measurable, hence its expectation exists.

**5. Distribution of a strategy's P&L.** A trading strategy's profit-and-loss is a random variable on the path space. Its distribution (law) determines VaR, expected shortfall, Sharpe ratio, etc. Because of Lusin/Egorov, we can approximate any strategy by "nearly continuous" or "nearly simple" strategies — useful for pathwise Monte Carlo estimates.

**6. Convergence in distribution of estimators.** The empirical CDF $F_n(x) = n^{-1} \sum \mathbf 1_{X_i \leq x}$ converges to the true CDF $F$ by the Glivenko-Cantelli theorem (an a.s. uniform convergence). This is measurability + modes of convergence applied to statistical estimation.

**7. Value-at-Risk is a measurable function.** $\operatorname{VaR}_\alpha(L) := \inf\{x : F_L(x) \geq \alpha\}$, the quantile function of the loss distribution. This is measurable as a function of the underlying distribution (a fact that makes statistical estimation well-defined).

**8. Discretization and tree models.** Binomial and trinomial trees approximate continuous models by simple functions on finite σ-algebras. The convergence from discrete to continuous (via a Donsker-type functional CLT) is measurability-preserving, ensuring that tree approximations give valid prices in the limit.

## Module 1.2 Summary

### Key Takeaways

1. **Measurability is the right notion for structure-preserving maps** in measure theory, analogous to continuity for topology.
2. **Limits of measurable functions are measurable** — a key stability property that fails for continuous functions.
3. **Every non-negative measurable function is a pointwise limit of simple functions.** This is the foundation for defining integrals.
4. **Different modes of convergence have different strengths** — understanding their implications is essential for the convergence theorems in Module 1.3.
5. **Egorov (a.e. ⇒ a.u. on finite measure) and Lusin (measurable ⇒ continuous on a big set)** connect measurable with smoother functions, giving measurability an approximation-theoretic flavor.
6. **A random variable is a measurable function on a probability space**; its distribution is the pushforward measure. Probability theory is measure theory with total mass one.
