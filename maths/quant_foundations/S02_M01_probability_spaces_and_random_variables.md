> *"Probability is measure theory that has been told a story."* — a common remark about Kolmogorov's 1933 foundation.

## Prerequisites

- **Subject 1 (all modules)** — measures, integration, $L^p$, Radon-Nikodym. We now specialize to the case $\mu(X) = 1$ and rename $(X, \mathcal F, \mu) \rightsquigarrow (\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)$.
- **Module 0.5** — characteristic functions as Fourier transforms.

## Overview

With Subject 1 in hand, probability theory becomes a very thin (and very beautiful) layer on top of measure theory. A probability space is a measure space with total measure $1$; a random variable is a measurable function; expectation is the Lebesgue integral. The conceptual shift from "measure theory" to "probability" is substantial, however:

- **Language**: measurable set $\to$ event; measurable function $\to$ random variable; integral $\to$ expectation; null set $\to$ almost sure event; $\sigma$-algebra $\to$ information.
- **Focus**: in measure theory we study *the measure* $\mu$; in probability we study *many* random variables jointly on a fixed $\mathbb P$.
- **New structures**: independence (a specifically probabilistic concept), distributions on $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb R^n$, conditional probability, and the interplay of many random variables.

This module lays out Kolmogorov's axioms, defines random variables and their distributions, develops independence, proves the two Borel-Cantelli lemmas, and Kolmogorov's $0$-$1$ law. Every result in this module has a clean quant interpretation — financial asset returns, trading signals, event risks, independence assumptions.

## Topic 2.1.1 — Probability Spaces

### Definition 2.1.1 (Kolmogorov's axioms)

A **probability space** is a triple $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)$ where:

1. $\Omega$ is a non-empty set, the **sample space**. Elements $\omega \in \Omega$ are **outcomes**.
2. $\mathcal F$ is a $\sigma$-algebra on $\Omega$. Elements $A \in \mathcal F$ are **events**.
3. $\mathbb P: \mathcal F \to [0, 1]$ is a **probability measure**: non-negative, countably additive, and $\mathbb P(\Omega) = 1$.

The requirement $\mathbb P(\Omega) = 1$ is the only change from a general measure space.

### Elementary properties (proved in Module 1.1, restated):

- $\mathbb P(\emptyset) = 0$
- **Complement**: $\mathbb P(A^c) = 1 - \mathbb P(A)$
- **Finite additivity**: $A \cap B = \emptyset \Rightarrow \mathbb P(A \cup B) = \mathbb P(A) + \mathbb P(B)$
- **Monotonicity**: $A \subset B \Rightarrow \mathbb P(A) \le \mathbb P(B)$
- **Inclusion-exclusion**: $\mathbb P(A \cup B) = \mathbb P(A) + \mathbb P(B) - \mathbb P(A \cap B)$; $n$-fold version
$$\mathbb P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right) = \sum_{k=1}^n (-1)^{k+1} \sum_{i_1 < \ldots < i_k} \mathbb P(A_{i_1} \cap \ldots \cap A_{i_k}).$$
- **Countable subadditivity**: $\mathbb P\!\left(\bigcup A_n\right) \le \sum \mathbb P(A_n)$
- **Continuity**: $A_n \uparrow A \Rightarrow \mathbb P(A_n) \uparrow \mathbb P(A)$; $A_n \downarrow A \Rightarrow \mathbb P(A_n) \downarrow \mathbb P(A)$

### Example 2.1.2 (Fair coin)

$\Omega = \{H, T\}$, $\mathcal F = \{\emptyset, \{H\}, \{T\}, \Omega\}$, $\mathbb P(\{H\}) = \mathbb P(\{T\}) = 1/2$. Trivial but the prototype of every probability model.

### Example 2.1.3 (Countable coin tosses)

$\Omega = \{H, T\}^\mathbb N$, the set of all infinite sequences of heads/tails. $\mathcal F = $ the $\sigma$-algebra generated by cylinder sets $\{\omega : \omega_i = a_i \text{ for } i = 1, \ldots, n\}$. The fair coin probability is defined on cylinders by $\mathbb P(\text{cylinder}) = 2^{-n}$ and extended (by Carathéodory, Module 1.1) to $\mathcal F$.

This space is the natural home of Bernoulli processes and is rich enough for SLLN, martingales, random walks.

### Example 2.1.4 (Uniform on $[0, 1]$)

$\Omega = [0, 1]$, $\mathcal F$ = Borel (or Lebesgue), $\mathbb P$ = Lebesgue measure restricted to $[0, 1]$. This is the "most flexible" probability space: every other probability distribution on $\mathbb R$ can be constructed from a uniform variable via the quantile transform.

### Example 2.1.5 (Infinite product: canonical Wiener space)

For a stochastic process indexed by $t \in [0, T]$, one takes $\Omega = C([0, T], \mathbb R)$ (continuous paths) and constructs Wiener measure (Module 3.1) to make the coordinate process $W_t(\omega) = \omega(t)$ into a Brownian motion.

## Topic 2.1.2 — Random Variables and Distributions

### Definition 2.1.6 (Random variable)

A **random variable** on $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)$ is a measurable function $X: (\Omega, \mathcal F) \to (\mathbb R, \mathcal B(\mathbb R))$. More generally, an **$S$-valued random variable** is a measurable $X: (\Omega, \mathcal F) \to (S, \mathcal S)$.

A **random vector** is a random variable with values in $\mathbb R^n$. A **random element** of a general Polish space $S$ uses its Borel $\sigma$-algebra.

### Definition 2.1.7 (Distribution / law)

The **distribution** (or **law**) of a random variable $X$ is the pushforward measure
$$\mu_X := X_* \mathbb P, \qquad \mu_X(B) = \mathbb P(X \in B) = \mathbb P(X^{-1}(B)).$$

Two random variables (possibly on different spaces) are **equal in distribution**, written $X \stackrel{d}{=} Y$, if $\mu_X = \mu_Y$.

### Definition 2.1.8 (Distribution function / CDF)

The **cumulative distribution function** (CDF) of a real-valued random variable $X$ is
$$F_X(x) := \mathbb P(X \le x) = \mu_X((-\infty, x]), \quad x \in \mathbb R.$$

### Proposition 2.1.9 (Characterization of CDFs)

A function $F: \mathbb R \to [0, 1]$ is the CDF of some random variable iff:
1. $F$ is non-decreasing.
2. $F$ is right-continuous: $F(x+) = F(x)$ for all $x$.
3. $F(-\infty) := \lim_{x \to -\infty} F(x) = 0$ and $F(+\infty) := \lim_{x \to +\infty} F(x) = 1$.

**Proof.** The "only if" direction follows from continuity of measure: $A_n \downarrow A$ implies $\mathbb P(A_n) \downarrow \mathbb P(A)$, applied to $\{X \le x + 1/n\} \downarrow \{X \le x\}$ gives right-continuity.

For "if": given such $F$, define $\mu((a, b]) := F(b) - F(a)$ on half-open intervals. This extends uniquely to a probability measure on $(\mathbb R, \mathcal B)$ by Carathéodory (Module 1.1). This is the **Lebesgue-Stieltjes measure** associated with $F$. Take $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P) = (\mathbb R, \mathcal B, \mu)$ and $X(\omega) = \omega$; then $F_X = F$. $\blacksquare$

### Theorem 2.1.10 (Quantile / inverse CDF transform)

Let $F$ be a CDF. Define the **generalized inverse** (quantile function)
$$F^{-1}(u) := \inf\{x \in \mathbb R : F(x) \ge u\}, \quad u \in (0, 1).$$
If $U$ is uniform on $(0, 1)$, then $X := F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$.

**Proof.** We show $\mathbb P(F^{-1}(U) \le x) = F(x)$.

$F^{-1}(u) \le x \iff u \le F(x)$:
- ($\Leftarrow$) If $u \le F(x)$, then $x \in \{y : F(y) \ge u\}$, so $F^{-1}(u) \le x$.
- ($\Rightarrow$) If $F^{-1}(u) \le x$, then for every $\varepsilon > 0$, $F^{-1}(u) + \varepsilon \in \{y : F(y) \ge u\}$ (by the inf), so $F(x + \varepsilon) \ge u$. By right-continuity of $F$, $F(x) \ge u$.

So $\mathbb P(F^{-1}(U) \le x) = \mathbb P(U \le F(x)) = F(x)$. $\blacksquare$

This is the basis of inverse-transform sampling: to simulate from any distribution, sample $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ and apply $F^{-1}$. It also guarantees that every distribution can be realized on $([0, 1], \mathcal B, \lambda)$.

### Definition 2.1.11 (Discrete, absolutely continuous, singular distributions)

By Lebesgue decomposition (Module 1.6), every distribution on $\mathbb R$ decomposes as
$$\mu_X = \mu_\mathrm{disc} + \mu_\mathrm{ac} + \mu_\mathrm{sing}$$
where:
- $\mu_\mathrm{disc} = \sum_k p_k \delta_{x_k}$ is **discrete** (supported on a countable set).
- $\mu_\mathrm{ac}$ is **absolutely continuous** w.r.t. Lebesgue: $\mu_\mathrm{ac}(B) = \int_B f(x) \, dx$ for some density $f \ge 0$.
- $\mu_\mathrm{sing}$ is **singularly continuous** (continuous CDF, supported on a Lebesgue null set, e.g. Cantor distribution).

Most distributions in practice are pure-type: **discrete** (Bernoulli, Binomial, Poisson, geometric) or **absolutely continuous** (Normal, Exponential, Gamma, Beta, Student-$t$, Uniform). Singular parts appear in special contexts (fractal random sets).

### Example 2.1.12 (Bernoulli, Binomial, Poisson)

- **Bernoulli**$(p)$: $\mathbb P(X = 1) = p, \mathbb P(X = 0) = 1 - p$.
- **Binomial**$(n, p)$: $X = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ with i.i.d. Bernoulli$(p)$. $\mathbb P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$.
- **Poisson**$(\lambda)$: $\mathbb P(X = k) = e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k / k!$.

**Poisson limit theorem.** If $X_n \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \lambda/n)$ then $X_n \xrightarrow{d} \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$. *Proof.* CF: $\mathbb E e^{it X_n} = (1 + (\lambda/n)(e^{it} - 1))^n \to \exp(\lambda(e^{it} - 1))$, the Poisson CF (Module 0.5). Lévy continuity gives convergence in distribution.

### Example 2.1.13 (Normal, Exponential, Gamma, Cauchy)

- **Standard Normal** $N(0, 1)$: density $\phi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}$, CDF $\Phi$.
- **Exponential**$(\lambda)$: density $\lambda e^{-\lambda x} \mathbf 1_{x \ge 0}$; memoryless.
- **Gamma**$(\alpha, \beta)$: density $\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta x} \mathbf 1_{x > 0}$; sum of Exp is Gamma (Exercise 1.4.E6).
- **Cauchy**$(0, 1)$: density $\frac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}$; heavy tails, no first moment.

### Proposition 2.1.14 (Law of the unconscious statistician — LOTUS)

If $X$ is a random variable and $g: \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ measurable with $\mathbb E|g(X)| < \infty$:
$$\mathbb E g(X) = \int_\Omega g(X(\omega)) \, d\mathbb P(\omega) = \int_\mathbb R g(x) \, d\mu_X(x).$$
If $X$ has density $f_X$: $\mathbb E g(X) = \int g(x) f_X(x) \, dx$.

**Proof.** Theorem 1.3.31 (change of variables). $\blacksquare$

## Topic 2.1.3 — Joint Distributions and Independence

### Definition 2.1.15 (Joint distribution)

For random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ on the same $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)$, the **joint distribution** is the pushforward
$$\mu_{X_1, \ldots, X_n} := (X_1, \ldots, X_n)_* \mathbb P,$$
a probability measure on $(\mathbb R^n, \mathcal B(\mathbb R^n))$. The **joint CDF** is $F(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \mathbb P(X_1 \le x_1, \ldots, X_n \le x_n)$.

### Definition 2.1.16 (Independence of random variables)

Random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are **independent** if their joint distribution is the product of marginals:
$$\mu_{X_1, \ldots, X_n} = \mu_{X_1} \times \ldots \times \mu_{X_n}.$$
Equivalently (by Dynkin's $\pi$-$\lambda$ theorem, Module 1.1), for all Borel $B_1, \ldots, B_n$:
$$\mathbb P(X_1 \in B_1, \ldots, X_n \in B_n) = \prod \mathbb P(X_i \in B_i).$$
Or (via CDFs): $F(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = F_1(x_1) \cdots F_n(x_n)$ for all $(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in \mathbb R^n$.

An **infinite collection** $\{X_i\}_{i \in I}$ is **independent** if every finite subcollection is.

### Definition 2.1.17 (Independence of $\sigma$-algebras and events)

$\sigma$-algebras $\mathcal G_1, \ldots, \mathcal G_n \subset \mathcal F$ are **independent** if for every choice $A_i \in \mathcal G_i$,
$$\mathbb P(A_1 \cap \ldots \cap A_n) = \prod \mathbb P(A_i).$$

Events $A_1, \ldots, A_n$ are **independent** if the $\sigma$-algebras $\{\emptyset, A_i, A_i^c, \Omega\}$ are independent, i.e. for every subset $J \subset \{1, \ldots, n\}$, $\mathbb P(\bigcap_{i \in J} A_i) = \prod_{i \in J} \mathbb P(A_i)$.

**Warning.** Pairwise independence does not imply joint independence. Classic example: $\Omega = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$ uniform, $A = \{HH, HT\}, B = \{HH, TH\}, C = \{HH, TT\}$; each pair is independent but the triple is not ($\mathbb P(A \cap B \cap C) = 1/4 \ne 1/8$).

### Proposition 2.1.18 (Independence and generators)

If $\mathcal C_1, \ldots, \mathcal C_n$ are $\pi$-systems (closed under finite intersections) and $\mathbb P(A_1 \cap \ldots \cap A_n) = \prod \mathbb P(A_i)$ for all $A_i \in \mathcal C_i$, then $\sigma(\mathcal C_1), \ldots, \sigma(\mathcal C_n)$ are independent.

**Proof.** Apply Dynkin's $\pi$-$\lambda$ theorem: fix all but one factor, note that the collection of sets making the product hold is a $\lambda$-system, hence contains the generated $\sigma$-algebra; iterate. Full proof in Billingsley Chapter 4, Durrett §2.1. $\blacksquare$

### Corollary 2.1.19 (Checking independence via events of the form $\{X \le x\}$)

For real random variables $X_1, \ldots, X_n$, independence is equivalent to the factorization of the joint CDF $F = F_1 \cdots F_n$.

**Proof.** $\{(-\infty, x] : x \in \mathbb R\}$ is a $\pi$-system generating $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$. Apply Proposition 2.1.18. $\blacksquare$

### Proposition 2.1.20 (Independence and measurable transformations)

If $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are independent and $g_i$ are measurable, then $g_1(X_1), \ldots, g_n(X_n)$ are independent.

**Proof.** For any Borel $B_i$: $\mathbb P(g_1(X_1) \in B_1, \ldots, g_n(X_n) \in B_n) = \mathbb P(X_1 \in g_1^{-1}(B_1), \ldots, X_n \in g_n^{-1}(B_n)) = \prod \mathbb P(X_i \in g_i^{-1}(B_i)) = \prod \mathbb P(g_i(X_i) \in B_i)$. $\blacksquare$

### Proposition 2.1.21 (Grouping independent random variables)

If $\{X_i\}_{i \in I}$ are independent and $I = \bigsqcup_j I_j$ is a partition, then the $\sigma$-algebras $\sigma(X_i : i \in I_j)$ for different $j$ are independent. In particular, measurable functions of disjoint groups are independent.

### Theorem 2.1.22 (Product measure construction of independent sequences)

Given probability distributions $\mu_1, \mu_2, \ldots$ on $\mathbb R$, there exists a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)$ and independent random variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ with $\mu_{X_i} = \mu_i$.

**Proof.** Take $\Omega = \mathbb R^\mathbb N$, $\mathcal F = \mathcal B^{\otimes \mathbb N}$, $\mathbb P = \prod_i \mu_i$. The product measure exists by **Kolmogorov's extension theorem** (proved below), or by an iterated application of Theorem 1.4.6 combined with an extension to infinite products.

Define $X_i: \Omega \to \mathbb R$ by $X_i(\omega) = \omega_i$ (the $i$-th coordinate). Measurability and independence are clear from the product structure. $\blacksquare$

### Theorem 2.1.23 (Kolmogorov's extension theorem)

Let $(S_t, \mathcal S_t)$ be Polish measurable spaces indexed by $t \in T$. Suppose we have a **consistent** family of finite-dimensional distributions: for each finite $F \subset T$, a probability measure $\mu_F$ on $(\prod_{t \in F} S_t, \otimes_{t \in F} \mathcal S_t)$, such that for $F \subset G$ finite, the marginal of $\mu_G$ on $\prod_{t \in F} S_t$ is $\mu_F$. Then there exists a unique probability measure $\mu$ on $\prod_{t \in T} S_t$ (with the product $\sigma$-algebra) such that all $\mu_F$ are its finite-dimensional marginals.

This is the basis of stochastic process construction: specify all finite-dim distributions consistently, get the process. Brownian motion is built this way (Module 3.1).

## Topic 2.1.4 — Borel-Cantelli Lemmas and 0-1 Laws

### Notation

For a sequence of events $\{A_n\}$, define
$$\limsup_n A_n := \bigcap_{m=1}^\infty \bigcup_{n \ge m} A_n = \{\omega : \omega \in A_n \text{ for infinitely many } n\}, \qquad \{A_n \text{ i.o.}\}$$
$$\liminf_n A_n := \bigcup_{m=1}^\infty \bigcap_{n \ge m} A_n = \{\omega : \omega \in A_n \text{ for all but finitely many } n\}, \qquad \{A_n \text{ eventually}\}$$

### Lemma 2.1.24 (Borel-Cantelli I)

If $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathbb P(A_n) < \infty$, then $\mathbb P(\limsup_n A_n) = 0$.

**Proof.** $\limsup A_n = \bigcap_m \bigcup_{n \ge m} A_n$. By monotonicity and countable subadditivity,
$$\mathbb P(\limsup A_n) \le \mathbb P\!\left(\bigcup_{n \ge m} A_n\right) \le \sum_{n \ge m} \mathbb P(A_n) \to 0 \text{ as } m \to \infty,$$
since the tail of a convergent series goes to zero. $\blacksquare$

### Lemma 2.1.25 (Borel-Cantelli II)

If $\{A_n\}$ are **independent** and $\sum_n \mathbb P(A_n) = \infty$, then $\mathbb P(\limsup A_n) = 1$.

**Proof.** We show $\mathbb P((\limsup A_n)^c) = 0$. Now $(\limsup A_n)^c = \bigcup_m \bigcap_{n \ge m} A_n^c$; we show each factor has probability zero.

By independence and $1 - x \le e^{-x}$ (for $x \in [0, 1]$):
$$\mathbb P\!\left(\bigcap_{n = m}^N A_n^c\right) = \prod_{n = m}^N (1 - \mathbb P(A_n)) \le \prod_{n = m}^N e^{-\mathbb P(A_n)} = \exp\!\left(-\sum_{n = m}^N \mathbb P(A_n)\right).$$
The sum $\sum_m^N \mathbb P(A_n) \to \infty$ as $N \to \infty$ (diverging series), so the RHS $\to 0$. By continuity from above, $\mathbb P(\bigcap_{n \ge m} A_n^c) = 0$. Taking a countable union over $m$, $\mathbb P((\limsup A_n)^c) = 0$. $\blacksquare$

### Example 2.1.26 (Records in an i.i.d. sequence)

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be i.i.d. continuous. Let $A_n = \{X_n > \max(X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1})\}$. By symmetry (each of the $n$ variables equally likely to be the max), $\mathbb P(A_n) = 1/n$. Since $\sum 1/n = \infty$, and **$A_n$'s are not independent** (they're "decoupled" only in a subtle sense), can we apply BC II?

In fact, the events $A_n$ are **independent** for $n$ varying: $\{A_n\}$ are the events that the max occurs at each position, and by the symmetry of the $n$-tuple's ordering, the event "position $n$ is the max" among $(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ doesn't influence "position $n+1$ is the max" among $(X_1, \ldots, X_{n+1})$. This is a subtle but true fact (see Durrett §2.3). Applying BC II: infinitely many records occur a.s., hence $\max(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \to \infty$ a.s.

### Theorem 2.1.27 (Kolmogorov's 0-1 law)

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be independent random variables. The **tail $\sigma$-algebra**
$$\mathcal T := \bigcap_{n=1}^\infty \sigma(X_n, X_{n+1}, \ldots)$$
is $\mathbb P$-trivial: $A \in \mathcal T \Rightarrow \mathbb P(A) \in \{0, 1\}$.

**Proof.** Fix $A \in \mathcal T$. We show $A$ is independent of itself, hence $\mathbb P(A) = \mathbb P(A \cap A) = \mathbb P(A)^2$, giving $\mathbb P(A) \in \{0, 1\}$.

For any $n$, $A \in \sigma(X_{n+1}, X_{n+2}, \ldots)$ (by definition of tail). So $A$ is independent of $\sigma(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ (independence of disjoint groupings). Let $\mathcal G = \sigma(X_1, X_2, \ldots) = \sigma(\bigcup_n \sigma(X_1, \ldots, X_n))$. Since $A$ is independent of each $\sigma(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$, which form a $\pi$-system generating $\mathcal G$, $A$ is independent of $\mathcal G$ (Proposition 2.1.18).

But $\mathcal T \subset \mathcal G$, so $A \in \mathcal G$ too. $A$ independent of $\mathcal G$ and $A \in \mathcal G$ implies $A$ independent of itself: $\mathbb P(A) = \mathbb P(A \cap A) = \mathbb P(A)^2$. Solve: $\mathbb P(A) \in \{0, 1\}$. $\blacksquare$

### Examples of tail events

- $\{\sum X_n \text{ converges}\}$
- $\{\lim_n X_n \text{ exists}\}$
- $\{\limsup_n X_n > c\}$, $\{\lim S_n/n = c\}$ where $S_n = X_1 + \ldots + X_n$

By Kolmogorov's 0-1 law, each of these has probability $0$ or $1$. The conclusion "either it happens a.s., or it doesn't happen a.s." is extraordinarily useful: if we can *prove* that such an event has positive probability, it actually has probability $1$.

### Theorem 2.1.28 (Hewitt-Savage 0-1 law)

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be i.i.d. The **exchangeable $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal E = \{A : \sigma A = A \text{ for every finite permutation } \sigma\}$ is $\mathbb P$-trivial.

(Proof: apply an approximation argument with cylinder sets. See Durrett §2.5.)

Useful for events like $\{\limsup_n S_n/\sqrt n > c\}$ which may not be tail events but are exchangeable.

## Topic 2.1.5 — Worked Examples in Probability

### Example 2.1.29 (Simple random walk returns to origin infinitely often iff in dimension $\le 2$)

Let $X_i$ be i.i.d. uniform on $\{\pm 1\}$, $S_n = \sum X_i$. Then the event $\{S_n = 0 \text{ i.o.}\}$ has probability $0$ or $1$ (it's a tail event). A direct computation gives $\mathbb P(S_{2n} = 0) = \binom{2n}{n} / 2^{2n} \sim 1/\sqrt{\pi n}$. So $\sum_n \mathbb P(S_n = 0) = \infty$. But $\{S_n = 0\}$ are **not independent**, so BC II doesn't apply directly.

Instead, use the **random walk Green's function** or a direct combinatorial argument (Pólya's theorem, 1921) to show that the walk is recurrent in $d \le 2$ and transient in $d \ge 3$. The 0-1 law guarantees the dichotomy; the computation picks out which side.

### Example 2.1.30 (Lévy's construction of Brownian motion)

Construct Brownian motion $W: [0, 1] \to \mathbb R$ as follows. Let $\{\xi_{n, k}\}$ be i.i.d. $N(0, 1)$. Define Schauder-Faber basis functions $\Delta_{n, k}(t)$ (tent functions at dyadic levels). Define
$$W_t := \sum_{n=0}^\infty \sum_{k=1}^{2^n} \xi_{n, k} \Delta_{n, k}(t).$$
Then $W$ is a Brownian motion. Independence of the $\xi_{n, k}$ is used to prove convergence of the series uniformly in $t$ via Borel-Cantelli (on the exceptional null set). Full construction: Module 3.1.

### Example 2.1.31 (Quantile function of exponential)

$X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ has CDF $F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \ge 0$. Solving $F(x) = u$ gives $x = -\log(1 - u)/\lambda$, so $F^{-1}(u) = -\log(1 - u)/\lambda$. Simulating exponentials: take $U \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$, return $-\log U / \lambda$ (using that $1 - U$ is also uniform).

### Example 2.1.32 (Box-Muller for normals)

$U_1, U_2 \sim \text{Unif}(0, 1)$ independent $\Rightarrow$ $Z_1 = \sqrt{-2 \log U_1} \cos(2\pi U_2)$ and $Z_2 = \sqrt{-2 \log U_1} \sin(2\pi U_2)$ are i.i.d. $N(0, 1)$. Proof: polar decomposition of $(Z_1, Z_2)$; the radius has density $r e^{-r^2/2}$ (Rayleigh), the angle is uniform, both independent.

## Topic 2.1.6 — Python: Simulating Distributions

```python
"""
Inverse-transform and Box-Muller, quantile functions, and verifying CDFs.
"""
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
N = 10**5

# ---------- Inverse CDF: sampling Exp(1) via -log(U) ----------
U = rng.uniform(0, 1, N)
X_exp = -np.log(1 - U)  # Exp(1)
print("Exp(1) mean, var:", X_exp.mean(), X_exp.var())  # should be 1, 1

# ---------- Box-Muller for N(0, 1) ----------
U1, U2 = rng.uniform(0, 1, N // 2), rng.uniform(0, 1, N // 2)
Z1 = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(U1)) * np.cos(2 * np.pi * U2)
Z2 = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log(U1)) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * U2)
Z = np.concatenate([Z1, Z2])
print("N(0,1) mean, var:", Z.mean(), Z.var())  # should be 0, 1
print("Kolmogorov-Smirnov p-value vs N(0,1):",
      stats.kstest(Z, 'norm').pvalue)

# ---------- Poisson limit: Binomial(n, lambda/n) -> Poisson(lambda) ----------
lam = 3.0
for n in [10, 100, 1000]:
    samples = rng.binomial(n, lam/n, N)
    emp_mean = samples.mean()
    emp_var = samples.var()
    print(f"n={n}: empirical mean={emp_mean:.3f}, var={emp_var:.3f}, "
          f"target Poisson(lam={lam}) mean=var={lam}")

# ---------- Borel-Cantelli demo: sum P(A_n) < infty => only finitely many occur ----------
# A_n = {|X_n| > n^2} for X_n ~ N(0, 1) iid. P(A_n) ~ exp(-n^4/2), sum converges.
# Expect only finitely many occurrences.
X_seq = rng.standard_normal(10**6)
occurrences = np.sum([np.abs(X_seq[n]) > n**2 for n in range(1, 100)])
print(f"Borel-Cantelli I demo: {occurrences} occurrences (should be 0 almost surely)")

# ---------- Independence check: correlation vs. true independence ----------
# Uncorrelated but not independent: X ~ N(0, 1), Y = X^2 - 1
X = rng.standard_normal(N)
Y = X**2 - 1
print(f"Corr(X, Y) = {np.corrcoef(X, Y)[0, 1]:.4f} (should be ~0)")
print(f"E[X Y] = {np.mean(X * Y):.4f}, E[X] * E[Y] = {np.mean(X) * np.mean(Y):.4f}")
# However, X and Y are clearly dependent: Y is a function of X!

# ---------- Tail sigma algebra demo: limsup of {|S_n|/sqrt(n) > c} ----------
n_trials = 1000
path_length = 10000
trial_outcomes = []
for _ in range(n_trials):
    steps = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=path_length)
    S = np.cumsum(steps)
    max_ratio = np.max(np.abs(S[1:]) / np.sqrt(np.arange(1, path_length)))
    trial_outcomes.append(max_ratio)
print(f"Max |S_n|/sqrt(n): empirical 95% ≈ {np.percentile(trial_outcomes, 95):.2f}")
# The distribution of sup|S_n|/sqrt(n) has its own theory; by LIL, eventually unbounded.
```

## Topic 2.1.7 — [QUANT APPLICATION] Probability in Finance

### Application 1. Asset return distributions

The log-return $r_t = \log(S_t/S_{t-1})$ is routinely modeled as:
- **Geometric Brownian Motion (Black-Scholes)**: $r_t \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ iid. Analytically tractable; mismatches fat tails and volatility clustering.
- **Student-t**: heavier tails, finite variance only if degrees-of-freedom $\nu > 2$.
- **Stable distributions**: infinite variance, mathematically elegant, match extreme tails of some asset classes.
- **Generalized hyperbolic**: skew and excess kurtosis, used for FX and commodity returns.

The empirical question is which distribution fits best; the answer depends on asset class, horizon, and purpose.

### Application 2. Tail risk and VaR

$\text{VaR}_\alpha(L) = \inf\{\ell : \mathbb P(L > \ell) \le 1 - \alpha\} = F_L^{-1}(\alpha)$: the quantile of the loss distribution. Computing VaR is inverse CDF evaluation. Basel III mandates ES (expected shortfall) which is $\mathbb E[L | L > \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha] = \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_\alpha^1 \mathrm{VaR}_u \, du$.

### Application 3. Independence and portfolio diversification

Classical mean-variance relies on *uncorrelated* (not just independent) returns: $\operatorname{Var}(\sum w_i r_i) = \sum w_i^2 \sigma_i^2$ under orthogonality. Actual returns are not independent (common factors, lead-lag, contagion), so practitioners use factor models $r_i = \beta_i F + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i$ more plausibly independent (across $i$, at least).

### Application 4. Borel-Cantelli and event risk

For an i.i.d. sequence of daily jump probabilities (e.g. earnings announcements, Fed days), if $\sum p_t = \infty$ then jumps occur infinitely often a.s. (BC II). For a long-horizon trader, "rare" events *will* happen.

### Application 5. Kolmogorov's 0-1 law for long-run behavior

Any event determined by the "infinite future" of an iid process (e.g. $\lim r_n = 0$, $\liminf r_n > 0$, convergence of sums) has probability $0$ or $1$. So many conjectures reduce to proving strict positive probability, which then forces full probability. Example: the question "does $\sum r_n$ converge?" is determined by the tail, so has probability $0$ or $1$; Kolmogorov's three-series theorem (Module 2.3) then gives necessary and sufficient conditions.

### Application 6. Hewitt-Savage for i.i.d. symmetric events

"Does the random walk $S_n$ hit $0$ infinitely often?" is exchangeable (permuting $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ preserves $S_n = 0$), so probability is $0$ or $1$ by Hewitt-Savage (Kolmogorov's 0-1 also works here but is weaker). This is recurrence/transience dichotomy.

### Application 7. Kolmogorov extension and stochastic process construction

Brownian motion, Poisson processes, Lévy processes, Markov chains, and Gaussian random fields are all constructed via Kolmogorov's extension theorem: specify finite-dimensional distributions consistently, build the process. For Brownian: $\mathcal L(W_{t_1}, \ldots, W_{t_n})$ = centered Gaussian with covariance $\min(t_i, t_j)$.

### Application 8. Copulas: joint distribution of returns

Sklar's theorem: $F(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = C(F_1(x_1), \ldots, F_n(x_n))$ for some copula $C$ on $[0, 1]^n$. Choosing the copula separately from marginals allows modelling tail dependence (assets jointly crash) while keeping marginals flexible. Used in credit derivatives (CDOs) and multivariate risk models.

## Module Summary

- A **probability space** is a measure space with $\mathbb P(\Omega) = 1$.
- A **random variable** is a measurable function; its **distribution** (or **law**) is the pushforward measure.
- Every CDF $F$ corresponds to a unique probability measure; the **quantile transform** lets us realize any distribution on $([0, 1], \lambda)$.
- **Independence** is the factorization of joint distributions. It is preserved by measurable transformations and by grouping.
- **Borel-Cantelli I**: $\sum \mathbb P(A_n) < \infty \Rightarrow \mathbb P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 0$.
- **Borel-Cantelli II** (independent events): $\sum \mathbb P(A_n) = \infty \Rightarrow \mathbb P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) = 1$.
- **Kolmogorov's 0-1 law**: tail events of an independent sequence have probability $0$ or $1$.
- **Hewitt-Savage 0-1 law**: exchangeable events of an iid sequence are trivial.
- **Kolmogorov's extension theorem**: consistent finite-dimensional distributions glue into a stochastic process.
