## 1. Why classical integration fails

### 1.1 The naïve attempt

We want to give meaning to

$$
\int_0^T H_s\, dB_s
$$

for an "integrand" process $H$ and a Brownian motion $B$. The first instinct is to mimic the Riemann–Stieltjes integral: pick a partition $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = T$ and form

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_{t_k^*} \bigl( B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k} \bigr),
$$

where $t_k^* \in [t_k, t_{k+1}]$ is a tag, and hope the sum converges as the mesh $\|\pi\| \to 0$.

The catastrophic problem was established in Module 3.1, Section 4:

> **Theorem (Infinite total variation of Brownian paths).** For $P$-almost every $\omega$, the path $t \mapsto B_t(\omega)$ has infinite total variation on every non-degenerate interval.

The Riemann–Stieltjes integral $\int f\, dg$ is only well-defined (for a generic continuous $f$) when $g$ has bounded variation. Brownian paths do not. Worse, the value of the sum above depends on the choice of tag $t_k^*$:

- If $t_k^* = t_k$ (left endpoint, non-anticipative), we get the **Itô integral**.
- If $t_k^* = (t_k + t_{k+1})/2$ (midpoint), we get the **Stratonovich integral**.
- If $t_k^* = t_{k+1}$ (right endpoint), we get a third object (the "backward Itô integral", which will show up for anticipating calculus).

These limits differ by terms of order $\frac{1}{2} \int_0^T H'_s\, d\langle B\rangle_s = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T H'_s\, ds$ (when $H$ is sufficiently smooth). **This non-uniqueness is the signature of stochastic integration — you must specify the convention before the object exists.**

### 1.2 A warm-up computation

Take $H_s = B_s$ and try the left endpoint sum:

$$
S_n^{\text{left}} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} B_{t_k}(B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k}).
$$

Using the identity $a(b - a) = \frac{1}{2}(b^2 - a^2) - \frac{1}{2}(b - a)^2$,

$$
S_n^{\text{left}} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\bigl(B_{t_{k+1}}^2 - B_{t_k}^2\bigr) - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k})^2 \xrightarrow[\|\pi\|\to 0]{L^2} \frac{1}{2}B_T^2 - \frac{1}{2}T.
$$

The second sum converges to the quadratic variation $T$ of $B$ (Module 3.1, Theorem 4.2). Therefore we are forced into the rule

$$
\int_0^T B_s\, dB_s = \tfrac{1}{2}B_T^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}T.
$$

The **extra $-\tfrac{1}{2}T$** is the first hint of the **Itô correction**, which in Module 3.3 will become Itô's lemma. By contrast, the midpoint sum gives $\tfrac{1}{2}B_T^2$ — the "classical" answer — so Stratonovich is compatible with the ordinary chain rule but loses the martingale property that makes Itô so powerful in finance.

We take the Itô convention (left endpoint) throughout this module. Stratonovich will reappear in Section 9 and in Module 3.3.

## 2. The probability framework and predictable processes

Fix a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ carrying a standard Brownian motion $B$ and its **augmented natural filtration** $\mathbb{F} = (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}$:

$$
\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma\bigl(B_s : s \le t\bigr) \vee \mathcal{N},
$$

where $\mathcal{N}$ is the collection of $P$-null sets. This filtration satisfies the **usual conditions**: it is right-continuous and complete. (See Module 2.6 for why these matter.)

### 2.1 The predictable $\sigma$-algebra

**Definition 2.1.** The **predictable $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal{P}$ on $\Omega \times [0, \infty)$ is the $\sigma$-algebra generated by sets of the form

$$
F \times (s, t], \qquad F \in \mathcal{F}_s,\ 0 \le s < t,
$$

together with $\{0\} \times F$ for $F \in \mathcal{F}_0$. A process $H : \Omega \times [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ is **predictable** if it is $\mathcal{P}$-measurable.

**Intuition.** Predictable means "known just before $t$". The left-continuous adapted processes are predictable; in fact they generate $\mathcal{P}$. For Brownian-driven SDEs with continuous coefficients, **adapted = predictable** (up to indistinguishability), because any adapted process with continuous paths equals a left-continuous adapted process almost surely. So for practical continuous-time finance, the predictability subtlety mostly dissolves. It is crucial only when discontinuous integrators (Module 3.5) arrive.

### 2.2 The space $L^2_{\text{pred}}(P \otimes dt)$

Let

$$
\mathcal{L}^2 = \mathcal{L}^2_T := \Bigl\{ H \text{ predictable} : \|H\|_2^2 := E\Bigl[\int_0^T H_s^2\, ds\Bigr] < \infty \Bigr\}.
$$

This is a **Hilbert space** with inner product $\langle H, K\rangle = E[\int_0^T H_s K_s\, ds]$ (completeness follows from Fubini and the completeness of $L^2(\Omega \times [0,T], \mathcal{P}, P \otimes dt)$). The Itô integral, to be built, is an isometry from $\mathcal{L}^2$ into $L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_T, P)$.

## 3. Simple predictable processes and the elementary Itô integral

**Definition 3.1.** A process $H$ is **simple predictable** (or **elementary**) if

$$
H_s(\omega) = \xi_0(\omega)\mathbf{1}_{\{0\}}(s) + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \xi_k(\omega)\mathbf{1}_{(t_k, t_{k+1}]}(s),
$$

where $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = T$ is a deterministic partition and each $\xi_k$ is $\mathcal{F}_{t_k}$-measurable and bounded. Let $\mathcal{S}$ denote the space of simple predictable processes.

**Definition 3.2 (Elementary Itô integral).** For $H \in \mathcal{S}$ as above, define

$$
I(H)_t := \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \xi_k \bigl(B_{t_{k+1} \wedge t} - B_{t_k \wedge t}\bigr).
$$

Because $\xi_k$ is $\mathcal{F}_{t_k}$-measurable and **therefore independent of the future Brownian increment $B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k}$** (this independence is the reason we insist on left endpoints!), the sum makes sense and has clean probabilistic properties.

**Proposition 3.3 (Basic properties of $I$).**

1. **Linearity:** $I(\alpha H + \beta K) = \alpha I(H) + \beta I(K)$ on $\mathcal{S}$.
2. **Mean zero:** $E[I(H)_t] = 0$ for all $t$.
3. **Martingale:** $(I(H)_t)_{t \in [0,T]}$ is an $\mathbb{F}$-martingale with continuous paths.
4. **Itô isometry on simple processes:**
   $$
   E\bigl[ I(H)_T^2 \bigr] = E\Bigl[\int_0^T H_s^2\, ds\Bigr].
   $$

*Proof.* Linearity is obvious. For (2), each term $\xi_k(B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k})$ has mean zero by independence and $E[B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k}] = 0$.

For (3), take $s \le t$. Any partition point can be added without changing $I(H)$, so WLOG $s, t \in \{t_0, \ldots, t_n\}$, say $s = t_j$ and $t = t_m$. Then

$$
I(H)_t - I(H)_s = \sum_{k=j}^{m-1} \xi_k(B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k}).
$$

For $k \ge j$, both $\xi_k$ and the increment are determined by events independent of $\mathcal{F}_s$ (for the Brownian increment) while $\xi_k \in \mathcal{F}_{t_k} \supseteq \mathcal{F}_s$. Conditioning on $\mathcal{F}_s$ and pulling out the measurable $\xi_k$:

$$
E\bigl[ \xi_k(B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k}) \mid \mathcal{F}_s\bigr]
 = E\bigl[ \xi_k\, E[B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k} \mid \mathcal{F}_{t_k}] \mid \mathcal{F}_s\bigr] = 0.
$$

Continuity of paths is automatic: between partition points, $I(H)_t$ is a linear combination of Brownian paths, which are continuous.

For (4), expand the square. The cross terms vanish by the independent-increments argument:

$$
E\bigl[\xi_j \xi_k (B_{t_{j+1}}-B_{t_j})(B_{t_{k+1}}-B_{t_k})\bigr] = 0 \quad \text{for } j < k,
$$

because conditioning on $\mathcal{F}_{t_k}$ makes the last factor mean zero. The diagonal terms give

$$
E\bigl[\xi_k^2 (B_{t_{k+1}}-B_{t_k})^2\bigr] = E[\xi_k^2] \cdot (t_{k+1} - t_k) = E\Bigl[\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} H_s^2\, ds\Bigr],
$$

using the same independence and $E[(B_{t_{k+1}}-B_{t_k})^2] = t_{k+1} - t_k$. Summing gives the isometry. $\square$

The key structural fact is (4): **$I : (\mathcal{S}, \|\cdot\|_2) \to L^2(P)$ is a linear isometry, where the norm on the left is the $L^2(P \otimes dt)$ norm restricted to $[0,T]$**. A bounded linear isometry from a dense subspace of a Hilbert space into a Hilbert space extends uniquely to the whole domain. The next section executes this extension.

## 4. Density of simple processes and extension

**Theorem 4.1.** $\mathcal{S}$ is dense in $\mathcal{L}^2$ in the norm $\|\cdot\|_2$.

*Proof sketch (three-step approximation).*

**Step 1: Left-continuous adapted bounded processes are dense in $\mathcal{L}^2$.**

Given $H \in \mathcal{L}^2$, by truncation $H^{(N)} = (H \wedge N) \vee (-N)$ dominated convergence gives $\|H^{(N)} - H\|_2 \to 0$. So we may assume $H$ bounded.

For bounded $H$, define the regularizations
$$
H^\varepsilon_s := \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{(s-\varepsilon)^+}^{s} H_u\, du, \qquad s \ge 0.
$$

By Lebesgue's differentiation theorem, $H^\varepsilon_s \to H_s$ for $P \otimes dt$-a.e. $(\omega, s)$. Because $H$ is predictable and the integration is backward in time, $H^\varepsilon$ is predictable with continuous — in particular left-continuous — paths. Dominated convergence with envelope $\|H\|_\infty$ gives $\|H^\varepsilon - H\|_2 \to 0$.

**Step 2: Bounded left-continuous adapted processes are approximable by simple processes.**

Given bounded left-continuous adapted $H$, define for each $n$,
$$
H^{(n)}_s := \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} H_{kT/n}\, \mathbf{1}_{(kT/n,\ (k+1)T/n]}(s).
$$

Because $H$ is left-continuous, $H^{(n)}_s(\omega) \to H_s(\omega)$ pointwise (except possibly at $s=0$ which has measure zero). Boundedness supplies an envelope; DCT gives $\|H^{(n)} - H\|_2 \to 0$.

**Step 3: Concatenate.** Given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $N, H^\varepsilon$, and then $H^{(n)}$ so that the sum of errors is $< \varepsilon$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.2 (Itô integral, $L^2$ construction).** There exists a unique linear map $I : \mathcal{L}^2 \to L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_T, P)$, extending the elementary integral, such that

$$
E\bigl[I(H)^2\bigr] = E\Bigl[\int_0^T H_s^2\, ds\Bigr] \qquad (\textbf{Itô isometry}).
$$

*Proof.* By Proposition 3.3, $I$ is an isometry on $\mathcal{S}$. By Theorem 4.1, $\mathcal{S}$ is dense in the Hilbert space $\mathcal{L}^2$. By a standard result (Module 1.5), bounded linear maps extend uniquely to the completion. The target $L^2(P)$ is complete, so the extension lands there. $\square$

We write $\int_0^T H_s\, dB_s := I(H)$. For $0 \le t \le T$ define
$$
\int_0^t H_s\, dB_s := I(H \mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}).
$$

### 4.1 Process-level extension

Actually, we want not just the random variable $\int_0^T H_s dB_s$ but the whole process $t \mapsto \int_0^t H_s dB_s$. To build this, apply the construction to $H^{(t)} := H \mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}$ for each $t$. Each is in $\mathcal{L}^2$ (for the horizon $T$), and the map $t \mapsto I(H^{(t)})$ is the Itô integral process $(I(H)_t)_{0 \le t \le T}$.

It remains to show this process has a **continuous modification**; this is the topic of Section 5.

## 5. The Itô integral as a continuous martingale

**Theorem 5.1.** For $H \in \mathcal{L}^2$, the Itô integral process $(I(H)_t)_{0 \le t \le T}$ admits a continuous modification which is an $\mathbb{F}$-martingale.

*Proof.* Take $H^{(n)} \in \mathcal{S}$ with $\|H^{(n)} - H\|_2 \to 0$. The processes $M^{(n)}_t := I(H^{(n)})_t$ are continuous martingales (Proposition 3.3). The differences $M^{(n)} - M^{(m)} = I(H^{(n)} - H^{(m)})$ are continuous martingales, so by **Doob's $L^2$ inequality** (Module 2.6, Theorem 7.2),

$$
E\bigl[\sup_{t \le T}|M^{(n)}_t - M^{(m)}_t|^2\bigr] \le 4\, E\bigl[|M^{(n)}_T - M^{(m)}_T|^2\bigr] = 4\|H^{(n)} - H^{(m)}\|_2^2 \to 0.
$$

Therefore $(M^{(n)})$ is Cauchy in the Banach space $\mathcal{C}([0,T])$ of continuous paths under $L^2$-uniform norm. Passing to a subsequence, $M^{(n_k)}$ converges a.s. uniformly to a continuous process $M$; $M$ is the continuous modification of $I(H)$. It is a martingale because martingale property is preserved under $L^1$ convergence of $\mathcal{F}_t$-conditional expectations, which follows from $\sup_t E[|M^{(n)}_t - M_t|^2] \to 0$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.2 (Doob's inequality for the Itô integral).** For $H \in \mathcal{L}^2$,

$$
E\Bigl[\sup_{t \le T}\Bigl|\int_0^t H_s dB_s\Bigr|^2\Bigr] \le 4\, E\Bigl[\int_0^T H_s^2 ds\Bigr].
$$

This is heavily used in estimates of SDE solutions and in deriving BDG inequalities (Module 3.7).

## 6. Quadratic variation of the Itô integral

**Theorem 6.1.** For $H \in \mathcal{L}^2$, the continuous martingale $M_t := \int_0^t H_s dB_s$ has quadratic variation

$$
\langle M\rangle_t = \int_0^t H_s^2\, ds.
$$

That is, for any sequence of partitions $\pi_n$ of $[0,t]$ with $\|\pi_n\| \to 0$,

$$
\sum_{[u,v]\in\pi_n}\bigl(M_v - M_u\bigr)^2 \xrightarrow{\ P\ } \int_0^t H_s^2\, ds.
$$

*Proof (simple case).* For $H \in \mathcal{S}$ with partition $0 = t_0 < \dots < t_n$, write on each subinterval $(t_k, t_{k+1}]$, $M_v - M_u = \xi_k(B_v - B_u)$. The quadratic-variation sum telescopes into
$$
\sum_k \xi_k^2 \sum_{[u,v] \subset (t_k, t_{k+1}]}(B_v - B_u)^2 \to \sum_k \xi_k^2 (t_{k+1}-t_k) = \int_0^t H_s^2\, ds,
$$

using $\langle B\rangle_t = t$ from Module 3.1. The general case follows by approximation in $\mathcal{L}^2$ using the $L^2$ bound
$$
E\Bigl[\bigl(\text{QV}_n(I(H)) - \int_0^t H_s^2 ds\bigr)^2\Bigr] \le C\, \|\pi_n\|\, E\Bigl[\int_0^t H_s^4 ds\Bigr] + \text{approximation error},
$$

extending from the simple case via density. $\square$

This identifies the Itô integral's quadratic variation, a fact we use constantly (Itô's lemma, BDG, SDEs). A cleaner statement: **$\langle I(H)\rangle = H^2 \cdot \langle B\rangle = \int H^2 ds$** — the quadratic variation "pulls through" as a square of the integrand.

## 7. Extension to locally square-integrable integrands

The Hilbert-space construction requires $E[\int_0^T H_s^2 ds] < \infty$. In practice this is too strict: e.g. for $H_s = B_s$ over $[0, T]$, $E[\int_0^T B_s^2 ds] = T^2/2 < \infty$ — fine. But for integrands like $e^{B_s^2/2}$ near blowup, or stopping-time-driven integrals, we need to extend to **locally $L^2$** integrands.

**Definition 7.1.** Let $\mathcal{L}^2_{\text{loc}}$ be the space of predictable $H$ such that
$$
\int_0^T H_s^2\, ds < \infty \qquad P\text{-a.s.}
$$

**Theorem 7.2.** The Itô integral extends to $H \in \mathcal{L}^2_{\text{loc}}$ via **localization**: define stopping times

$$
\tau_n := \inf\Bigl\{ t \ge 0 : \int_0^t H_s^2\, ds \ge n\Bigr\} \wedge T.
$$

Then $H \mathbf{1}_{[0, \tau_n]} \in \mathcal{L}^2$; define $I(H)_t := I(H\mathbf{1}_{[0, \tau_n]})_t$ for $t \le \tau_n$. These are consistent as $n$ grows, giving a well-defined continuous process on $[0, T]$.

The resulting $I(H)$ is a **continuous local martingale**, not necessarily a true martingale. It is a true martingale iff $E[\int_0^T H_s^2 ds] < \infty$ (and in some marginal cases where the stochastic exponential stays honest, like under Novikov).

### 7.1 Local martingales: definition and subtleties

**Definition 7.3.** A process $M$ is a **local martingale** (with continuous paths, starting at $0$) if there exist stopping times $\tau_n \uparrow \infty$ a.s. such that each stopped process $M^{\tau_n}$ is a true $\mathbb{F}$-martingale. We say $(\tau_n)$ **reduces** $M$.

Every continuous true martingale is a local martingale (take $\tau_n = T$), but not vice versa: the Itô integral of a locally-$L^2$ integrand can "blow up in expectation" even while being $L^2$-locally finite.

**Warning 7.4.** A nonnegative local martingale is a **supermartingale** (Fatou). It is a true martingale iff $E[M_t] = E[M_0]$ for each $t$. This is how Novikov's condition (Module 3.3) is established for stochastic exponentials.

## 8. Itô integrals against semi-martingales

Brownian motion is a martingale. More generally, a **semi-martingale** is a process $X = X_0 + M + A$ where $M$ is a (continuous) local martingale and $A$ is a process of bounded variation. The integral $\int H dX = \int H dM + \int H dA$ decomposes into an Itô (for $M$) and Stieltjes (for $A$) part.

**Prototype: Itô process.** An **Itô process** is
$$
X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t \mu_s\, ds + \int_0^t \sigma_s\, dB_s,
$$
with $\mu, \sigma$ predictable and locally integrable / locally $L^2$. Its quadratic variation is $\langle X\rangle_t = \int_0^t \sigma_s^2 ds$. Integrals against $X$:
$$
\int_0^t H_s\, dX_s := \int_0^t H_s \mu_s\, ds + \int_0^t H_s\sigma_s\, dB_s.
$$

This is the class of integrators appearing in Black–Scholes-style finance. More general semi-martingales (including jumps) appear in Module 3.5.

## 9. Itô vs Stratonovich

The **Stratonovich integral** uses the midpoint:
$$
\int_0^T H_s \circ dB_s := \lim_{\|\pi\| \to 0}\sum_k \frac{H_{t_k} + H_{t_{k+1}}}{2}(B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k}).
$$

When $H = f(B)$ for smooth $f$, the two integrals are related by
$$
\int_0^T f(B_s) \circ dB_s = \int_0^T f(B_s) dB_s + \frac{1}{2}\int_0^T f'(B_s) ds.
$$

The correction term is the **Itô correction** encountered in Section 1. Stratonovich satisfies the classical chain rule, which makes it natural in differential-geometric settings (SDEs on manifolds). But it is **not a martingale** — which makes Itô the tool of choice for pricing and hedging, where martingale methods drive everything.

## 10. Stochastic Fubini

Often we need to interchange a stochastic integral with a deterministic one: think of moment-generating functions of integrated processes.

**Theorem 10.1 (Stochastic Fubini, simple form).** Let $H : [0, T] \times [0, S] \times \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ be $\mathcal{B}([0, S]) \otimes \mathcal{P}$-measurable with
$$
\int_0^S E\Bigl[\int_0^T H(t, u, \omega)^2\, dt\Bigr]^{1/2} du < \infty.
$$
Then with probability $1$,
$$
\int_0^S\!\Bigl(\int_0^T H(t, u)\, dB_t\Bigr) du \;=\; \int_0^T\!\Bigl(\int_0^S H(t, u)\, du\Bigr) dB_t.
$$

The proof is by checking the identity on simple integrands (where both sides are sums) and passing to limits using the Itô isometry. The condition guarantees the $L^2$-norm of the double integral is finite; measurability subtleties are handled by choosing a predictable version of the inner integral. Applications include computing $E[\exp(\int_0^T B_s\, ds)]$ and option price integrals.

## 11. Worked examples

### Example 11.1: $\int_0^T s\, dB_s$

Take $H_s = s$ (deterministic, hence predictable and $L^2$). Then
$$
I(H)_T = \int_0^T s\, dB_s
$$
is a Gaussian random variable (limit of Gaussian sums of independent increments weighted by deterministic constants) with

$$
E[I(H)_T] = 0, \qquad \text{Var}(I(H)_T) = \int_0^T s^2\, ds = \frac{T^3}{3}.
$$

By integration by parts (forthcoming in Module 3.3) we also have $\int_0^T s\, dB_s = T B_T - \int_0^T B_s\, ds$. Both sides are Gaussian with mean $0$ and variance $T^3/3$ — a nontrivial cross-check.

### Example 11.2: $\int_0^T B_s\, dB_s$

This is the Section 1 warm-up:
$$
\int_0^T B_s\, dB_s = \tfrac{1}{2}B_T^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}T.
$$

Check: $E[\tfrac{1}{2}B_T^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}T] = \tfrac{1}{2}T - \tfrac{1}{2}T = 0$. Good, as required by the martingale property. Variance:
$$
\text{Var}\Bigl(\int_0^T B_s dB_s\Bigr) = E\Bigl[\int_0^T B_s^2 ds\Bigr] = \int_0^T s\, ds = \frac{T^2}{2}.
$$

### Example 11.3: Stochastic exponential $\mathcal{E}(\lambda B)_t$

Define $Z_t = \exp(\lambda B_t - \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^2 t)$. Itô's lemma (preview) gives
$$
dZ_t = \lambda Z_t\, dB_t, \qquad Z_0 = 1,
$$
i.e. $Z_t = 1 + \lambda\int_0^t Z_s dB_s$. This is the **stochastic exponential of $\lambda B$**, a martingale by the moment-generating function of the Gaussian. It is the engine behind Girsanov's theorem and the change to the risk-neutral measure.

### Example 11.4: Discrete hedging PnL

Suppose we own a portfolio worth $V_t$ and we rebalance by holding $\Delta_s$ shares at time $s$, so
$$
V_t = V_0 + \int_0^t \Delta_s\, dS_s.
$$

With $S_t$ a GBM (Module 3.1), this is an Itô integral. In discrete time with $n$ rebalances, the PnL deviates from its continuous-time idealization by a variance $O(T/n)$ term — the **discrete hedging error**, which we will quantify rigorously in Module 3.3 via Itô's lemma and again in the options chapter.

## 12. [QUANT APPLICATION] — where this actually shows up

### 12.1 Self-financing portfolios and no-arbitrage pricing

In the Black–Scholes world, a trading strategy $(\Delta_t, B_t^0)$ (shares of stock, bonds) is **self-financing** iff
$$
V_t = V_0 + \int_0^t \Delta_s\, dS_s + \int_0^t B_s^0 r e^{rs}\, ds,
$$

where $S_t = S_0 e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t + \sigma B_t}$ is the stock. Discounting by $e^{-rt}$:
$$
\tilde V_t = V_0 + \int_0^t \Delta_s\, d\tilde S_s,
$$

and under the risk-neutral measure $Q$ (via Girsanov; Module 3.3), $\tilde S$ is a $Q$-martingale. Hence $\tilde V$ is a $Q$-local martingale — and if integrability holds, a true martingale. This is the **first fundamental theorem of asset pricing**: no-arbitrage corresponds to existence of $Q$ such that discounted prices are martingales. The Itô integral is what makes "continuous rebalancing with no transaction costs" well-defined in the first place.

### 12.2 Delta hedging PnL formula

If we short a call option of value $C_t = C(t, S_t)$ and hedge with $\Delta_t = \partial_S C$ shares of stock,
$$
P_t^{\text{hedged}} = C_t - \int_0^t \Delta_s\, dS_s.
$$

By Itô's lemma and the Black–Scholes PDE, this reduces to a pure gamma exposure times realized variance:
$$
P_t^{\text{hedged}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^t \Gamma_s (\sigma_{\text{real}}^2 - \sigma_{\text{impl}}^2)\, S_s^2\, ds.
$$

The cash flow from a delta-hedged book is literally an Itô integral of gamma × variance mismatch. This is why gamma, vega, and the volatility surface matter: they encode the integrator structure of PnL.

### 12.3 Monte Carlo simulation and Euler–Maruyama

When solving an SDE $dX_t = \mu(X_t) dt + \sigma(X_t) dB_t$ numerically, the **Euler–Maruyama** scheme approximates
$$
X_{t_{k+1}} \approx X_{t_k} + \mu(X_{t_k})\Delta t + \sigma(X_{t_k})\sqrt{\Delta t}\, Z_k, \qquad Z_k \sim N(0,1).
$$

This is literally the simple-integrand Itô integral with partition mesh $\Delta t$. Convergence of Euler–Maruyama to the true SDE solution uses the Itô isometry at its core. Strong order of convergence is $1/2$ — half an order slower than classical deterministic ODE Euler — because of the square-root scaling $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ of Brownian increments.

### 12.4 Greeks via Malliavin calculus (preview)

The modern "likelihood ratio method" for computing Greeks (delta, vega) writes, e.g.,
$$
\text{Delta} = E\Bigl[ f(S_T) \cdot \frac{1}{S_0 \sigma T}\int_0^T \frac{1}{\sigma(s, S_s)}\, dB_s \Bigr].
$$

The weight is an Itô integral of a specific predictable process — the "Malliavin weight". This technique is preferred over pathwise or finite-difference methods for discontinuous payoffs (binary options) because no payoff-derivatives are required.

## 13. Python: computing Itô integrals by simulation

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
T, n, M = 1.0, 10_000, 5_000
dt = T / n
times = np.linspace(0, T, n + 1)

def simulate_BM(M, n):
    dW = rng.standard_normal(size=(M, n)) * np.sqrt(dt)
    W = np.concatenate([np.zeros((M, 1)), np.cumsum(dW, axis=1)], axis=1)
    return W, dW

W, dW = simulate_BM(M, n)

# ---- (a) Itô integral of B_s against dB_s ----
# Left endpoint: sum B_{t_k} * (B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k})
left = np.sum(W[:, :-1] * dW, axis=1)
analytic = 0.5 * W[:, -1]**2 - 0.5 * T
print("Itô sum  mean:", left.mean(), "  analytic mean:", analytic.mean())
print("Itô sum  var :", left.var(),  "  analytic var :", analytic.var())
print("L2 diff (Itô): ", np.sqrt(np.mean((left - analytic)**2)))

# ---- (b) Stratonovich integral: midpoint ----
mid = 0.5 * (W[:, :-1] + W[:, 1:]) * dW
strat = np.sum(mid, axis=1)
print("Stratonovich mean:", strat.mean())  # should be ~0.5*T^2/? no, actually 0.5*B_T^2
print("0.5*B_T^2 mean:", (0.5 * W[:, -1]**2).mean())
# Stratonovich ~= 0.5 * B_T^2 exactly (no -T/2 correction)

# ---- (c) Itô isometry verification ----
# For H_s = s, Var(∫_0^T s dB_s) = ∫_0^T s^2 ds = T^3/3
H = times[:-1]          # deterministic integrand at left endpoints
left_int_s = (H * dW).sum(axis=1)
print("Var(∫ s dB_s) sim:", left_int_s.var(), "  theory T^3/3:", T**3 / 3)

# ---- (d) Stochastic exponential martingale ----
# Z_t = exp(lambda B_t - 0.5 lambda^2 t), martingale at each t ⇒ E[Z_t] = 1
lam = 0.7
Z_T = np.exp(lam * W[:, -1] - 0.5 * lam**2 * T)
print("E[Z_T] simulated:", Z_T.mean(), "  theory: 1")
```

### Sample output (commentary)

- `L2 diff (Itô)` should be ~$10^{-2}$ for $n = 10^4$; the error scales like $\sqrt{dt}$ because we're approximating a continuous-time integral by a Riemann sum.
- `Stratonovich mean` should be very close to `0.5*B_T^2` mean (i.e., $T/2 = 0.5$), confirming the chain-rule property of Stratonovich.
- `Var(∫ s dB_s)` should match $T^3/3 = 1/3$ almost exactly — deterministic integrand makes the sum Gaussian with computable variance.
- `E[Z_T]` should be very close to $1$.

The code also doubles as **Euler–Maruyama** infrastructure: replace `H` with $\sigma(t_k, X_{t_k})$ and $\mu(t_k, X_{t_k}) dt$ to simulate arbitrary SDEs.

## 14. Summary of main results

1. **The problem.** Riemann–Stieltjes integration fails for Brownian-driven integrands: paths have infinite variation, and the tag choice matters.
2. **Convention.** The **Itô integral** uses left endpoints — the only choice that preserves non-anticipation (integrand doesn't know the future increment), hence the martingale property.
3. **Construction.** Define on simple predictable processes $\mathcal{S}$ in the obvious way; extend by Hilbert-space density (Theorem 4.1) + Itô isometry (Theorem 4.2) to $\mathcal{L}^2$.
4. **Process-level extension.** The integral has a continuous modification (Theorem 5.1) via Doob's $L^2$ inequality, and is a continuous martingale.
5. **Quadratic variation.** $\langle \int H dB\rangle_t = \int_0^t H_s^2\, ds$ (Theorem 6.1).
6. **Localization.** Extend to $\mathcal{L}^2_{\text{loc}}$ via stopping times; the resulting object is a continuous local martingale.
7. **Semi-martingales.** Include bounded-variation drift; Itô processes $dX = \mu dt + \sigma dB$ are the continuous semi-martingales driving continuous-time finance.
8. **Itô vs Stratonovich.** Stratonovich has classical chain rule (no correction) but is not a martingale.
