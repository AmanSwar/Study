## 4.5.0 Why Merton?

Robert C. Merton's 1969 paper "Lifetime Portfolio Selection under Uncertainty: the Continuous-Time Case" and his 1971 companion "Optimum Consumption and Portfolio Rules in a Continuous-Time Model" are the foundational papers of continuous-time finance. Together with Black-Scholes (1973) they form the mathematical spine of modern quantitative finance; Merton shared the 1997 Nobel Prize in Economics with Scholes largely for this work.

Three reasons the Merton problem matters:

1. **It is solvable.** Under CRRA (power) utility, or CARA (exponential) utility, the HJB equation separates and reduces to an ODE with closed-form solution. There are almost no other nontrivial continuous-time control problems with such clean solutions.

2. **It gives the "Merton ratio"** $\pi^* = (\mu - r)/(\gamma \sigma^2)$, a formula so compact and interpretable that it is taught in every finance PhD program and used, in various generalizations, in the actual portfolio allocation algorithms of pension funds, endowments, and life-cycle robo-advisers.

3. **It is the reference point.** Every extension — transaction costs, labor income, stochastic volatility, partial information, ambiguity aversion, habit formation — is phrased as "the Merton problem plus X." Understanding the pure Merton solution lets you instantly see what each extension adds or takes away.

## 4.5.1 The Consumption-Investment Problem

### Setup

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a probability space with filtration $(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}$ generated by a 1-dimensional Brownian motion $(B_t)$.

**Assets.** Two assets are available:
- **Riskless asset** (money-market account) with price $S^0_t$ growing at constant rate $r$:
$$dS^0_t = r S^0_t \, dt.$$
- **Risky asset** (stock) following geometric Brownian motion:
$$dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dB_t,$$
with constants $\mu > r$ (equity premium positive), $\sigma > 0$.

**Control variables.** At each time $t$ the investor chooses:
- $\pi_t$: the **fraction** of wealth invested in the risky asset (so $1 - \pi_t$ is in cash). Short selling ($\pi_t < 0$) and leverage ($\pi_t > 1$) are permitted unless otherwise restricted.
- $C_t \ge 0$: the consumption **rate** (dollars per unit time).

Both are $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-progressively measurable and subject to admissibility conditions given below.

### Wealth dynamics

Let $W_t$ denote the wealth at time $t$. The dollar amount in the stock is $\pi_t W_t$; the dollar amount in cash is $(1 - \pi_t) W_t$. Over $[t, t+dt]$:

- Stock holding grows by $\pi_t W_t \cdot (\mu \, dt + \sigma \, dB_t)$.
- Cash holding grows by $(1 - \pi_t) W_t \cdot r \, dt$.
- Consumption withdraws $C_t \, dt$.

So the self-financing wealth equation is:
$$\boxed{dW_t = \left[ (r + \pi_t (\mu - r)) W_t - C_t \right] dt + \pi_t \sigma W_t \, dB_t,}$$
with initial wealth $W_0 = w > 0$.

**Admissibility.** A control $(\pi, C)$ is admissible if:
- $(\pi_t, C_t)$ is progressively measurable.
- $\int_0^T (\pi_t^2 + C_t) \, dt < \infty$ a.s.
- $W_t \ge 0$ for all $t \in [0, T]$ a.s. (non-negativity / no-bankruptcy).

The non-negativity constraint matters: with CRRA utility $U(c) = c^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ we have $U(0) = -\infty$ for $\gamma > 1$, so hitting zero is infinitely painful and the investor will never choose a policy that lets wealth touch zero. With CARA utility $U(c) = -e^{-\alpha c}/\alpha$ we allow $W_t$ to go negative (the investor just "borrows against future labor income"), so admissibility differs.

### Utility functions

The investor maximizes expected utility. We consider **intertemporal separable preferences**:

$$J(t, w; \pi, C) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \int_t^T e^{-\rho (s-t)} U(C_s) \, ds + e^{-\rho (T-t)} B(W_T) \, \Big| \, W_t = w \right],$$

where:
- $\rho \ge 0$ is the subjective discount rate (time preference);
- $U(c)$ is the per-period utility of consumption;
- $B(w)$ is the utility of terminal wealth (bequest function).

**CRRA (power) utility.**
$$U(c) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{c^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} & \gamma > 0, \gamma \ne 1 \\ \ln c & \gamma = 1 \end{cases}, \qquad B(w) = \varepsilon \, \dfrac{w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}.$$

The parameter $\gamma > 0$ is the **coefficient of relative risk aversion** (CRRA): $-c U''(c)/U'(c) = \gamma$. Higher $\gamma$ = more risk averse. The log case ($\gamma = 1$) is the famous "Kelly" investor.

The parameter $\varepsilon \ge 0$ is the weight on bequest. Set $\varepsilon = 0$ for pure consumption, or let $\varepsilon \to \infty$ with $U \equiv 0$ for a pure terminal-wealth problem.

**CARA (exponential) utility.**
$$U(c) = -\dfrac{1}{\alpha} e^{-\alpha c}, \qquad B(w) = -\dfrac{1}{\alpha} e^{-\alpha w},$$
with constant absolute risk aversion $-U''/U' = \alpha$. Under CARA the investor's *dollar* (not *fractional*) allocation to stocks is independent of wealth, which is the opposite of CRRA.

### Value function and HJB

The value function is
$$v(t, w) = \sup_{(\pi, C) \in \mathcal{A}} J(t, w; \pi, C),$$
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of admissible controls.

By the HJB derivation from Module 4.4, applied to the wealth SDE, we get:

$$\boxed{\partial_t v + \sup_{\pi, c \ge 0} \left\{ U(c) - \rho v + \left[ (r + \pi (\mu - r)) w - c \right] \partial_w v + \tfrac{1}{2} \pi^2 \sigma^2 w^2 \partial_{ww} v \right\} = 0,}$$
with terminal condition $v(T, w) = B(w)$.

More precisely, if we define the time-independent operator we should write $\partial_t v$ on the LHS but note that our objective is NOT in standard form because the running reward is $e^{-\rho (s-t)} U(C_s)$ not $U(C_s)$. Equivalently, define $\tilde v(t, w) = e^{\rho t} v(t, w)$ (discount flag); then $\tilde v$ satisfies the HJB with running cost $U(c)$ and generator shifted by $-\rho$. In practice, the standard convention is to absorb the discount into the HJB as above, which is equivalent to saying the Hamiltonian is
$$\mathcal{H}(t, w, v, v_w, v_{ww}) = -\rho v + U(c^*) + [(r + \pi^*(\mu-r))w - c^*] v_w + \tfrac{1}{2} (\pi^*)^2 \sigma^2 w^2 v_{ww}.$$

### Pointwise maximization

The $(\pi, c)$ optimization inside the sup is pointwise (no coupling through time). We can separate:

**First-order condition for $c$:**
$$U'(c) - \partial_w v = 0 \ \Longrightarrow \ c^*(t, w) = (U')^{-1}(\partial_w v(t, w)).$$

This is the **envelope condition**: marginal utility of consumption equals marginal utility of wealth. For CRRA $U'(c) = c^{-\gamma}$, so $(U')^{-1}(y) = y^{-1/\gamma}$ and $c^* = (v_w)^{-1/\gamma}$.

**First-order condition for $\pi$:** Setting $\partial_\pi$ of the objective to zero:
$$(\mu - r) w \, \partial_w v + \pi \sigma^2 w^2 \partial_{ww} v = 0 \ \Longrightarrow \ \pi^*(t, w) = -\dfrac{(\mu - r) \partial_w v}{\sigma^2 w \, \partial_{ww} v}.$$

Substituting $c^*$ and $\pi^*$ back into the HJB gives a PDE **in $v$ alone**. For specific utility functions the PDE decouples into an ODE.

## 4.5.2 HJB for CRRA and the Power Ansatz

Assume $\gamma > 0, \gamma \ne 1$. The HJB is a nonlinear PDE in $(t, w)$. The key insight: because CRRA utility is homogeneous of degree $1-\gamma$ in $c$, and the wealth dynamics are homogeneous of degree 1 in $w$ (with $\pi$ a fraction), the value function inherits this homogeneity.

### The ansatz

Try
$$\boxed{v(t, w) = h(t) \cdot \dfrac{w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}}$$
for some function $h(t) > 0$ to be determined, with terminal condition $v(T, w) = \varepsilon w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ implying $h(T) = \varepsilon$.

**Why this ansatz?** Under CRRA, scaling all of wealth, consumption, and risky-asset dollar holdings by a factor $\lambda$ multiplies the objective by $\lambda^{1-\gamma}$. So $v$ must be homogeneous in $w$ of degree $1-\gamma$, hence $v(t, w) = h(t) w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$.

Compute partial derivatives:
- $\partial_t v = h'(t) w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$.
- $\partial_w v = h(t) w^{-\gamma}$.
- $\partial_{ww} v = -\gamma h(t) w^{-\gamma - 1}$.

So $\partial_w v > 0$ and $\partial_{ww} v < 0$ (strictly concave, as expected for a risk-averse value function).

### Optimal controls from the ansatz

**Optimal portfolio.**
$$\pi^*(t, w) = -\dfrac{(\mu - r) h(t) w^{-\gamma}}{\sigma^2 w \cdot (-\gamma) h(t) w^{-\gamma - 1}} = \boxed{\dfrac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}} \equiv \pi^*_M.$$

**This is the Merton ratio.** It is **constant** — independent of $t$, $w$, and even $h(t)$. This is the single most famous formula in continuous-time portfolio theory.

**Optimal consumption.** $c^* = (v_w)^{-1/\gamma} = (h(t) w^{-\gamma})^{-1/\gamma} = h(t)^{-1/\gamma} w$. So
$$\boxed{C^*_t = \kappa(t) W_t, \quad \kappa(t) := h(t)^{-1/\gamma}.}$$

Consumption is a **linear function of wealth** with time-varying propensity $\kappa(t)$.

### ODE for $h(t)$

Substitute the ansatz and optimal controls into the HJB:

$$h'(t) \cdot \dfrac{w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} + \left\{ U(c^*) - \rho \dfrac{h w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} + [(r + \pi^*(\mu-r)) w - c^*] h w^{-\gamma} + \tfrac{1}{2} (\pi^*)^2 \sigma^2 w^2 (-\gamma) h w^{-\gamma - 1} \right\} = 0.$$

Compute each term using $c^* = h^{-1/\gamma} w$:

- $U(c^*) = \dfrac{(h^{-1/\gamma} w)^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} = \dfrac{h^{-(1-\gamma)/\gamma} w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} = \dfrac{h^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}$. (Note: $h^{-(1-\gamma)/\gamma} = h^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}$.)
- $[(r + \pi^* (\mu-r)) w - c^*] h w^{-\gamma} = [r + \pi^* (\mu - r)] h w^{1-\gamma} - h \cdot h^{-1/\gamma} w^{1-\gamma} = [r + \pi^*(\mu-r)] h w^{1-\gamma} - h^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} w^{1-\gamma}$.
- $\tfrac{1}{2} (\pi^*)^2 \sigma^2 w^2 (-\gamma) h w^{-\gamma-1} = -\tfrac{\gamma}{2} (\pi^*)^2 \sigma^2 h w^{1-\gamma}$.

Note $\pi^*(\mu - r) = (\mu-r)^2/(\gamma \sigma^2)$ and $(\pi^*)^2 \sigma^2 = (\mu-r)^2/(\gamma^2 \sigma^2)$. So
$$r + \pi^*(\mu - r) - \tfrac{\gamma}{2} (\pi^*)^2 \sigma^2 = r + \dfrac{(\mu-r)^2}{\gamma \sigma^2} - \dfrac{\gamma}{2} \cdot \dfrac{(\mu-r)^2}{\gamma^2 \sigma^2} = r + \dfrac{(\mu-r)^2}{\gamma \sigma^2} \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = r + \dfrac{(\mu-r)^2}{2 \gamma \sigma^2}.$$

Define the **effective return**
$$\boxed{\beta := r + \dfrac{(\mu - r)^2}{2 \gamma \sigma^2}}$$
which equals $r +$ half the squared **Sharpe ratio** divided by $\gamma$. (With Sharpe $S = (\mu - r)/\sigma$, we have $\beta = r + S^2/(2\gamma)$.)

The HJB reduces to:
$$\dfrac{h'(t)}{1-\gamma} w^{1-\gamma} + \dfrac{h^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}}{1-\gamma} w^{1-\gamma} - \dfrac{\rho h}{1-\gamma} w^{1-\gamma} + \beta h w^{1-\gamma} - h^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} w^{1-\gamma} = 0.$$

Dividing by $w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$:
$$h'(t) + h^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} - \rho h + \beta (1-\gamma) h - (1-\gamma) h^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} = 0$$
$$h'(t) + [\beta (1-\gamma) - \rho] h + \gamma h^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} = 0.$$

Define $A := \rho - \beta(1 - \gamma)/\gamma \cdot \gamma = \rho - \beta(1-\gamma)$... let me re-simplify more carefully. Factor the constant:

$$h'(t) + \gamma h^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} - [\rho - \beta(1-\gamma)] h = 0.$$

Write $K := [\rho - \beta(1-\gamma)]/\gamma$ so that

$$\boxed{h'(t) = K \gamma h(t) - \gamma h(t)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}, \qquad h(T) = \varepsilon.}$$

### Solving the ODE

This is a **Bernoulli-type** ODE. Substitute $g(t) = h(t)^{1/\gamma}$, so $h = g^\gamma$, $h^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma} = g^{\gamma-1}$, and $h' = \gamma g^{\gamma-1} g'$. The ODE becomes:
$$\gamma g^{\gamma - 1} g' = K \gamma g^\gamma - \gamma g^{\gamma - 1}$$
$$g^{\gamma - 1} g' = K g^\gamma - g^{\gamma - 1}$$
$$g' = K g - 1.$$

This is a **linear first-order ODE** with constant coefficients! General solution:
$$g(t) = \dfrac{1}{K} + A e^{K t}.$$

Terminal condition: $g(T) = h(T)^{1/\gamma} = \varepsilon^{1/\gamma}$. So
$$A = \left( \varepsilon^{1/\gamma} - \dfrac{1}{K} \right) e^{-K T}.$$

Therefore
$$g(t) = \dfrac{1}{K} \left(1 - e^{-K(T-t)}\right) + \varepsilon^{1/\gamma} e^{-K(T-t)},$$
and $h(t) = g(t)^\gamma$.

**Special case: no bequest ($\varepsilon = 0$).** Then
$$g(t) = \dfrac{1 - e^{-K(T-t)}}{K}, \qquad h(t) = \left( \dfrac{1 - e^{-K(T-t)}}{K} \right)^\gamma.$$

Substituting $K = [\rho - \beta(1-\gamma)]/\gamma = [\rho - (1-\gamma)(r + (\mu-r)^2/(2\gamma\sigma^2))]/\gamma$.

**Consumption rate.** $\kappa(t) = h(t)^{-1/\gamma} = 1/g(t)$. So

$$\boxed{\kappa(t) = \dfrac{K}{1 - e^{-K(T-t)} + K \varepsilon^{1/\gamma} e^{-K(T-t)}}.}$$

With $\varepsilon = 0$: $\kappa(t) = K/(1 - e^{-K(T-t)})$. As $t \to T$ we get $\kappa(t) \to \infty$ (you consume at an infinite rate at the horizon because there's no bequest and no reason to save).

## 4.5.3 The Merton Ratio: Interpretation

$$\pi^*_M = \dfrac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}.$$

### Interpretations

1. **Sharpe scaled by risk tolerance.** Writing $\pi^* = (\mu-r)/\sigma \cdot 1/(\gamma \sigma) = S/(\gamma \sigma)$ where $S = (\mu-r)/\sigma$ is the Sharpe ratio, the fraction in the risky asset is Sharpe × inverse vol, scaled by risk tolerance $1/\gamma$.

2. **Marginal Sharpe = marginal risk.** At the optimum, an additional unit of risk (one more percentage of portfolio in stock) delivers expected return $(\mu - r) \pi^*$ plus variance $\sigma^2 (\pi^*)^2$; the investor trades off these on the margin by their risk aversion $\gamma$.

3. **No wealth dependence.** $\pi^*$ does not depend on $W_t$. A poor and a rich CRRA investor with the same $\gamma$ hold the same **fraction** in the risky asset; the rich investor simply owns more dollars of stock. This is the famous "scale invariance" of CRRA.

4. **No time dependence.** $\pi^*$ does not depend on $t$ or on the remaining horizon $T - t$. This is the **myopic portfolio** result: in a constant-opportunity-set world (constant $\mu, \sigma, r$), horizon doesn't matter for portfolio allocation. (It *does* matter for consumption; $\kappa(t)$ depends on $T - t$.)

5. **Stocks "look better" than their Sharpe suggests.** Notice the denominator has $\sigma^2$, not $\sigma$. So doubling volatility with the same Sharpe ratio does not halve the allocation — it quarters it. Volatility is doubly punitive.

### Numbers

Typical long-run equity parameters: $\mu - r \approx 6\%$, $\sigma \approx 16\%$, giving Sharpe $\approx 0.375$. Merton ratios:

| $\gamma$ | $\pi^* = 0.06 / (\gamma \cdot 0.0256)$ | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 (risk-seeking) | 4.69 | Lever 369% |
| 1 (log, Kelly) | 2.34 | Lever 134% |
| 2 (moderate) | 1.17 | Slight leverage |
| 4 (cautious) | 0.59 | 59% stock, 41% cash |
| 10 (very risk averse) | 0.23 | 23% stock |

Empirical surveys put reasonable $\gamma$ in the range 2-5 for the median investor, matching the observed 40%-70% equity allocations of balanced funds.

### The "myopic + hedging" decomposition (preview)

In general (time-varying $\mu, \sigma, r$) the optimal portfolio decomposes as
$$\pi^* = \underbrace{\dfrac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}}_{\text{myopic}} + \underbrace{\text{hedging demand}}_{\text{intertemporal}}.$$

The myopic term is the Merton ratio pretending the future opportunity set is frozen at the current state. The hedging term is the adjustment for how the opportunity set evolves — this is zero in our constant-coefficient case but arises as soon as $\mu$ or $\sigma$ depend on state variables (Section 4.5.7).

## 4.5.4 The Optimal Consumption Rule

### Closed form

$$C^*_t = \kappa(t) W_t, \quad \kappa(t) = \dfrac{K}{1 - e^{-K(T-t)}} \text{ (with } \varepsilon = 0 \text{)}$$

where $K = [\rho - (1-\gamma) \beta]/\gamma$ and $\beta = r + (\mu-r)^2/(2\gamma \sigma^2)$.

**Interpretations.**

1. **Linear in wealth.** Same reason as portfolio: CRRA homogeneity implies consumption is a constant (time-varying) fraction of wealth.

2. **$K$ has an economic meaning.** Rewrite
$$K = \dfrac{\rho}{\gamma} + \dfrac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma} \beta = \dfrac{\rho}{\gamma} + \dfrac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma} \left[ r + \dfrac{(\mu - r)^2}{2 \gamma \sigma^2} \right].$$
For log utility ($\gamma = 1$): $K = \rho$ — subjective discount rate. Consumption propensity is $\kappa(t) = \rho/(1 - e^{-\rho(T-t)})$.

3. **Infinite-horizon limit.** As $T \to \infty$, if $K > 0$ then $\kappa(t) \to K$, a constant propensity to consume from wealth. The condition $K > 0$ is the "transversality condition" ensuring the value function is finite; see Section 4.5.6.

4. **Wealth process under optimal control.**
$$dW_t^* = [r + \pi^* (\mu - r) - \kappa(t)] W_t \, dt + \pi^* \sigma W_t \, dB_t.$$
This is **geometric Brownian motion with time-varying drift**. In the infinite-horizon case with constant $\kappa = K$:
$$dW_t = (\beta - K) W_t \, dt + \pi^* \sigma W_t \, dB_t.$$
The drift $\beta - K$ equals $(\beta - K) = \beta - \rho/\gamma - (\gamma-1)\beta/\gamma = \beta/\gamma - \rho/\gamma = (\beta - \rho)/\gamma$. So wealth grows (in expectation) at rate $(\beta - \rho)/\gamma$.

### Worked example: log utility

For $\gamma = 1$, $U(c) = \ln c$, the ansatz becomes $v(t, w) = h(t) \ln w + H(t)$ for additional function $H(t)$. (The power-ansatz limit as $\gamma \to 1$ produces a log in $w$.) One finds:
- $\pi^* = (\mu - r)/\sigma^2$ — the **Kelly criterion**.
- $\kappa(t) = \rho/(1 - e^{-\rho(T-t)})$, tending to $\rho$ as $T \to \infty$.

The log investor is "Kelly-optimal": they maximize the expected log of terminal wealth, equivalent to maximizing the geometric growth rate.

## 4.5.5 CARA Utility and the HARA Family

### CARA setup

$$U(c) = -\dfrac{1}{\alpha} e^{-\alpha c}, \quad B(w) = -\dfrac{1}{\alpha} e^{-\alpha w}.$$

Under CARA, the *dollar* allocation to stocks (not fractional) is what matters. Denote by $A_t := \pi_t W_t$ the dollar amount in stocks. The wealth dynamics become:
$$dW_t = [r W_t + A_t (\mu - r) - C_t] \, dt + A_t \sigma \, dB_t,$$
which is **linear in $A_t$** (since wealth enters only through $r W_t$, and $A_t$ is the control variable, not $\pi_t$).

### CARA ansatz and solution

Try $v(t, w) = -\dfrac{1}{\alpha} e^{-\alpha w h(t)}$ for some $h(t)$. One finds (exercise) that the ansatz works with:

$$h(t) = \dfrac{\alpha r}{1 - e^{-r(T-t)}}, \quad A^*_t = \dfrac{\mu - r}{\alpha \sigma^2 h(t)} = \dfrac{\mu - r}{\alpha \sigma^2} \cdot \dfrac{1 - e^{-r(T-t)}}{\alpha r}... \text{ (hmm let me redo)}$$

Actually the standard CARA-HJB solution (with constant interest rate $r$ and pure terminal-wealth objective, no running consumption) has:
$$v(t, w) = -\dfrac{1}{\alpha e^{r(T-t)}} \exp\left( -\alpha w e^{r(T-t)} - \dfrac{(\mu-r)^2 (T-t)}{2 \sigma^2} \right),$$
and the optimal dollar allocation is
$$A^*_t = \dfrac{\mu - r}{\alpha \sigma^2} \cdot e^{-r(T-t)}.$$

**Key contrast with CRRA:**
- CRRA: fraction $\pi^*$ is constant, dollar amount scales with wealth.
- CARA: dollar amount $A^*$ is (approximately) constant (with $r = 0$), fraction shrinks as wealth grows.

This is *empirically* rejected: people tend to hold roughly constant fractions of wealth in stocks over their lifetime (supporting CRRA), not constant dollar amounts.

### HARA family

HARA (Hyperbolic Absolute Risk Aversion) utility generalizes both:
$$U(c) = \dfrac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma} \left( \dfrac{a c}{\gamma - 1} + b \right)^\gamma$$
(various parameterizations exist; this is one). HARA is characterized by $-U'/U'' = $ linear in $c$. Setting parameters recovers CRRA, CARA, and quadratic utility. Merton's 1971 paper shows *all* HARA utilities admit closed-form solutions with linear-in-wealth consumption and linear-in-wealth dollar stock holdings. The HARA family is exactly the family for which Merton's problem has ansatz-based closed forms.

## 4.5.6 Infinite Horizon and Transversality

### Infinite-horizon problem

$$v(w) = \sup_{(\pi, C)} \mathbb{E}\left[ \int_0^\infty e^{-\rho t} U(C_t) \, dt \, \Big| \, W_0 = w \right].$$

By homogeneity, $v(w) = \kappa^{-\gamma} w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ for some constant $\kappa > 0$. The HJB reduces to an **algebraic** equation:
$$\sup_{\pi, c \ge 0} \left\{ U(c) - \rho v + [(r + \pi(\mu-r)) w - c] v' + \tfrac{1}{2} \pi^2 \sigma^2 w^2 v'' \right\} = 0.$$

With the ansatz and FOCs:
$$\pi^* = \dfrac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2}, \quad C^*_t = \kappa W_t,$$
where $\kappa$ is determined by
$$\kappa = \dfrac{\rho}{\gamma} + \dfrac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma} \left[ r + \dfrac{(\mu - r)^2}{2 \gamma \sigma^2} \right] = \dfrac{\rho - (1-\gamma) \beta}{\gamma} = K.$$

So in infinite horizon the consumption propensity is the constant $K$.

### Transversality condition

For the value function to be finite and the ansatz valid, we need $K > 0$:
$$\rho > (1 - \gamma) \beta = (1-\gamma)\left(r + \dfrac{(\mu-r)^2}{2\gamma\sigma^2}\right).$$

Interpretations:
- **If $\gamma > 1$** (risk averse): $(1-\gamma) \beta < 0$ so the condition reduces to $\rho > $ negative number, always satisfied.
- **If $\gamma < 1$** (risk tolerant): $\beta > 0$ so we need $\rho$ large enough; otherwise the investor wants to consume zero (save everything) and the wealth grows so fast the integral diverges.
- **$\gamma = 1$ (log):** $K = \rho$, condition is $\rho > 0$.

Transversality is the infinite-horizon analog of the "boundary conditions" of finite-horizon problems. Failure means the value function is infinite or the Bellman equation has no admissible solution.

## 4.5.7 Stochastic Investment Opportunity Set

### Motivation

The constant-coefficient Merton model is static: $\mu, \sigma, r$ never change. In reality, the equity premium is forecastable (by dividend yield, earnings yield, short rate, etc.), volatility is stochastic, and interest rates vary over time. Allowing these state variables introduces **intertemporal hedging demand**.

Merton (1973) "Intertemporal CAPM" (ICAPM) extended the model to a general setup:
$$dS_t = \mu(X_t) S_t \, dt + \sigma(X_t) S_t \, dB_t,$$
$$dX_t = \eta(X_t) \, dt + \nu(X_t) \, dZ_t,$$
where $X_t$ is a state variable (e.g., dividend yield, short rate) and $B_t, Z_t$ have correlation $\rho_{BZ}$.

### HJB with state variable

Value function $v(t, w, x)$. HJB:
$$\partial_t v + \sup_\pi \left\{ [r w + \pi w(\mu(x) - r)] v_w + \eta(x) v_x + \tfrac{1}{2} \pi^2 \sigma(x)^2 w^2 v_{ww} + \pi w \sigma(x) \nu(x) \rho_{BZ} v_{wx} + \tfrac{1}{2} \nu(x)^2 v_{xx} \right\} - \rho v + U(c^*) = 0.$$

FOC for $\pi$:
$$(\mu - r) w v_w + \pi w^2 \sigma^2 v_{ww} + w \sigma \nu \rho_{BZ} v_{wx} = 0$$
$$\boxed{\pi^*(t, w, x) = -\dfrac{(\mu(x) - r) v_w}{\sigma(x)^2 w v_{ww}} - \dfrac{\nu(x) \rho_{BZ}}{\sigma(x) w} \cdot \dfrac{v_{wx}}{v_{ww}}.}$$

**Two terms:**
1. **Myopic demand** $(\mu - r)/(\gamma \sigma^2)$-like — what you'd do in a frozen opportunity set.
2. **Hedging demand** $-\nu \rho_{BZ} v_{wx} / (\sigma w v_{ww})$ — adjustment for how the opportunity set evolves.

The hedging demand is positive (more stock) if the state variable $X$ and returns are negatively correlated with wealth utility ($v_{wx}$ of right sign): you want to hold extra stock because it hedges adverse shifts in the investment opportunity set.

### Kim-Omberg model

Kim and Omberg (1996) solved a model with:
- Mean-reverting equity premium: $dX_t = -\lambda(X_t - \bar X) dt + \nu \, dZ_t$ where $X_t = \mu - r$ (time-varying risk premium).
- Constant volatility $\sigma$.

They find a **closed-form** value function in the form $v(t, w, x) = h_1(t, x) w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ with $h_1$ satisfying a linear PDE solvable in closed form via matrix exponential. The optimal portfolio is:
$$\pi^*(t, w, x) = \dfrac{x}{\gamma \sigma^2} + \text{hedging term}(t, x).$$

The hedging term is typically positive for $\gamma > 1$ (investor goes long extra equity to hedge against lower future premia).

### Wachter model

Wachter (2002) solved a similar model with complete markets (returns perfectly correlated with the state variable $\rho_{BZ} = -1$, i.e., $X$ is a function of past returns). She obtains an explicit solution where:
$$\pi^*(t, w, x) = \dfrac{x}{\gamma \sigma^2} + \text{positive hedge if } \gamma > 1.$$

Intuition: with negative correlation, low-return periods coincide with high-future-premium states, so stocks partially hedge against their own bad times, increasing optimal equity.

## 4.5.8 Multiple Risky Assets and the Mutual Fund Theorem

### Setup

$n$ risky assets $S^1, \ldots, S^n$ with returns
$$dS^i_t / S^i_t = \mu_i \, dt + \sum_{j=1}^d \sigma_{ij} \, dB^j_t.$$

In vector form: $d\vec S_t / \vec S_t = \vec \mu \, dt + \Sigma \, d\vec B_t$, where $\Sigma$ is the diffusion matrix ($n \times d$), and $\vec B$ is $d$-dimensional Brownian motion. Covariance matrix $\Omega := \Sigma \Sigma^\top$.

Control: portfolio weight vector $\vec \pi_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$, with $\sum \pi_i = 1 - \pi_0$ and $\pi_0 = 1 - \vec 1^\top \vec \pi$ the cash weight.

Wealth dynamics:
$$dW_t = W_t \left[ r + \vec \pi^\top (\vec \mu - r \vec 1) \right] dt - C_t \, dt + W_t \vec \pi^\top \Sigma \, d\vec B_t.$$

### HJB with $n$ assets

$$\partial_t v + \sup_{\vec \pi} \left\{ -\rho v + U(c^*) + [r w + w \vec \pi^\top (\vec \mu - r \vec 1) - c^*] v_w + \tfrac{1}{2} w^2 \vec \pi^\top \Omega \vec \pi \, v_{ww} \right\} = 0.$$

FOC for $\vec \pi$:
$$w (\vec \mu - r \vec 1) v_w + w^2 \Omega \vec \pi \, v_{ww} = 0$$
$$\boxed{\vec \pi^* = -\dfrac{v_w}{w v_{ww}} \Omega^{-1} (\vec \mu - r \vec 1).}$$

For CRRA with ansatz $v = h w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$, we get $-v_w/(w v_{ww}) = 1/\gamma$, so
$$\vec \pi^* = \dfrac{1}{\gamma} \Omega^{-1} (\vec \mu - r \vec 1).$$

### Mutual fund theorem

All investors (with CRRA, varying $\gamma$) hold the same **portfolio direction** $\Omega^{-1} (\vec \mu - r \vec 1)$ — scaled by $1/\gamma$. This means they all hold the same *fund* of risky assets, just with different proportions of cash vs fund.

This is the continuous-time analog of the static **two-fund separation theorem**: every risk-averse investor holds (cash) + (tangency portfolio), with the mix depending on risk aversion.

In the static Markowitz world, $\Omega^{-1}(\vec \mu - r \vec 1)$ is the direction of the **tangency portfolio** — the portfolio with maximum Sharpe ratio. So the Merton investor holds a scaled tangency portfolio plus cash.

### Three-fund and beyond

With a stochastic opportunity set, the hedging demand produces additional hedging portfolios, giving **three-fund** or multi-fund separation theorems (Merton 1973).

## 4.5.9 Labor Income and Human Capital

### Setup

Investor earns labor income at rate $Y_t$ which follows its own stochastic process:
$$dY_t = \mu_Y Y_t \, dt + \sigma_Y Y_t \, dB^Y_t.$$

Wealth equation becomes:
$$dW_t = [rW + \pi W (\mu - r) + Y_t - C_t] \, dt + \pi W \sigma \, dB_t.$$

### Human capital as a "bond"

Define **human capital** $H_t := \mathbb{E}[\int_t^T e^{-r(s-t)} Y_s \, ds | \mathcal{F}_t]$ — the present value of future labor income. Total "wealth" is $W_t + H_t$.

If labor income is deterministic (or riskless in present-value terms), $H_t$ is bond-like. The investor should allocate total wealth (financial + human) in the Merton ratio. Since human capital is already bond-like, the **financial portfolio overweights stocks** to compensate:
$$\dfrac{\pi^* W}{W + H} = \dfrac{\mu - r}{\gamma \sigma^2} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \pi^* = \dfrac{(\mu - r)}{\gamma \sigma^2} \cdot \dfrac{W + H}{W}.$$

This justifies the **life-cycle glide-path**: young workers have high $H/W$ so should hold near-100% stocks; retirees have $H \approx 0$ so hold the static Merton fraction; the optimal equity allocation declines with age. Target-date funds are built on exactly this logic.

### Risky labor income

If labor income is partially correlated with equity ($\rho$ between $B^Y$ and $B$), then human capital is not purely bond-like, and the hedging demand adjusts. Full solution in Cocco-Gomes-Maenhout (2005) and Campbell-Viceira (2002); the numerical life-cycle literature uses these formulas in DC pension design.

## 4.5.10 Stochastic Volatility and Hedging Demand

### Heston-style setup

$$dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sqrt{V_t} S_t \, dB^S_t,$$
$$dV_t = \kappa(\theta - V_t) \, dt + \xi \sqrt{V_t} \, dB^V_t,$$
with correlation $\rho$ between $B^S$ and $B^V$ (typically $\rho < 0$, the leverage effect).

### HJB

Value function $v(t, w, V)$. FOC for $\pi$:
$$(\mu - r) w v_w + \pi V w^2 v_{ww} + \xi V w \rho v_{wV} = 0$$
$$\boxed{\pi^* = \dfrac{\mu - r}{V} \cdot \dfrac{-v_w}{w v_{ww}} - \dfrac{\xi \rho}{w V^{1/2} \cdot V^{1/2}/V} \cdot \dfrac{v_{wV}}{v_{ww}} = \dfrac{\mu - r}{\gamma V} + \dfrac{\xi \rho}{V} \cdot \dfrac{(1-\gamma)}{\gamma} \dfrac{h_V(t,V)}{h(t,V)},}$$
where the second equality uses the CRRA ansatz $v = h(t, V) w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$.

### Liu's semi-closed solution

Liu (2007) and others show that under CRRA Heston, $h(t, V)$ has the form $\exp(A(t) + B(t) V)$ (affine in $V$) where $A, B$ solve a Riccati ODE system. This gives **semi-closed** solutions with the hedging demand explicitly:
$$\pi^*_H = \pi^*_{myopic} + \dfrac{(1-\gamma) \xi \rho B(t)}{\gamma}.$$

For $\gamma > 1$, $\rho < 0$, and $B(t) > 0$: hedging demand is **negative**, i.e., reduce equity in response to stochastic vol. This matches intuition: leverage effect means stocks fall when vol rises, so stocks don't hedge volatility shocks and there's no positive hedging demand.

### Fleming-Hernández-Hernández

For more general SV (not just Heston), Fleming-Hernández-Hernández (2005) use viscosity-solution techniques to characterize $v$ even without closed forms, and show that under reasonable regularity $v$ is $C^{1,2,2}$ in $(t, w, V)$ and the optimal policy is smooth.

## 4.5.11 Python: Solving Merton's Problem

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

# -----------------------------
# Parameters
# -----------------------------
mu    = 0.08
sigma = 0.20
r     = 0.02
gamma = 3.0         # CRRA coefficient
rho   = 0.04        # subjective discount
T     = 30.0        # horizon (years)
epsilon = 0.0       # bequest weight (0 = pure consumption)

# Merton quantities
pi_star = (mu - r) / (gamma * sigma**2)
beta    = r + (mu - r)**2 / (2 * gamma * sigma**2)
K       = (rho - (1 - gamma) * beta) / gamma

print(f"Sharpe ratio      : {(mu-r)/sigma:.4f}")
print(f"Merton ratio pi*  : {pi_star:.4f}")
print(f"Effective beta    : {beta:.4f}")
print(f"Consumption K     : {K:.4f}")

# -----------------------------
# Solve ODE for h(t)
# -----------------------------
# h'(t) = K * gamma * h(t) - gamma * h(t)^((gamma-1)/gamma)
# h(T) = epsilon
def h_rhs(t, h):
    return K * gamma * h - gamma * h**((gamma - 1) / gamma)

# Solve backwards from T to 0
sol = solve_ivp(lambda t, h: -h_rhs(T - t, h), [0, T],
                [epsilon if epsilon > 0 else 1e-10],
                dense_output=True, max_step=0.01)
t_grid = np.linspace(0, T, 200)
h_grid = sol.sol(T - t_grid).flatten()
kappa_grid = h_grid**(-1/gamma)

# Compare with closed form for epsilon=0
g_closed = (1 - np.exp(-K * (T - t_grid))) / K
h_closed = g_closed**gamma
kappa_closed = K / (1 - np.exp(-K * (T - t_grid)))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4))
ax[0].plot(t_grid, kappa_grid,  label='ODE numeric')
ax[0].plot(t_grid, kappa_closed, '--', label='closed form')
ax[0].set_xlabel('t'); ax[0].set_ylabel(r'$\kappa(t)$ consumption propensity')
ax[0].set_title('Consumption rate')
ax[0].legend(); ax[0].grid(alpha=0.3)

# -----------------------------
# Monte Carlo verification of v(0, w) = h(0) * w^(1-gamma)/(1-gamma)
# -----------------------------
N   = 50_000
dt  = 0.01
n   = int(T / dt)
w0  = 1.0

W  = np.full(N, w0)
J  = np.zeros(N)
ts = 0.0
for step in range(n):
    kappa_t = np.interp(ts, t_grid, kappa_grid)
    C_t = kappa_t * W
    J += np.exp(-rho * ts) * (C_t**(1-gamma) / (1-gamma)) * dt
    dB = np.sqrt(dt) * np.random.randn(N)
    W  = W + (r + pi_star * (mu - r) - kappa_t) * W * dt + pi_star * sigma * W * dB
    W  = np.maximum(W, 1e-10)
    ts += dt

V_MC   = J.mean()
V_true = h_grid[0] * w0**(1 - gamma) / (1 - gamma)
print(f"MC value estimate    : {V_MC:.5f} (SE {J.std()/np.sqrt(N):.5f})")
print(f"Analytic value v(0,1): {V_true:.5f}")

ax[1].hist(J, bins=60, density=True, alpha=0.6)
ax[1].axvline(V_true, color='red', label=f'analytic = {V_true:.4f}')
ax[1].set_xlabel('realized lifetime utility')
ax[1].legend(); ax[1].grid(alpha=0.3)
ax[1].set_title('Monte Carlo check')
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

Expected output: MC estimate within 2-3 standard errors of the analytic value, and $\kappa(t)$ curves coincide.

## 4.5.12 [QUANT APPLICATION] Where the Merton Framework Shows Up

1. **Life-cycle robo-advisers (Betterment, Wealthfront, Vanguard).** Target-date funds and age-based glide-paths use the human-capital extension of Merton. Young savers' labor income dominates financial wealth → high $\pi^*$. Near retirement, human capital depletes → standard Merton ratio with moderate equity.

2. **Endowment management.** Yale's David Swensen model (in his book *Pioneering Portfolio Management*) is essentially a long-horizon Merton portfolio generalized to multiple asset classes with different hedging properties. The infinite-horizon Merton ansatz matches the perpetual nature of university endowments.

3. **Pension fund ALM.** Defined-benefit pension funds solve a Merton-style problem with liabilities as negative wealth. The "liability-driven investment" (LDI) paradigm adds hedging demand against interest-rate shocks.

4. **Separation of beta and alpha.** The mutual-fund theorem says you can decompose any portfolio into (tangency) × scalar + cash. In practice: index fund (beta) + cash (risk-free) + idiosyncratic alpha bets layered on. This "core-satellite" structure is a direct descendant of Merton.

5. **Intertemporal CAPM asset pricing.** Merton's (1973) generalization gave birth to ICAPM, which says risk premia are determined by covariances with both market returns AND state variables forecasting future returns. Leads to Fama-French factors (SMB, HML) being interpretable as hedging-portfolios against adverse shifts.

6. **Execution algorithms.** Almgren-Chriss optimal execution is a dual of Merton: the trader solves a Merton problem for "liquidating a position optimally" where risk aversion plays the role of implementation-shortfall tolerance. See Module 4.6 for LQG framing.

7. **Habit-formation utility.** Campbell-Cochrane (1999) replaced CRRA with $U = ((C - X)^{1-\gamma})/(1-\gamma)$ where $X$ is an external habit. This explains equity premium puzzle. The HJB still has an ansatz solution but with state variable $X$.

8. **Ambiguity and robust portfolio choice.** Hansen-Sargent-style robust control replaces $\sup$ with $\inf$-$\sup$: investor is uncertain about the true model. Leads to "shrink the Merton ratio" by a factor depending on ambiguity aversion. Used in institutional asset-allocation models worried about estimation error in $\mu$.

9. **Option-based risk management.** Leland's (1980) option strategies arise as solutions to a Merton problem with kinked utility (loss aversion). Solution: hold equity plus put options. Product: principal-protected notes, variable annuities with GMWB/GMDB riders.

10. **Climate-aware portfolio theory.** Stanford/IIF/CFA research on "climate stress testing" extends Merton with state variables for carbon prices, physical risk. Hedging demand against climate transition shocks → tilt toward green assets.

## 4.5.13 Summary

Take-aways from this module:

- **Merton ratio** $\pi^* = (\mu - r)/(\gamma \sigma^2)$ is constant in $(t, w)$ and is the "DNA" of continuous-time portfolio theory.
- **Consumption rate** $C^*_t = \kappa(t) W_t$ is linear in wealth with $\kappa(t)$ closed-form in terms of $K$.
- **Transversality condition** $K > 0$ is needed for finite value in infinite horizon.
- **HARA utilities** are exactly the family admitting ansatz-based closed-form solutions; CRRA and CARA are the two key cases.
- **Stochastic opportunity set** adds **hedging demand** beyond the myopic ratio — positive or negative depending on correlations.
- **Multiple assets** give the **mutual fund theorem**: everyone holds cash + (tangency portfolio).
- **Labor income** converts to human capital, with financial portfolio overweighting equities for young workers (glide-path).
- **Stochastic vol** introduces hedging demand typically negative under leverage effect.
