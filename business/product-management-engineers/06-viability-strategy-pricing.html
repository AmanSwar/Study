Business Viability for a Developer Product: Stakeholders, Strategy, Pricing and Positioning

Cagan says product managers too often under-estimate or avoid business viability1, and for a runtime or SDK it decides whether the product survives as a business. This page covers stakeholder constraints, deciding under disagreement, four definitions of strategy, commoditizing complements, the pricing mechanics of PostHog and Stripe, and positioning. Afterwards you can name the viability questions a developer product must answer, derive which price metric fits a cost structure, read PostHog's rules as one company's policy and not a consensus, and tell evidence from inference on this page.

1. Viability: the risk with the least engineering in it

1.1 What the source claims, and who inherits the risk

Cagan's claim is that business-viability risks "are too often under-appreciated and under-estimated (or simply avoided) by the product manager"1. His list is open-ended: fit with the go-to-market or sales channel, constraints in partner contracts and legal compliance, whether "we can afford to cost-effectively acquire customers", whether "we can effectively monetize the product", and consistency with the brand promise, "as just a few examples". He assigns the risk to the product manager, who is "responsible for the value and viability risks, and overall accountable for the product's outcomes"1. Two limits apply. The claim is one practitioner's assertion, with no count of how often teams skip viability. And it is about product managers: nothing fetched for this track measures what engineers weigh.

What follows for an engineer depends on who holds the risk when the team has no product manager, and PostHog is the fetched case. Its handbook says that in early-stage products "a team usually doesn't have a PM yet" and "product engineers own all product decisions", that "it's typically the team lead's job to decide" three things, "What to build", "When to release the first version" and "How to charge for it", and that a PM is usually added "after a product has launched and started generating revenue"2. Under that model, pricing is a decision an engineer lead makes by default. Page 5 compares the role models; the narrower point here is that under at least one fetched model viability is not something an engineer can leave to someone else.

The engineer-side guidance is a prompt and not a method. Orosz's advice to engineers on user-facing product teams begins: "Understand how and why your company is successful. What is the business model? How is money made? What parts are most profitable, what parts of the company are expanding the most?"3. How a team then tests a viability hypothesis is described by SVPG's stakeholder article: prototypes shown to the people who own business constraints are "how your product partners demonstrate they understand your business constraints", and "more generally, this is how the product team assesses whether the proposed solution would be viable for the business"4. Section 2 takes that exchange apart.

1.3 Letting the market answer: precedent and revealed prices

PostHog's engineering handbook says that before product-market fit "We only build things that already have successful competitors with real revenue", and draws the implication that "'just build it' works disproportionately well because other people have already figured out that new product X has product market fit"6. Its pricing rule for an established market has the same structure: "roughly match the pricing of the cheapest big competitor", counting only a competitor "making actual revenue at significant scale"7. Each rule swaps a forecast for an observed fact. The forecast being avoided is a hard one. Spolsky argues in a 2004 essay that you cannot learn the demand curve by asking, because the answers swing from day to day, and that "the only way to determine how much someone will pay for something is to put it up for sale, and see how many people actually buy it"; even then, he says, you would need "something like 1,000,000 customers" before price experiments mean anything8. A competitor earning real revenue at scale has run that experiment already. That is the writer's reading of why the two PostHog rules are the same rule; PostHog states neither reason.

The rule needs precedent. For "innovative products where there is no established market", PostHog says it is "also the innovator on price", should not be "afraid to experiment", and should change pricing early if "the usage-based unit we picked is unappealing to customers"7. Whether a given runtime has such precedent is a market fact no fetched source supplies. The same handbook gives one cheap interview probe aimed at willingness to pay: it says to "look for evidence that users have actually done anything about the problems they say they have", and suggests asking "Has your company spent money trying to solve this before?"6.

1.4 Price decides the channel

Go-to-market fit is not separable from price. Spolsky's 2004 argument is that software is priced "free, cheap, and dear". Cheap is "$10 – $1000, sold to a very large number of people at a low price without a salesforce"; dear is "$75,000 – $1,000,000, sold to a handful of rich big companies using a team of slick salespeople". Nothing is priced between, he says, because above $1,000 "you need to get serious corporate signoffs", and "the cost of making one successful sale is going to average about $50,000"8. That figure is his estimate in 2004 dollars with no data behind it, so treat it as an illustration of the mechanism and not as a number to plan with.

The mechanism is arithmetic. A sales-led channel carries a fixed cost per won deal, \( C_s \). With deal value \( D \) and the fraction \( m \) of it left after delivery cost, the channel pays only if

$$ m\,D \;\ge\; C_s \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D \;\ge\; D^{*} = \frac{C_s}{m}. $$

With his \( C_s = \$50{,}000 \), the lower bound is \( D^{*} = \$50{,}000 \) at \( m = 1 \). His own boundary of $75,000 implies \( m \approx 50{,}000 / 75{,}000 = 0.67 \); he does not state \( m \), so that is inferred. Below \( D^{*} \) the only viable channel is self-serve, which forces a price a buyer can approve without procurement. Stripe's 2026 guidance makes the same split from the buyer's side: if the buyer is "an individual developer or a team lead with a credit card, per-seat simplicity wins", while a platform-engineering team or vice president with a software budget "might reach for predictable annual spending and a clean contract structure", and "trying to serve both customers with the same model usually won't work"5. PostHog's price list carries both channels: a pay-as-you-go plan with a "$0/mo base price", and annual credit commitments discounted 20% from $25,000 of credit up to 40% at $500,000 to $999,999, with "Contact us" above $1,000,0009. The arithmetic shows the failure to avoid: a price chosen for self-serve adoption, sold into a channel that needs \( D^{*} \) per deal, loses money on every contract.

2. Stakeholders as suppliers of constraints

In SVPG's account, a stakeholder is anyone "responsible for some key aspect of your company's business", whether that is operations, profit and loss for a business unit, or a service group such as legal, finance or HR; "most importantly", it is someone who is "not directly part of the product organization, but are still reliant on that group to create technology solutions that support your business needs and constraints"4. What stakeholders supply is the other half of the problem. Constraints range "from go-to-market considerations, to industry regulations, to financials (both costs and monetization), to business partnerships", and the reason product work is hard is that "solutions must solve for both customers and also for the various dimensions of the business"4. The categories line up with Cagan's viability list in Figure 1.

2.1 The exchange, step by step

  1. Constraints shared. The stakeholder explains the business context: go-to-market, regulation, financials, partnerships. The article says product leaders "rely on you" for recommended reading, introductions and specific guidance4.
  2. Request reframed. Requests for features and projects, "no matter where they originate", become "problems to solve, along with a definition of success". The most useful thing to share is "the specific problem you need solved, who specifically you need that problem solved for, and how you will measure success"4.
  3. Access granted. Teams "require direct, unencumbered access to customers, users and data"; where privacy or governance limit access, "this can all be accomplished with tooling and authorization controls"4.
  4. Prototype reviewed. Before anything is built, the stakeholder sees "very quick to create simulations" and gives feedback "while changes are still easy and inexpensive to make"; the same prototypes go to users for usability and value and to engineers for feasibility4.
  5. Built and instrumented. Engineers build "a production-quality solution, which includes instrumenting the product" so that stakeholders and team "can immediately see whether the new offering is generating the necessary business outcomes"4.
  6. Iterate until the result is met. If outcomes fall short, "the product team will immediately investigate the reasons, and proceed to iterate until the desired business results are achieved"4.

Three details matter for an engineer. Step 5 is engineering work: evidence that viability held after launch exists only if someone logged it (page 4 covers what to measure). The article exempts "keeping the lights on", meaning essential reporting, compliance changes and critical fixes, from reframing and discovery, and says that if that load "climbs too high" the company must decide whether it can also move the business forward4. And dates are where the old model returns: a "high-integrity commitment" is "a date you can count on, but it requires some product discovery work", and since "these come at a cost" it "should be used sparingly"4; page 3 (Section 8.4) covers the mechanism.

3. When people still disagree: who ends the argument

Section 2 describes how a constraint reaches a team. It does not say what happens when the people involved still disagree about the solution or the constraint. Four mechanisms in the fetched sources answer, and they answer differently: one ends the argument by commitment, one declines to end it by role, one assigns a single owner per decision, and one is the failure mode of the rest.

3.1 Four mechanisms

Amazon's 2016 shareholder letter has the sharpest statement of the first. It says to "never use a one-size-fits-all decision-making process", that "many decisions are reversible, two-way doors" suited to "a light-weight process", and that "most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had"10; page 4 (Section 8.1) turns that into an inequality. The conflict mechanism is the next paragraph: "If you have conviction on a particular direction even though there's no consensus, it's helpful to say, 'Look, I know we disagree on this but will you gamble with me on it? Disagree and commit?' By the time you're at this point, no one can know the answer for sure, and you'll probably get a quick yes." It runs both ways ("If you're the boss, you should do this too"): the example is Bezos committing to an Amazon Studios original he doubted because the team "wanted to go ahead", saving what he calls the slower cycle "if the team had actually had to convince me rather than simply get my commitment". He also says what it is not: not "these guys are wrong and missing the point, but this isn't worth me chasing", but "a genuine disagreement of opinion, a candid expression of my view, a chance for the team to weigh my view, and a quick, sincere commitment to go their way"10.

Eriksson's 2017 essay is the opposite pole on authority. He argues that "unless you're the founder and the product manager at the same time, you are not the CEO of anything", because product managers "don't have any direct authority over most of the things needed to make their products successful", and quotes Ken Norton: "You need to gain authority through your actions and your leadership skills, not your role"11. The failure he names is the manager who believes "their teams should just do what they're told", and he says such managers often produce "disastrous results", an assertion without examples or counts. He also does not let the absence of authority excuse the absence of accountability: "Just because you can pass the buck to someone higher up the chain of command, it doesn't mean you should"11.

PostHog's handbook supplies the third mechanism for exactly the decisions this page is about. It says that "when major calls (like positioning, pricing, or launch scope) need to be made, the PM and team lead collaborate closely to select an informed captain: One person who makes the decisions and sees them through. The other can provide feedback when requested, but focuses elsewhere"2. The fourth item is Bezos's warning about the thing PostHog also uses: "the process becomes the proxy for the result you want. You stop looking at outcomes and just make sure you're doing the process right", with the diagnostic question "do we own the process or does the process own us?"10. PostHog runs a yearly pricing evaluation that is "submit[ted] as an RFC for others to review"7; whether that process owns the team depends on whether outcomes are checked, and the role page does assign the PM to "track the impact of pricing changes on revenue, conversion, and churn to understand what worked and what didn't"2. That is the writer's reading of how the two documents fit; neither makes the connection.

3.2 Opinions can be committed to, facts cannot

None of the four is specific to a viability constraint, and one distinction is missing from all of them. A constraint is either a fact about the world, such as a licence clause or a compliance rule, or a judgment about the business, such as how much acquisition cost is tolerable. Disagree-and-commit settles disagreements of opinion, which is what Bezos's example is; it has no purchase on a clause that says what may be redistributed. In Figure 2 the prototype review is where a fact-type constraint shows up as a pass or fail and a judgment-type constraint becomes a decision someone can commit to. This split is the writer's reading, not a claim in any source.

3.3 What no fetched source supplies

The stakeholder article says product managers work with several stakeholders "so that they can come up with solutions that satisfy the various, sometimes conflicting, constraints", and stops4. It gives no method for two stakeholders whose constraints cannot both hold and none for an executive who demands a specific feature, and it defers to a book that was not fetched. The nearest mechanisms elsewhere on this track are page 4's guardrails, which turn a constraint into a pass-or-fail gate, and its exchange-rate derivation of an overall evaluation criterion, which puts two goals in one currency. Neither source presents either as a method for stakeholder conflict, so that pointer is the writer's. How to refuse an executive without losing the argument or the working relationship is not in the evidence this page rests on.

4. Four things called strategy

PostHog's handbook lists "Can't explain our company strategy" among the marks of a bad product engineer, and "Understand the company strategy, and prioritize based on this and what they believe users want" among the marks of a good one6. An engineer asked to do either meets a problem: the fetched sources use the word for four different objects. Rumelt means a particular structure of argument. Cagan's team-facing statement of direction fills the slot a strategy fills, without using the word. Kohavi, Tang and Xu mean something that can be written as a metric. Spolsky means a position among the products around yours. They are set side by side under "Where experts disagree".

4.1 Rumelt: coherent action backed by an argument

The text here comes from Rumelt's own June 2011 McKinsey Quarterly article, "adapted from his forthcoming book"12. The book itself, Good Strategy/Bad Strategy, was not read: the fetched catalog page gives only the publisher's description and a table of contents, which state the thesis, "Strategy should not be equated with ambition, leadership, vision or planning", and show chapters titled "Bad Strategy" (p. 32), "The Kernel of Good Strategy" (p. 77), "Sources of Power" (p. 95) and "Proximate Objectives" (p. 106), with page numbers from the Profile Books UK edition13. Treat anything about the book beyond that as book not fetched. The article's version of the thesis matches the blurb: "strategy is none of these. Rather, it is coherent action backed by an argument"12.

The kernel has three parts: "a diagnosis: an explanation of the nature of the challenge", which "simplifies the often overwhelming complexity of reality by identifying certain aspects of the situation as being the critical ones"; "a guiding policy: an overall approach chosen to cope with or overcome the obstacles identified in the diagnosis"; and "coherent actions: steps that are coordinated with one another to support the accomplishment of the guiding policy"12. His worked example is Nvidia's. Its first product, a PC add-in board for video, audio and 3-D graphics, "was a commercial failure"; a rival, 3Dfx, took the lead with gamers, and Intel was rumored to be preparing its own chip. The diagnosis was "We are losing the performance race". The policy, from CEO Jen-Hsun Huang's insight that releasing a chip every 6 months instead of "the industry-standard rate of every 18 months" would matter, was to "release a faster, better chip three times faster than the industry norm". The coherent actions were three development teams on overlapping schedules, "massive simulation and emulation facilities" to avoid delays in fabrication and driver development, and, over time, regaining control of driver development from the board makers12. Rumelt adds that good strategies are not permanent: Nvidia "has been challenged in the fast-growing mobile-graphics business, where cost is often more important than performance"12. For an engineer whose product runs on devices, that caveat is the useful sentence. A diagnosis is indexed to a market and a date, and the performance race of one decade is not the cost race of the next.

He condenses bad strategy to four hallmarks: failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, bad strategic objectives, and fluff12. Each has a case: International Harvester's 1979 plan, which never mentioned its "grossly inefficient production facilities"; a "20/20 plan" of 20% annual growth and a margin of 20% or higher, defended with "the will to win"; a city plan with "47 strategies and 178 action items", the 122nd being "create a strategic plan"; and "customer-centric intermediation", which meant being a bank12. Of the two roots he gives, "the inability to choose" connects to Section 3. At DEC in 1992, in Rumelt's simplified telling with three executives, each argued for a different strategy ("Boxes", "Solutions", "Chips"); a majority preferred Solutions to Boxes, Boxes to Chips and Chips to Solutions, so consensus had no solution, and the group issued a statement Rumelt calls "a political outcome reached by individuals who, forced to reach a consensus, could not agree on which interests and concepts to forego"12. He does not claim a captain or a commitment rule would have saved DEC; the case shows that consensus on a strategic choice can be impossible, which is what Section 3's mechanisms are for. The cases are the author's own, with no counts or controls.

4.2 Cagan: what the team is given in place of a roadmap

Cagan's 2015 article never uses the word "strategy"14. What it supplies is the context a team needs: "a deep understanding of where the company is heading" and knowledge of "how their particular team is supposed to contribute to the larger purpose". It offers two components: a product vision, "the holistic view of what the organization as a whole is trying to accomplish", and "specific, prioritized business objectives for each product team", managed by OKRs, "my current favorite"14. A roadmap, he says, serves two purposes, working "on the highest business value items first" and holding "date-based commitments", and an alternative has to meet both14. Calling vision plus objectives his answer to "what is the strategy" is the writer's mapping, made because the article fills the same slot.

Set beside Rumelt, the difference is where the work sits. Cagan's example objective, with a key result of "average on-boarding time less than 3 hours" against a current 30 days, is hypothetical; it names a target, and the article leaves the diagnosis of why onboarding takes 30 days and the policy for shortening it to the team, which is told to "figure out the best way"14. In Rumelt's terms the objective is neither good nor bad until someone has done the diagnosis. In Cagan's model the team does it; in Rumelt's the strategist does, and Rumelt's tests for bad objectives are the "blue sky" restatement of the desired state and the "dog's dinner of goals"12. The tension on vision is explicit: the blurb says strategy "should not be equated with" vision13, and Cagan puts vision at the head of what a team is given.

4.3 Kohavi, Tang and Xu: strategy as the choice of metric

The experimentation textbook's position is operational. "Having a strategy is critical for running experiments: the strategy is what drives the choice of OEC"; "by encapsulating strategy into an OEC, controlled experiments can provide a great feedback loop for the strategy", and the OEC is "the perfect mechanism to make the strategy explicit and to align what features ship with the strategy" (Ch. 1, pp. 21-22). Guardrail metrics state "what the organization is not willing to change, since a strategy also 'requires you to make tradeoffs in competing – to choose what not to do'", a line they quote from Porter15. The book also gives a test that a strategy can fail: one experiment that misses the OEC may indict only a tactic, but "if many tactics evaluated through controlled experiments fail, it may be time to think about Winston Churchill's saying: 'However beautiful the strategy, you should occasionally look at the results'" (p. 23). Their case is Bing's two-year social-media strategy, abandoned after "over $25 million" with "no significant impact to key metrics" (pp. 23-24)15; page 4 covers the sunk-cost argument. The book says nothing about where a strategy comes from, so it complements Rumelt without replacing him: it checks a strategy after someone has chosen one.

4.4 Spolsky: position

The fourth object is a position among substitutes and complements, argued in a 2002 essay16. Section 5 gives it a full treatment because it is the only one of the four with a mechanism that can be written as arithmetic.

5. Commoditize your complements

5.1 The argument, as written in 2002

Spolsky's "Strategy Letter V" starts from two definitions. "A substitute is another product you might buy if the first product is too expensive." "A complement is a product that you usually buy together with another product", with gas and cars, hardware and operating systems, and babysitters and dinners out as examples16. The claim follows: "All else being equal, demand for a product increases when the prices of its complements decrease". From it he derives a corporate rule: a company's "strategic interest is going to be to get the price of their complements as low as possible", the floor being "the commodity price", "the price that arises when you have a bunch of competitors offering indistinguishable goods". Hence "Smart companies try to commoditize their products' complements", and "if you can do this, demand for your product will increase and you will be able to charge more and make more"16. One refinement matters for anything free: economists "consider the total price, including some intangible things like the time it takes to set up, reeducate everyone, and convert existing processes", so a zero sticker price is not a zero price16.

The essay is a verbal argument. It gives no demand data and no elasticities, it is dated June 2002, and its author hedges the Netscape case himself: "My argument is even more shaky because it's pretty clear that Netscape in the days of Barksdale didn't exactly know what it was doing"16. Read it as a mechanism, not as a finding.

5.2 A derivation of the mechanism (writer's toy model)

The mechanism can be made exact under simple assumptions. Customers buy one unit each of product A and its complement B, and the number who buy depends on the total price: \( Q = 1000 - (p_A + p_B) \). The vendor of A has a constant marginal cost \( c_A = \$100 \) and chooses \( p_A \) to maximize \( \pi_A = (p_A - c_A)\,Q \), taking \( p_B \) as given. Setting the derivative to zero gives \( Q = p_A - c_A \), so

$$ p_A^{*} = \frac{1000 - p_B + c_A}{2}, \qquad Q^{*} = \frac{1000 - p_B - c_A}{2}, \qquad \pi_A^{*} = \left(\frac{900 - p_B}{2}\right)^{2}. $$

Each dollar taken off \( p_B \) raises A's best price by $0.50 and its volume by 0.5 customers. At \( p_B = \$600 \) the vendor of A prices at $250, sells 150 and earns $22,500. At \( p_B = \$300 \) it prices at $400, sells 300 and earns $90,000, four times as much. At \( p_B = 0 \) it prices at $550, sells 450 and earns $202,500. That is the sentence "demand for your product will increase and you will be able to charge more and make more" with numbers: price up, volume up, profit up. Two caveats belong to the model, not to Spolsky. The parameters are illustrative; nothing here is measured. And the strategy has a cost the model leaves out: from $600 to $300 the gain is $67,500, so spending to push B's price down pays only if it costs less than that. The vendor of B loses what A gains, which is why B's vendor resists.

5.4 Mapped onto an inference runtime

The mapping below is not in any source. It applies the essay's definitions to a runtime vendor and stops where the premises stop. A runtime and the models it executes are complements, since they are used together; two runtimes are substitutes. By the essay's logic the runtime's demand rises as the price of models falls and as device silicon becomes interchangeable, which is what a hardware-abstraction layer does. The same logic gives each complement's vendor, whether silicon or model provider, an interest in a cheap runtime, as it gave Sun and HP an interest in cheap windowing systems. The asymmetry favors the runtime only if switching away from it is costly for its users. Whether any of that holds depends on facts the sources do not contain: how large the switching costs of a runtime are, what silicon vendors charge for their own runtimes, and how elastic demand for on-device inference is.

6. PostHog's pricing rules as a mechanism

PostHog sells an open-source product and data tools platform, and publishes both its pricing principles and its price list79. That makes it the one fetched case where a developer-product company's pricing policy can be set beside the artifact it produced. Everything in this section is one company's stated policy, as of 2026-10-26. No page fetched gives revenue, margin, churn or conversion figures, so nothing here shows that the policy works; it shows what the policy is, whether the price list agrees with it, and what arithmetic follows from it.

6.1 The rules and the reason each one states

The handbook opens with five properties PostHog wants from its pricing: generous free allowances for hobbyists and pre-product-market-fit teams, the chance to try the product before paying, starting to pay "on their own, with few hurdles", paying "transparently" for the value received, and making it "a no-brainer to pick PostHog over other competitors"; its goals include keeping engineers "as close to our customers as possible" and tying "our success with that of our customers'"7. The runbook that follows is a list of rules. Table 5 states each with the reason the handbook gives and, in the last column, the assumption about cost or value that the rule needs. 6.4 A worked derivation: what the event brackets cost

Product analytics is billed per event in seven brackets: the first 1,000,000 free, then from $0.00005 per event up to 2,000,000 down to $0.000009 above 250,000,000, with brackets ending at 15, 50, 100 and 250 million9. The Product Analytics pricing page shows, for each bracket, an allocation, a price and a subtotal18, which means each event is charged at the price of the bracket it falls in: a graduated schedule in the sense Section 7.2 defines. With bracket bounds \( U_k \) and prices \( p_k \), the monthly bill for \( N \) events is

$$ \text{bill}(N) = \sum_{k} p_k \,\max\bigl(0,\ \min(N, U_k) - U_{k-1}\bigr), \qquad U_0 = 0. $$

For \( N = 10{,}000{,}000 \): \( 1{,}000{,}000 \times \$0 + 1{,}000{,}000 \times \$0.00005 + 8{,}000{,}000 \times \$0.0000343 = \$50.00 + \$274.40 = \$324.40 \). Table 7 gives the bill, the average price per million events and the marginal price at ten volumes, computed from the list.

One reading follows. The price list's tagline is "the more you use, the cheaper it gets"9, and it is exactly true of the marginal price: from $50.00 per million in the first paid bracket to $9.00 above 250,000,000, which is 82% lower. It is not true of the average price, which is what a customer experiences as cost per event. The average rises from $25.00 per million at 2,000,000 events to a peak of $33.06 at 15,000,000, 32% higher, and only then falls, because the free first million dilutes the average at low volumes and every bracket up to 15,000,000 costs more per event than the average so far. Counting only paid events, the average never rises. A team that doubles from 5,000,000 to 10,000,000 events pays 2.12 times as much (324.40 divided by 152.90), not twice and not less. PostHog states no reason for its brackets, but its contract rules give one for volume discounts on annual commitments: "Our cost to serve a $500k customer is not 10x a $50k customer, so we can share those savings"19. Applying that reasoning to the brackets is the writer's reading.

6.5 When the billable unit meets the code

A price metric is also a specification, and feature flags show it. Flag requests are billed per request, with 1,000,000 free per month9. PostHog's usage documentation says that with local evaluation "each poll for feature flag definitions is charged as 10 feature flag requests when the definitions changed, and as 1 request when they didn't", that polls are made "every 30 seconds by default", and that a constantly running server whose flags rarely change is charged "about 2 * 60 * 24 * 30 = 86,400 feature flag requests each month, plus 10 requests for each poll that returns new definitions". The one-request price depends on the SDK sending the ETag of the definitions it holds so that the server can answer "304 Not Modified"; "SDK versions without ETag support are charged 10 requests for every poll", which "adds up to 10 * 2 * 60 * 24 * 30 = 864,000 feature flag requests"17.

Set against the allowance, one server polling at the default interval uses 8.64% of the free million with ETag support and 86.4% without. Billing is organization-wide, "one bill with one free allowance"20, so two servers use 172,800 requests with ETag support, inside the allowance, and 1,728,000 without. The latter is 728,000 above the free million, which at the second bracket's $0.0001 per request9 is 728,000 × $0.0001 = $72.80 per month, by my arithmetic from the list. Identical flags and identical polling cost $0 or $72.80 depending on whether the client library supports a conditional request. For an SDK vendor the implication is that adding conditional-request support is a price cut, and that deciding what counts as one unit is a product decision made in code. Predictability, the cost Stripe attaches to usage pricing (Section 7), is handled here with two instruments: the free tier gives "an accurate projected volume in a few days"17 before any payment, and billing limits cap spend, with the price that "PostHog drops data after you go over your billing limit"20. Cost certainty is bought with the risk of lost data, and the customer carries that risk.

6.6 Who decides, and what is not shown

The pricing page names no owner. The role page does. A PM "owns" pricing for a product: reviewing "whether pricing aligns with the value users get", identifying when "pricing or packaging creates friction for adoption, retention, or expansion", designing any change "taking into account our pricing principles" with the billing and marketing teams, and tracking "the impact of pricing changes on revenue, conversion, and churn"2. For early products without a PM, the team lead decides "how to charge for it" (Section 1.1), and the handbook's example project for the stage at which a PM decides the pricing model is the pricing of PostHog AI2. The review is yearly and goes to an RFC7.

Caveat

Two limits apply to everything in this section. It is one company's policy, written by the people who set the prices, with no revenue, margin or churn figures in any page fetched. The match-the-cheapest-competitor rule carries its own justification, that "the total ACV is higher even if the per-product ACV is lower" because PostHog can sell other products to the same customer7; a single-product vendor has no such offset, so the same rule would be a pure discount for it. That is the writer's reading; PostHog does not say it.

7.2 Volume and graduated tiers differ in what they bill

Stripe's documentation sorts the same space differently: flat rate, per-seat, tiered ("volume-based pricing" or "graduated pricing"), and usage-based, whose forms include "fixed fee and overage, pay as you go and credit burndown"21. The two tiered modes differ in a way that matters to an engineer implementing a meter. In volume pricing "the entire quantity (or usage) is multiplied by the unit cost of the tier" reached; in graduated pricing "the totals for each tier are summed together". The documentation's example has 1 to 5 fonts at 7 USD, 6 to 10 at 6.5 USD and 11 or more at 6 USD: 5 fonts cost 35 USD under both, 6 fonts cost 39 USD under volume pricing (6 × 6.5) and 41.50 USD under graduated pricing (35 + 6.5)22. The documentation warns that under volume pricing "the total might decrease when calculating the final cost". The condition is simple. Moving from \( n_k \) to \( n_k + 1 \) units across a boundary lowers the total exactly when

$$ (n_k + 1)\, p_{k+1} \;\lt\; n_k\, p_k \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{p_{k+1}}{p_k} \;\lt\; \frac{n_k}{n_k + 1}. $$

In the documentation's example the ratio at the first boundary is \( 6.5 / 7 = 0.929 \), above \( 5/6 = 0.833 \), so the total rises. A steeper cut would not: with a hypothetical 5.5 USD for the sixth unit onward, 6 units would cost 33 USD against 35 for 5. Graduated pricing cannot do that, because each unit is charged at its own tier's price. A volume schedule with a steep cut therefore pays a customer to buy more, or to split or batch usage across the boundary. PostHog's per-bracket calculator18 is the graduated kind, which is the safer one for a metered developer product.

8. Choosing a price metric from the cost structure

8.1 Three questions for any candidate unit

The sources never state a procedure for choosing a metric, but their rules imply three questions to ask of any candidate unit \( u \): a seat, an event, a request, a token, a device. Does the vendor's cost scale with \( u \)? PostHog reserves flat fees "for functionality where cost doesn't scale with usage"7, and Stripe's margin warning is the case where cost scales and the price does not5. Does the customer's value scale with \( u \)? PostHog's documentation rejects monthly active users on this ground: "Some users are very valuable. They use your product a ton, they respond to your feedback surveys, and they store a lot of data in your platform. Other users hit your landing page and bounce immediately. From a value perspective, you shouldn't need to pay the same amount for both of these users"17. And does charging for \( u \) tax something you want more of? PostHog does not charge for extra users because "the more people we get inside PostHog, the better"7, while a per-event meter taxes instrumentation, which is why its documentation has a section on how "to reduce your PostHog costs" by capturing fewer events17. The third question is the writer's addition, drawn from those two statements: a metric is an incentive on the customer's behavior.

8.2 What a flat seat does to margin: a derivation and a calculator

Stripe's statement that a flat seat fee "will eventually create a margin problem" under heavy use5 follows from arithmetic. Let seat price be \( s \), vendor cost per task \( c \), and customer \( i \) use \( u_i \) tasks. Margin on that customer is \( s - c\,u_i \), negative whenever \( u_i > s/c \), and the blended margin is \( s - c\bar u \). Under a usage price \( p \), margin is \( (p - c)\,u_i \) and the margin rate \( (p - c)/p \) does not depend on the mix. A hybrid with base \( b \), \( q \) included tasks and overage \( o \) bills \( b + o\max(0, u_i - q) \). The calculator uses illustrative numbers, none from a source.

Formulas: flat \( (s - c\bar u)/s \); usage \( (p - c)/p \); hybrid \( \text{revenue} - c\bar u \) over revenue. At the defaults the flat seat earns 52.0% blended and loses 20.00 USD on each heavy user.

At the defaults the light majority hides the loss: 80% of customers cost 2.00 USD against a 20 USD seat. Double the heavy tier to 4,000 tasks and the blend falls from 52.0% to 12.0% (mean usage 880 tasks, cost 17.60 USD per seat) while the median customer has not changed. Usage pricing holds 60.0% but the heavy bill doubles to 200 USD, which is the predictability cost Stripe names. The hybrid holds 46.3% with the heavy bill at 124 USD. These are the three numbers behind "will eventually", and none is evidence about any real product.

8.4 The on-device inversion

In every source here, the cost that makes usage pricing necessary is the vendor's own: PostHog processes and stores the customer's data17, and Stripe's examples are tools whose agentic workflows "can consume much more compute than AI autocomplete does"5. For a runtime that executes on the customer's device, the compute is the customer's. If the vendor's marginal cost per inference is near zero, the first question of Section 8.1 answers "no" for every candidate unit, the left column of Figure 9 opens, and the choice rests on the value question and on what the vendor can observe. Usage pricing also needs metering: PostHog bills the events it receives, while an on-device runtime sends the vendor nothing unless it is built to, so metering becomes an engineering and trust decision before it is a pricing one. None of the units one would try, per device, per app, per active user, per model shipped or per inference, appears in a fetched source for on-device software; PostHog's argument against monthly active users is the one fetched argument about a per-user unit. This page therefore gives no price, margin or licence figure for an on-device runtime, and none is implied.

9. Positioning, briefly

book not fetched The source assigned to this topic is the author's book page for Obviously Awesome, which is marketing copy. It promises "a step by step process that any startup can follow", says positioning breaks into "five component pieces" and "three styles of positioning", and names none of them; it says "newly updated and expanded edition", so at least two editions exist, and no year is stated here24. The component names below are from Dunford's own March 2021 post summarizing the book25, which is a blog post and not the book.

She defines positioning as "how your product is a leader at delivering something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about", built from five components: competitive alternatives, differentiated capabilities, value for customers, target customer segmentation and market category25. The components depend on each other, and the method fixes where to start: "the starting point had to be competitive alternatives", meaning "what would customers do if our solution didn't exist", which often is the status quo and not a named competitor. Then come "What do we have that the alternatives do not?", "So what for customers?", who cares most, and last the category. She warns against "phantom competitors", ones that "theoretically could compete with you; you just never actually see them or lose to them in deals"25. It matters on this page because the category carries price expectations: it "sets off a really powerful set of assumptions about who your product competes with, what features your product should have, who the product is intended for, and even things like what the product should cost"25. Matching the cheapest competitor, as in Section 6, amounts to accepting the category's price; that link is the writer's reading.

Her opening example is the nearest thing in the sources to on-device software. A product sold as a "Microsoft Access killer" had "barely sold 200 copies"; phoning 100 customers, she found that "94 didn't even know they had it. 6 had transformed their business with it"; repositioned as an "Embeddable Database for Mobile Devices", it "took off"25. It is one author's account of an event about twenty years before she wrote it, and the $100 per copy that her records show in the story is not evidence about runtime pricing. PostHog's handbook lists "shape initial positioning" ("who the product is for and why it matters") among typical PM work at stage 12. No fetched source applies Dunford's method to an infrastructure runtime, and her book page describes B2B startup products; the first step, asking what customers would do without the product, has an answer the sources do not contain.

10. Where the evidence ends

The PostHog role page has the one sentence in any fetched source: "When a team is working on an infrastructure-heavy product, it's more important that the team lead has strong engineering and infra skills than product skills. 10.2 An evidence map for this page

Areas the sources here could not carry, and that need a course with a second research pass and not another page: willingness-to-pay research methods, inference unit economics and cost of goods, go-to-market motions for developer products, opportunity sizing and business cases, strategy frameworks beyond the four above, platform-product management, and product careers.

Where experts disagree

Two disagreements run through the sources: how to price a developer product, and what the word strategy refers to. No fetched source compares any position with another on outcomes, so each is stated by its mechanism and its own evidence.

Pricing a developer or infrastructure product

Meter it, publish it, match the rival

PostHog handbook and price list79

Usage-based by default, no per-seat charge, anchor to the cheapest big rival earning real revenue, a free allowance on every product, a yearly review.

  • One unit carries both cost and value; the list is public
  • The free tier doubles as a metering instrument and a growth channel
  • One company's policy; no outcome data
  • The rival rule leans on cross-selling; billing limits buy predictability by dropping data

A seat with a usage layer, governance behind the top tier

Stripe's guide5; Linear's plan page as a seat-priced example23

Per-seat is simple but cross-subsidizes heavy users, so a hybrid keeps the seat predictable and the vendor's economics intact; identity and governance features mark the move to enterprise.

  • Fits buyers who need a predictable budget
  • Tiers follow procurement stages
  • Qualitative guidance from a billing vendor, no numbers; hybrid's downside unstated
  • Linear's costs and results are not published

Price is a signal; the lever is the stack

Spolsky, 2002 and 2004816

Demand cannot be observed by asking, price sends a message, segmentation and unlisted prices backfire, and a company gains most by making its complements cheap.

  • Honest about what cannot be known
  • Points to actions that raise demand rather than to a price
  • Essays without data, from the desktop-software era
  • Silent on subscriptions and metering
Assessment

The first two differ on where predictability comes from: PostHog meters and caps, Stripe puts a seat under the meter. Both default to cost-tracking and both gate governance behind a higher fee. The third answers a different question: the price level, and which lever raises demand. Which is right depends on three things no source measures: whether the vendor's cost scales with use (Section 8), how the buyer budgets (Section 1.4), and whether a rival with real revenue exists to anchor on. For an on-device runtime the cost condition may fail (Section 8.4, the writer's unverified inference), which would remove the main argument for the first two and leave the third. The evidence that would settle it is margin and churn by pricing model after the first renewal cycle, by cohort; none is in the sources.

What a strategy is, and where it comes from

Coherent action backed by an argument

Rumelt1213

A diagnosis, a guiding policy and coordinated actions; not ambition, vision or planning.

  • A concrete test; names how strategies fail
  • The cases are the author's; the book was not read

Vision plus ranked objectives per team

Cagan14

Not defined as strategy; what teams are given in place of a roadmap.

  • Transmits direction to autonomous teams
  • Example is hypothetical; leaves the diagnosis to the team

Strategy as the choice of metric

Kohavi, Tang and Xu15

The strategy drives the OEC; guardrails state what is not traded; results can falsify it.

  • Measurable and falsifiable
  • Presupposes a strategy exists

Position among complements

Spolsky16

Make your complements cheap so demand for your product rises.

  • A mechanism that can be written as arithmetic
  • 2002; no data; the author hedges
Assessment

These answer different questions. Rumelt says what makes a strategy good, Cagan how a team receives direction, Kohavi et al. how a strategy is checked, Spolsky where an advantage comes from. They collide only on vision: the blurb says strategy is not vision, and Cagan puts vision first. An engineer can use them together: Rumelt's test on the claim, Kohavi's metric on the result. The evidence that would settle the vision question, a comparison of organizations with and without a stated diagnosis, does not exist in the sources.

What is changing

Every pricing source here is likely to change within a year. Stripe's guide, last updated 19 April 2026, argues that agentic use is straining flat seats5; Stripe's documentation now says "Metronome is now part of Stripe" and names it the recommended starting point for new usage-based billing27; Linear's page already footnotes AI credits on a seat-priced plan23. PostHog's product team lists, among its Q2 2026 themes, "tracking human vs agent actions, and defining success for each"28: if agents generate the events and requests that a meter counts, the meaning of a usage unit shifts.
