Metrics, Experiments and Goals: OEC, Guardrails, HEART, OKRs and Trust

A team needs a number it can move in two weeks that still points at what it wants in two years, a list of things it will not break to move it, and a habit of distrusting results that look too good. This page covers the overall evaluation criterion and guardrails, the HEART and Goals-Signals-Metrics frameworks, what experiment base rates imply for a portfolio of work, how OKRs and their alternatives set goals, and what changes when a feature behaves probabilistically. Afterwards you can write a goal-signal-metric chain for a feature, choose an OEC and guardrails for it, grade an OKR set, and say what evidence would justify shipping with or without an experiment.

1. The overall evaluation criterion

An experiment needs one question: did the treatment make things better? Kohavi, Tang and Xu name the quantity that answers it the overall evaluation criterion and define it by two constraints. It "must be measurable in the short term (the duration of an experiment) yet believed to causally drive long-term strategic objectives" (Ch. 1, p. 6)1. The first constraint is operational, and the book puts a number on it: an organization "should define an OEC that can be easily measured over relatively short durations (e.g., one to two weeks)" (p. 12)1. The second is a belief, not a measurement, and the book says where the effort goes. The authors' companies keep teams "focused on metrics: choosing metrics, validating metrics, and evolving metrics over time," and those teams work out "which metrics measurable in the short term drive long-term objectives, since experiments usually run over a shorter time frame" (p. 22)1.

For an engineer the structure is familiar: the OEC is a surrogate objective, and the experiment optimizes the surrogate. How far the result can be trusted depends on the correlation between surrogate and true objective, which is the one quantity a two-week experiment cannot observe (my framing of the book's definition). The book states the difficulty as "finding metrics measurable in a short period, sensitive enough to show differences, and that are predictive of long-term goals" (p. 12)1. It prefers a single metric, "possibly as a weighted combination" of objectives (p. 7)1, while allowing that large organizations may have several OECs or "several key metrics that are shared with refinements for different areas" (p. 12)1.

1.2 A worked derivation: the OEC as an exchange rate

A composite OEC adds quantities that do not share a unit. The weights are exchange rates, and the MSN video test shows what that means in numbers. The Control played an ad before the first video; the Treatment moved the ad after the content. The site owners' question was "Would the loyalty of users increase enough in the Treatment to make up for the loss of revenue from not showing the ad up front?" The OEC was the six-week return rate of a cohort. The return rate "increased just over 2% in the Treatment, not enough to make up for the loss of ad impressions, which dropped more than 50%"2.

The paper gives the two deltas and the verdict but not the valuations. They can be backed out. At baseline, let \( a_0 \) be the pre-roll ad revenue per user, \( r_0 \) the share of the cohort that returns, and \( V \) the value of one return. Assume ad revenue is proportional to impressions and read both reported changes as relative (the paper does not say whether the 2% is relative or absolute). The Treatment loses \( 0.5\,a_0 \) per user and gains \( 0.02\,r_0 V \). It wins only if

$$ 0.02\, r_0 V \;>\; 0.5\, a_0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r_0 V \;>\; 25\, a_0 . $$

So the baseline value of return visits per user must exceed 25 times the pre-roll revenue per user before a 2% loyalty gain pays for a 50% impression loss. If the 2% is instead two percentage points on the return rate, the condition becomes \( V > 25\,a_0 \): same factor, different quantity. Both reported figures are rounded ("just over 2%," "more than 50%"), so 25 is an approximate threshold. The authors' conclusion, "not enough," therefore says something about their valuation: it implied returns worth less than roughly that multiple. This is my arithmetic on the paper's two reported figures, not a figure from the paper.

The same team did the explicit version in the MSN home-page ads test. It valued clicks two ways (what the destination property assigns to a click, and what Microsoft pays search engines to win that visit back), and "the numbers were close enough to get agreement on the monetization value to use." In a 12-day test on 5% of US home-page users, click-through rate and page views per user-day each fell 0.35% (relative); the lost clicks were worth more than the added ad revenue, an estimated loss of "millions of dollars per year" had it shipped2. The experiment supplied two small deltas. The valuation carried the argument, and it was settled by agreement.

1.3 Strategy picks the OEC; experiments climb within it

The book is direct about the dependency: "Having a strategy is critical for running experiments: the strategy is what drives the choice of OEC" (p. 21). Experiments then "help hill-climb to a local optimum based on your current strategy and product," and the failure the authors have seen is "when the OEC is not properly chosen"1. Their image for a bad OEC is "experimenting to improve the food or lighting on a sinking cruise ship" (p. 22)1. Page 6 treats strategy as a contested concept; the consequence here is narrower. Whoever chooses the OEC is making a strategic decision, and an engineer handed a metric to optimize has been handed a strategy.

The one fetched case where an OEC is used as a team goal with a number is Bing's relevance team: "several hundred people tasked with improving a single OEC metric by 2% every year." The 2% "is the sum of the Treatment effects (i.e., the delta of the OEC) in all controlled experiments that shipped to users over the year, assuming they are additive," and because "the team runs thousands of experiment Treatments, and some may appear positive by chance," credit is assigned from a separate certification experiment run with a single Treatment (pp. 14-15)1. Dilution matters when counting: "a 5% improvement for 10% of your users" is 0.5% overall, if the triggered users resemble the rest (p. 14)1. By that arithmetic the 2% target is four shipped wins of 0.5% each (my division, under the book's additivity assumption). Section 10 returns to this as one of the alignment mechanisms.

2. Guardrails and the ship decision

An OEC says what the organization wants more of. A guardrail says what it will not give up to get it. The book ties the two to strategy: "Defining guardrail metrics for experiments is important for identifying what the organization is not willing to change, since a strategy also 'requires you to make tradeoffs in competing – to choose what not to do'" (Ch. 1, p. 22, quoting Porter)1. The software example it offers is crashes: "if a feature is increasing crashes for the product, the experience is considered so bad, other factors pale in comparison." The Bing headline win passed this test by construction: revenue rose 12% "without significantly hurting key user-experience metrics" (p. 3)1.

2.1 A guardrail is a constraint, not a heavy weight

The book says safety can be handled "either via high weight in the OEC, or, equivalently, using passenger safety as a guardrail metric" (p. 22)1. The two are equivalent only in a limit. Write the composite as \( \text{OEC} = \sum_i w_i m_i \) and let \( g \) be the guarded metric with floor \( g^{*} \). A heavy weight corresponds to a penalty \( \lambda \min(0,\, g - g^{*}) \) with finite \( \lambda \). That penalty is an exchange rate in the sense of Section 1.2: a large enough OEC gain still buys a small loss in \( g \). A guardrail is the case \( \lambda \to \infty \), maximize the OEC subject to \( g \ge g^{*} \), where no gain buys the loss. The practical difference is who gets to argue. With a weight, every ship review reopens the price. With a gate, the price was settled in advance and the review only checks whether the gate held. This is my formalization of the book's one-line equivalence.

The fetched chapter gives three guardrail examples (crashes, latency, sessions per user) and no taxonomy. The book's dedicated treatment is a sidebar in Chapter 6 (p. 98 in its table of contents), which was not fetched1. Not every constraint is a metric either: some of Bing's monthly ad packages were known to be negative "as a result of space constraints or legal requirements" (p. 15)1.

2.2 The anti-metric: percent of plan delivered

The opposite of an OEC is a metric that cannot fail. The authors describe the temptation: "It is often easier to generate a plan, execute against it, and declare success, with the key metric being: 'percent of plan delivered,' ignoring whether the feature has any positive impact to key metrics" (p. 12)1. A team measured this way ships on schedule and learns nothing. Its failure is a case of a general one: a proxy becomes the target. The book gives Goodhart's law, Campbell's law and the Lucas critique a section in Chapter 7 (p. 109 in the table of contents), unfetched here1; the chapter's own instance is click-through rate, "too gameable and needing to evolve" (p. 22)1. Section 9 shows the same failure in goal-setting, where key results written as outputs make a team's grade a function of delivery.

3. What has to be true before you can experiment

The book is candid that "not every decision can be made with the scientific rigor of a controlled experiment": you cannot run one on a merger, "as we cannot have both the merger/acquisition and its counterfactual" (Ch. 1, p. 10)1. It lists four technical ingredients (pp. 10-11) and three organizational tenets (p. 11)1. The ingredients are the part an engineer can check against his own product. 4. Experiment results as a portfolio

The book's third tenet is that "the organization recognizes that it is poor at assessing the value of ideas" (Ch. 1, p. 11)1. The evidence offered is a set of success rates, and they are four different quantities. Figure 2 draws them as separate bars and does not average them. Page 1 uses the same figures for a different argument, how ideas die before and after testing; here they matter for what a team should plan on.

4.1 Four base rates, kept apart

Only the Microsoft row has a primary source in hand: the 2009 paper reports that among "well-designed and executed experiments that were designed to improve a key metric, only about one-third were successful at improving the key metric"2. The other three reach this page through the book, as a citation of Manzi, a quotation of a Slack executive's post, and a secondhand reading of Netflix. The 2009 paper adds an offline base: QualPro, a consultancy running multivariable controlled experiments, tested 150,000 business-improvement ideas over 22 years and reported that 75 percent "either have no impact on performance or actually hurt performance"2. The same paper reports that "only three out of 21 people guessed the winner" among six widget designs, and the three "were from the ExP team" (§4.1)2. What survives is the weaker claim the authors make: "Not every domain has such poor statistics, but most who have run controlled experiments in customer-facing websites and applications have experienced this humbling reality" (p. 14)1.

4.2 The payoff tail

The same chapter opens with the opposite end of the distribution. A Bing ad-headline change was "prioritized low and languished in the backlog for more than six months until a software developer decided to try the change, given how easy it was to code"; the test raised revenue 12%, "over $100M annually in the US alone" (p. 3), a return on "a few days' work for one engineer" (p. 4)1. The authors then say how rare that is: "Bing runs over 10,000 experiments a year, but simple features resulting in such a big improvement happen only once every few years" (p. 5)1. Taking "a few" as two to five years, which is my reading, that is roughly one such result in 20,000 to 50,000 experiments. Typical gains are of a different order: "improvements to key metrics are achieved by many small changes: 0.1% to 2%" (p. 14)1. Success rates of 10 to 33%, a typical gain of 0.1 to 2%, and a tail event worth $100M a year describe a heavy-tailed payoff, and in such a game the cost per draw decides the strategy (my inference). The book points the same way when it lists "the overhead of running an experiment must be small" among the themes of the Bing case (p. 5)1. What the case implies for backlogs and for how ideas are ranked is the subject of page 3.

4.3 How many experiments before a win

If each experiment independently improves its target metric with probability \( p \), the chance of at least one win in \( n \) is \( 1-(1-p)^n \). To reach confidence \( c \), solve for \( n \):

$$ n \;\ge\; \frac{\ln(1-c)}{\ln(1-p)} . $$

At \( c = 0.9 \) this gives 5.68 (so 6 experiments) for \( p = 1/3 \), 10.32 (so 11) for \( p = 0.2 \), and 21.85 (so 22) for \( p = 0.1 \), the edges of the reported range. The assumptions are strong: independence, a constant \( p \), and "win" meaning the metric moved, not that the change was worth shipping. The arithmetic is mine. Its use is to turn a base rate into a capacity requirement. A team that can finish four experiments a month and works where \( p \) is near 0.1 should expect about five and a half months before a 90% chance of one win, and the calculator below lets you move all three knobs.

4.4 Near bets, radical bets, and sunk costs

The book's recommendation follows from the numbers: "always having a portfolio of ideas: most should be investments in attempting to optimize 'near' the current location, but a few radical ideas should be tried," because "most big jumps fail (e.g., big site redesigns), yet there is a risk/reward tradeoff: the rare successes may lead to large rewards that compensate for many failures" (Scenario 2, p. 23)1. Redesigns fail for a measurable reason: they "subject users to primacy effects" and "commonly fail not only to achieve their goals, but even fail to achieve parity with the old site on key metrics" (p. 21)1. The same effect biases the test, since a short experiment on a redesign measures "primacy effects or change aversion" and "may not measure the true long-term effect" (p. 23)1.

The portfolio also has an exit rule. Bing pursued a social-media integration strategy for about two years; "after spending over $25 million on the strategy with no significant impact to key metrics, the strategy was abandoned," and the authors add that "failed bets are sunk costs, and we should make a forward-looking decision based on the available data" (pp. 23-24)1. A single failed experiment tests one tactic; many failed tactics are a signal about the strategy. That is the row-4 caveat of Table 2 and the reason the OEC and the strategy must be revisited together.

5. Trusting a number: Twyman's law

The Bing headline test did not announce itself as a triumph. "A few hours after starting the test, a revenue-too-high alert triggered, indicating that something was wrong with the experiment." The authors call these "too good to be true" alerts "very useful, as they usually indicate a serious bug, such as cases where revenue was logged twice (double billing) or where only ads displayed, and the rest of the web page was broken." In this case the increase "was valid," and "the experiment was replicated multiple times over a long period" (Ch. 1, p. 3)1. The Preface turns the habit into a rule: "We believe in the skepticism implied by Twyman's Law: Any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong; we encourage readers to double-check results and run validity tests, especially for breakthrough positive results" (p. xv)1.

5.1 Twyman's law as Bayes' rule with a heavy-tailed prior

The rule has a mechanism, and Section 4.2 supplied its input. Let \( q \) be the prior probability that an experiment contains a genuine effect as large as the one observed, and \( b \) the probability that it contains a defect (double logging, a broken page) that fakes a jump of the same size. If either cause produces the jump with certainty, then

$$ P(\text{real} \mid \text{jump}) \;=\; \frac{q}{q+b}. $$

From the book's figures, Bing runs 10,000 or more experiments a year and a result of the $100M kind comes "once every few years" (p. 5)1, so with \( y \) years between such wins \( q = 1/(10{,}000\,y) \). At \( y = 3 \) that is 3.3 per 100,000 experiments. The odds turn even when \( b = q \): a defect that fakes a jump of that size in one experiment out of every 30,000 is enough. Nothing in the source gives \( b \); it is the free knob in Figure 4. The structural point does not depend on its value. The larger the claimed effect, the smaller \( q \), because the payoff tail is heavy, while defects do not become rarer as they get bigger (a metric logged twice doubles regardless). That argument is mine, not the book's. Skepticism should therefore grow with the size of the surprise, which is what "especially for breakthrough positive results" asks for. The book's definition of an interesting result points the same way: "Interesting experiments are ones where the absolute difference between the expected outcome and the actual result is large" (p. 16)1. The results most worth learning from are the ones most likely to be wrong.

5.2 Causation, and the ladder of evidence

The second trust problem is older than experiments. The book's example is Microsoft Office 365: users who see error messages and crashes churn less, "but that does not mean that Office 365 should show more error messages." All three facts have one cause, usage: "heavy users of the product see more error messages, experience more crashes, and have lower churn rates" (pp. 8-9)1. The authors rank evidence on a ladder adapted from medicine (Figure 1.3, p. 9): meta-analyses of randomized experiments at the top, then randomized controlled experiments, then other controlled experiments, then observational studies, and at the bottom "case studies ..., anecdotes, and personal (often expert) opinion, a.k.a. HiPPO"1. Section 8 uses this ladder to say what evidence a ship decision needs. The rest of the book's trust machinery, including Chapter 3 on threats to validity (p. 39) and Chapter 21 on sample ratio mismatch (p. 219), sits in chapters not fetched1.

6. PULSE and HEART

An experiment platform tells you whether a treatment moved a metric. It does not tell you which metrics deserve to exist. Google's UX research team addressed that gap in a 2010 CHI note by Rodden, Hutchinson and Fu3. They start from what teams already track, which they call PULSE: "Page views, Uptime, Latency, Seven-day active users (i.e. the number of unique users who used the product at least once in the last week), and Earnings." These are "all extremely important," and an engineer will recognize them as what an operations dashboard shows. The authors' complaint is that they are "either very low-level or indirect metrics of user experience, making them problematic when used to evaluate the impact of user interface changes," and may be ambiguous: a rise in page views may occur "because the feature is genuinely popular, or because a confusing interface leads users to get lost in it, clicking around to figure out how to escape" (section "Pulse Metrics")3.

6.1 HEART is a set of categories, not a set of metrics

HEART stands for "Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, and Task success." The authors stress that "these are categories, from which teams can then define the specific metrics that they will use," that "it is not always appropriate to employ metrics from every category," and that the framework's value is to make "an explicit decision about including or excluding a particular category." Their example of exclusion is Engagement in an enterprise product where use is required, with the note that "it may still be meaningful to consider Engagement at a feature level"3. Happiness and Task success generalize older usability measures; Engagement, Adoption and Retention are "new categories, made possible by large-scale behavioral data"3.

The paper supports two further uses. In September 2008 Google Finance saw a surge in page views and seven-day actives, and those totals "did not indicate whether the surge was driven by new users interested in the crisis, or existing users panic-checking their investments." Adoption and Retention metrics separated the two groups and showed the rate at which new users kept using the site3. And the Gmail metric was chosen with an explicit reason, "engaged users should check their email account regularly, as part of their daily routine," and the authors found it "strongly predictive of longer-term retention, and therefore could be used as a bellwether for that metric"3. That sentence is the only place in the note where the short-term-to-long-term link that an OEC depends on (Section 1) is reported as tested, and it gives no numbers.

6.2 Why totals mislead: the seven-day-active trap

The note makes a precise claim about its own PULSE metric: seven-day actives "measures the overall volume of the user base, but gives no insight into the users' level of commitment," does not "differentiate between new users and returning users," and "in a worst-case retention scenario of 100% turnover in the user base from week to week, the count of seven-day active users could still increase, in theory"3. The reason is arithmetic. If \( N_t \) new users arrive in week \( t \) and none returns, then \( \mathrm{7DA}_t = N_t \), which rises whenever acquisition rises. Figure 5 draws that case with an illustrative 10% weekly growth in new users. The cure the paper gives is normalization: "raw counts will go up as your user base grows, and need to be normalized; ratios, percentages, or averages per user are often more useful"3.

Three limits are worth stating plainly. The framework was "applied to more than 20 different products and projects from a wide variety of areas within Google," a company whose products are "almost all web-based, and have millions of users each." The note reports no evidence that using HEART improved a product's outcomes; its examples show decisions that metrics informed3. The metrics "are primarily useful for evaluation of launched products, and are not a substitute for early or formative user research," and "should be triangulated with findings from other sources, such as usability studies and field studies"3. And no HEART category has been checked for a product whose users are developers: whether an SDK's "Adoption" is a signup, a first successful call, or a production release is a definition the team has to make, which is the work the next section structures.

7. Goals, signals, metrics

HEART names categories; a second piece of the same paper says how to get from a category to a number. The process has three steps. In the paper's words it "steps teams through articulating the goals of a product or feature, then identifying signals that indicate success, and finally building specific metrics to track on a dashboard," because a metric "is unlikely to be useful in practice unless it explicitly relates to a goal"3. Each step has tips from Google's experience, and they read as engineering advice.

  • Goals. "Different team members may disagree about what the project goals are. This process provides a great opportunity to collect all the different ideas and work towards consensus (and buy-in for the chosen metrics)." Goals for a feature "may be different from those for the product as a whole." Do not "get too distracted at this stage by worrying about whether or how it will be possible to find relevant signals or metrics"3.
  • Signals. Ask what behavior or attitude would show the goal met. Choose signals "sensitive and specific to the goal," which "should move only when the user experience is better or worse, not for other, unrelated reasons." "Sometimes failure is easier to identify than success," such as task abandonment or "undo" events. Logs and surveys are the two sources used most often, and the check to make is whether "the relevant actions" are "currently being logged, or could they be?"3.
  • Metrics. Normalize raw counts. Filter "traffic from automated sources (e.g. crawlers, spammers)." Confirm that "all of the important user actions are being logged (which may not happen by default, especially in the case of AJAX or Flash-based applications)"3.

The goal step carries more weight than its length suggests. If teammates "disagree about what the project goals are," the metrics that follow will each serve a different goal and the dashboard will not settle any argument. That is why the paper treats the process as a consensus tool before it is a measurement tool.

A hypothetical chain shows the template on a developer-facing feature. It is not from any source, and no source covers infrastructure products. Goal: a developer integrates an on-device runtime and gets a first successful inference without help. Signals: an install followed by a successful inference call; no support contact before it; an install that never reaches one, since "failure is easier to identify than success." Metrics: the share of each week's new installs that reach a first successful inference within a stated window, and the median time to it, both per install cohort rather than totals. By analogy with the paper's enterprise note, where Engagement may still be meaningful "at a feature level"3, the engagement category would belong at the feature level for such a product; that extension is mine. Latency, memory and crash rate are not in the chain; they are the guardrails of Section 2.

8. How much evidence before acting

Sections 1 to 5 describe an instrument. Whether to wait for its reading is a separate decision, and two fetched sources answer it in opposite tones. Kohavi et al. say a "data-driven or a data-informed organization gathers relevant data to drive a decision and inform the HiPPO ... rather than relying on intuition," and that "a decision should be made with many sources of data, including controlled experiments, surveys, estimates of maintenance costs for the new code" (Ch. 1, p. 12)1. Bezos, in Amazon's 2016 shareholder letter, writes that "most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had. If you wait for 90%, in most cases, you're probably being slow," and that "being wrong may be less costly than you think, whereas being slow is going to be expensive for sure"4.

8.1 The decision as an inequality

The two positions are answers to the same cost question, which can be written down. Let \( p_f \) be the probability that the idea does not help (the base rates in Section 4 put it at roughly 0.67 to 0.9), \( C_w \) the cost of shipping a dud until it is found and reversed, \( C_e \) the cost of running the experiment, \( t \) the time it takes, and \( D \) the value a good idea loses per unit of delay. Assume the test is reliable. Shipping now is worth \( (1-p_f)V - p_f C_w \); testing first is worth \( (1-p_f)(V - Dt) - C_e \). Testing wins when

$$ p_f\, C_w \;>\; C_e + (1-p_f)\, D\, t \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{C_w}{D\,t} \;>\; \frac{1-p_f}{p_f} + \frac{C_e}{p_f\, D\, t}. $$

If the platform makes the experiment nearly free (\( C_e \to 0 \)), the right-hand side is \( (1-p_f)/p_f \): 0.5 at \( p_f = 2/3 \), 0.25 at 0.8, 0.11 at 0.9. A test is worth waiting for whenever a dud costs more than about a tenth to a half of what the delay costs a good idea. That is a low bar, and it explains why the book insists that "the overhead of running an experiment must be small" (p. 5)1: cutting \( C_e \) and \( t \) is what moves the decision. A two-way door is the case where \( C_w \) is small because reversal is cheap, and there the inequality flips toward shipping at 70% and watching, which is Bezos's rule. A one-way door makes \( C_w \) large and the rule inverts. This is my derivation. It omits imperfect tests, statistical power, and the information a decision generates for later ones (the book names the Expected Value of Information, p. 241).

8.2 What each source warns against

The warnings differ. Kohavi et al. fear the plan that cannot fail ("percent of plan delivered," Section 2.2) and note that "objective measurements typically show that progress is not as rosy as initially envisioned" (p. 12)1. They also record the exception that proves nothing: Apple under Steve Jobs, where Ken Segall "claimed that 'we didn't test a single ad. Not for print, TV, billboards, the web, retail, or anything'" (pp. 11-12)1. That is one sentence about advertising, reported secondhand, not a study of product decisions. Bezos fears stand-ins for the real thing and names two: process ("the process becomes the proxy for the result you want") and averages ("market research and customer surveys can become proxies for customers"). His example is "Fifty-five percent of beta testers report being satisfied with this feature. That is up from 47% in the first survey," which he calls "hard to interpret." His prescription is not to skip testing: "I'm not against beta testing or surveys. But you, the product or service owner, must understand the customer ... Then, beta testing and research can help you find your blind spots"4. Rodden et al. say metrics "should be triangulated" with qualitative findings and are no "substitute for early or formative user research"3.

9. OKR mechanics and what they optimize

An OEC is a single scalar that a team moves by experiment. An OKR set is a different kind of object: a handful of ambitious statements, each with measurable key results, graded after a fixed period. Google's re:Work guide, "originally published 2016" and preserved "as a record," is the primary description5. Marty Cagan's 2015 essay on roadmaps names OKRs "my current favorite" and supplies the product-side rationale6. Table 6 lists the parameters.

Table 6. OKR parameters as stated in Google's re:Work guide (2016 record)5.

9.1 The grading scale is a calibration target

Most goal systems treat a miss as a failure. This one budgets for it: if a team's average is 0.6 to 0.7 the system is working, and "if someone consistently fully attains their objectives, their OKRs aren't ambitious enough"5. A consequence the guide spells out is for dependents. "If they are using stretch goals you should expect them to deliver on about 70% of their stated OKRs," so a team that depends on another team's stretch objective should plan on 70%5. An OKR grade is therefore an estimate with a built-in haircut, not a commitment. Cagan separates the two: dates are handled by "high-integrity commitments," used "for those situations where we need to actually commit to a date or a specific deliverable"6, and page 3 covers their cost.

The figure makes a limit visible. Because the objective grade is a mean, a team that fully misses one key result and fully makes the others lands in the same place as a team that was partly short on all three. The guide's own grading examples show how binary scoring enters: a key result "to 'Launch a new widget marketing campaign'" is 0 or 1, while "Launch six new features" with three launched is "graded with a 0.5"5.

9.2 Failure modes the guide lists, and one it contains

The guide's own list of mistakes is a catalog of ways a number stops measuring what it was written for. Business-as-usual OKRs are "written based on what the team believes it can achieve without changing anything they're currently doing." Sandbagging: "teams who can meet all of their OKRs without needing all of their team's bandwidth may either be hoarding resources, not pushing their teams, or both." Low-value objectives: "could the OKR get a 1.0 under reasonable circumstances without providing direct organizational benefit?" Insufficient key results: if they "don't represent all that is needed to fully achieve that objective," a miss arrives late and unexpected5.

The guide also contains the failure it warns against. Its text says key results "should describe outcomes, not activities," yet five of the seven key results in its two sample sets name a deliverable or an activity and no measured change, among them "Launch xx feature to all users," "Secure tech support to build xx feature in Q1," and "Re-establish [product]'s leadership by speaking at three industry events"; only the revenue-per-user and error-response-time results carry a measured change5. That is the pattern Kohavi et al. call "percent of plan delivered" (Section 2.2)1. Cagan's version of the principle is that the system "is all about outcome rather than output," illustrated by an objective to cut onboarding from 30 days to under 3 hours; he introduces the figures with "suppose your product currently requires 30 days," so they are an illustration, not data6. The guide's evidence for OKRs is one sentence: Sears Holding Company "rolled out OKRs to their 20,000 employees and saw positive impacts on bottom-line sales and individual performance," with no figures5.

10.1 PostHog: why a company dropped metric goals

PostHog's James Hawkins and a colleague wrote in December 2024 that "we used to do OKRs and metric-based goals, but we ran into two problems with it": "teams wasted too much time coming up with the right metric to focus on," and "engineers focused on small quick wins to move those metrics, rather than building what our users actually wanted"7. Their replacement is a quarterly list of things to build and who owns them, chosen by engineers after executives share high-level company goals (for example, "we need to increase top of funnel adoption")7. The accountability moves to a monthly growth review run by the product manager, which compiles "revenue metrics" (MRR, churn), "product analytics" (active users, retention) and "user feedback" (NPS, interviews, tickets) and asks, for example, "Can we find leading indicators that predict long-term product usage?" The engineering team lead then decides whether to continue, reprioritize or change goals. The stated reason: "Without this accountability, autonomy can become directionless"7. The handbook's current planning page keeps the shape. Goals are "primarily output-based," with "metrics only if they help you," and the stated worry about metrics is that "all objectives are bad - they have many compromises, are fallible, easy to game"8. The same page also asks for "what we'll ship," a rationale, and "we'll know we're successful when: metric or outcome," and says "hitting an overarching objective is more important than shipping specific things"8. The handbook page carries no date and is a living document.

One difference from Google deserves a line. The re:Work guide treats a 0.6 to 0.7 average as the system working5. PostHog's page says "if your team is consistently missing objectives, they are too hard or possibly the wrong objectives," and "consistently hitting ambitious objectives over the long term is an important factor in the pay review process"8. The first uses misses as a calibration signal and keeps goals out of evaluation; the second treats misses as a goal-setting error and ties hits to pay. The phrase "goal" covers two different contracts.

PostHog's loop reads metrics after the fact instead of setting them as targets. That avoids the gaming problem of Section 2.2 at the price of the one Kohavi et al. describe, a plan that can be executed and declared a success. What separates PostHog from that failure is the review, not the goals, and the review is only as good as the PM's data and the willingness of the CEO or a senior leader to "challenge assumptions, ask hard questions, and ultimately hold the team accountable"7.

10.2 Linear and Shape Up: progress as work, bets as commitments

Linear's method asks teams to "measure progress with actual work," and says "the clearest way to see whether something is complete or not is to show the diff in the code or design file"9. Its direction page calls initiatives "a critical tool for shaping this direction" and wants a timeline "ideally a bit out of reach"9. Its "set useful goals" page asks for "a goal that propels you forward in some measurable way," with a startup example that runs from finding 10 users, to 100, to $1000 in monthly recurring revenue9. Linear's own account of how it ships says readiness "comes down to the engineers working on the project feeling that the project is ready," and the fetched post has no passage on judging a project's effect after release11. Shape Up's mechanism is the cap: "a smart bet has a cap on the downside. If we bet six weeks on something, the most we can lose is six weeks," and the betting table looks "at the bigger movement of progress on the whole product after the six weeks"10. The cap bounds the loss from a wrong bet but does not say how a team learns it was wrong. Neither source gives one.

11. Metrics for features that behave probabilistically

Everything above assumes a feature whose behavior the team can read off the code. A feature built on a model does not satisfy that. Amershi and colleagues at Microsoft Research open their CHI 2019 paper with the cause: "automated inferences are typically performed under uncertainty, often producing false positives and false negatives," so AI-infused systems "can violate established usability guidelines of traditional user interface design." Their example is consistency: "many AI components are inherently inconsistent due to poorly understood, probabilistic behaviors based on nuances of tasks and settings, and because they change via learning over time" (§1)12. Two properties follow for measurement. The error rate is part of the product, and the behavior can change between two visits by the same user.

11.1 The 18 guidelines

The paper consolidates "more than 150 design recommendations" into 18 guidelines, with a user study of 49 practitioners testing them on 20 AI-infused products (abstract, §3)12. Each is "a rule of action, containing about 3-10 words and starting with a verb," grouped by when in an interaction it applies (§3, Table 1). Figure 8 draws them in the paper's four phases. I have marked the four that govern a system that changes over time; the marking is mine.

The three that matter most for a metrics decision are G2, G11 and G14. G2: "Make clear how well the system can do what it can do. Help the user understand how often the AI system may make mistakes." G11: "Make clear why the system did what it did." G14: "Update and adapt cautiously," glossed as "limit disruptive changes when updating and adapting the AI system's behaviors"12. The paper reports 785 examples across the 20 products: 313 applications, 277 violations, 89 neutral and 106 judged "does not apply." G11 "had one of the highest number of violations," with among the fewest "does not apply" ratings, which the authors read as participants who could imagine an explanation but were often unable to obtain one. G5 and G6 were "noted as the least clear." G10 and G14 were hard to observe: "it's a bit difficult to assess this in a single session," one participant wrote about G1412. This study measured whether practitioners could apply and understand the guidelines. It did not measure retention, revenue or task success, so no sentence in it says that following a guideline raises any business metric.

G14 connects to the experiment machinery. The book warns that short tests of big changes measure "primacy effects or change aversion" (p. 23)1, and Google saw iGoogle satisfaction fall after a redesign and recover3. A model that adapts changes the product continuously, and the effects of adaptation on a user may accumulate more slowly than an OEC window of one to two weeks can show. G14 is therefore a design constraint to hold even when the OEC does not move.

11.2 Offline gain is not business gain

A team building a model has a second metric the web-experiment literature does not discuss, the offline score on held-out data. The fetched evidence on how it relates to business impact is one paper: Bernardi, Mavridis and Estevez at Booking.com, KDD 2019, an analysis of "about 150 successful customer facing applications of Machine Learning" validated through randomized controlled trials13. Its Section 3 reports that "increasing the performance of a model, does not necessarily translates to a gain in value." The data are 23 comparisons (46 models) between a baseline and a new model, classifiers scored by ROC AUC and rankers by mean reciprocal rank, each compared in an online trial against one business metric. The correlation between relative offline gain and relative business gain is -0.1 (Pearson, 90% confidence interval -0.45 to 0.27) and -0.18 (Spearman, interval -0.5 to 0.19)13.

The authors hedge the result themselves. They write that they "do not want to overstate the generality," because the models work in one context, "all target the same business metric," and each is "trying to improve it after a previous model already did"; they note a similar weak link for machine-translation scores and human judgment, and say a correlation shows up "only where the offline metric is almost exactly the business metric"13. They list four mechanisms. Value-performance saturation: the value-versus-performance curve flattens, and gains become "impossible to detect in an RCT in reasonable time." Segment saturation: triggered analysis counts only users for whom two models disagree, and as models converge that population shrinks, cutting statistical power. Uncanny valley: users find a model that predicts too well unsettling. Proxy over-optimization: a recommender trained on click-through might "end up 'just driving clicks'," recommending near-identical hotels, which risked "drowning them into the paradox of choice and hurting conversion"13. The last is Section 1's Buy-button failure with a model as the optimizer: a better optimizer finds the gap between proxy and goal faster. Their conclusion is operational: "Offline model performance metrics are only a health check."

11.3 Latency has a measured price, in web products

A model's cost is also a product metric. The same Booking.com paper ran a four-arm experiment with synthetic latency and found "an increase of about 30% in latency costs more than 0.5% in conversion rate"13. Its design for separating a model's benefit from its slowdown is a three-group experiment. Control sees no change. A first treatment calls the model and shows its output to the users it triggers. A second calls the model and shows nothing different. Comparing the second with control prices the slowdown alone; comparing the two treatments, which carry the same computational load, prices the functionality alone (§7.2-7.3)13. 11.4 What the sources do not cover

Offline-versus-online evaluation is a stated gap. The one candidate source, the Booking.com paper, covers classifiers and rankers at one company in 2019. The fetched material says nothing on evaluating generative models, on offline evaluation sets as a specification, or on guardrails for output quality. The guidelines paper itself says its list "might not adequately address all types of AI-infused systems," and that it leaves out principles that "require decisions to be made at the modeling layer of a system" (§7)12. A team shipping a model-backed feature has the OEC and guardrail structure of Sections 1 and 2, the base rates of Section 4 and the cautions above. It does not have a sourced method for choosing between an offline score and an experiment.

Where experts disagree

Three disagreements run through the fetched sources: how much evidence to wait for, whether goals are set as measurable targets or reviewed as work, and how fast a product should change under its users. None of the positions below is backed by outcome data comparing it with the others; each is a company or author describing its own practice, so the comparison is of mechanisms and costs.

Evidence or judgment: how much data before acting?

Be data-informed; formalize the OEC

Kohavi, Tang and Xu, Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments (2020)1

The organization "is poor at assessing the value of ideas," so run controlled experiments and let data "inform the HiPPO."

  • Base rates of 10 to 33% success (Figure 2) show intuition fails often.
  • A marketing senior vice-president's objection to cart recommendations at Amazon lost to an experiment (p. 17).
  • Needs units, an agreed OEC and cheap changes (Table 3); many decisions cannot be tested.
  • The authors' companies are the best-instrumented in the industry.

Decide near 70%; resist proxies

Jeff Bezos, 2016 shareholder letter4

Wait for 90% and "you're probably being slow." Averages from surveys and betas "can become proxies for customers"; the owner "must understand the customer."

  • Prices delay, which a test-everything rule ignores (Section 8.1).
  • Names a real failure: a satisfaction average that moves without anyone knowing why.
  • Apple under Jobs did not test ads, reported secondhand in the book1.
  • No evidence offered that deciding at 70% beats waiting; the letter's one extended example is a disagree-and-commit story about a TV show.

Tie metrics to goals; triangulate

Rodden, Hutchinson and Fu, CHI 20103

Metrics "should be triangulated" with usability and field studies and are "not a substitute for early or formative user research."

  • Fits the two others: numbers evaluate, qualitative work generates.
  • Google-internal examples; no outcome data.
Assessment

The positions answer different cost regimes. Section 8.1's inequality says to test when a dud is expensive relative to a good idea's delay cost, and base rates make duds the common case, so for reversible changes in a product with cheap experiments Kohavi's rule wins. For a one-way door, a product with too few units, or an invention where no baseline exists, Bezos's 70% rule and Rodden's triangulation are what remains. The one genuine collision is the status of surveys: Kohavi lists them as one source among several; Bezos warns that an average can replace understanding. A study that assigned comparable decisions to evidence-heavy and judgment-heavy styles and tracked outcomes would settle it; none appears in the fetched sources.

Aligning teams to goals: targets, or bounded work and review?

Commit to measurable targets

Google re:Work (OKRs)5; Cagan6; Kohavi et al. (OEC)1

Teams are told the result and its measure, and choose the method: "tell the team what you need them to accomplish, and how the results will be measured." The OEC is the single-metric version, certified by replication.

  • Moves argument from features to results; "percent of plan delivered" cannot pass.
  • The grading scale budgets for misses.
  • Google's own sample key results are mostly outputs (Section 9).
  • PostHog reports that teams "wasted too much time" choosing metrics and chased "small quick wins"7.

Commit to bounded work, then review

Shape Up10; Linear9; PostHog78

Fix the time or the list of things to build, cap the downside, and judge the effect afterward: a six-week cap, "measure progress with actual work," a monthly growth review.

  • Avoids metric gaming and metric debates before the work starts.
  • Engineers choose the work.
  • "Autonomy can become directionless" without the review7.
  • Shape Up and Linear name no method for learning that a shipped bet was wrong.
Assessment

The axes are not exclusive. PostHog's own handbook asks for "what we'll ship" and a success measure in the same template8, and Kohavi's Bing relevance team commits to an OEC delta yet works through shipped experiments. A target works when a measurable proxy is trusted and gaming is covered by a guardrail; bounded work works when the team is small, senior and has a reviewer who reads the data. For an inference-runtime team, a small one by assumption, I would take bounded work with a stated outcome and one or two guardrails. That is my judgment, not a finding. A comparison of teams under each regime on shipped-effect per quarter would settle it; the sources have none.

Speed or trust: how fast should a product change under its users?

Change quickly, because most changes fail cheaply

Kohavi et al.1

Bing runs over 10,000 experiments a year; most big redesigns fail, and continuous iteration is preferred to complete redesigns that "subject users to primacy effects."

  • Cheap tests find the rare large win.
  • Their own caution: short tests of big changes confound "change aversion."

Change cautiously, because users bear the cost

Amershi et al. (G14)12; Linear11; Cagan14; Rodden et al.3

"Update and adapt cautiously"; "if you release something and iterate too much, it can be difficult for your customers"; customers can feel "abused because of constant, erratic change."

  • Names a cost the OEC window can miss; satisfaction dipped after an iGoogle redesign before recovering.
  • No measured size for the trust cost in any fetched source.
Assessment

Cagan resolves most of it: rapid test-and-learn runs "on select groups of users and customers, enabling us to protect our general users and customers from this experimentation," and some customers opt in14. That is a statement about who is exposed, not how fast the team learns. A runtime whose behavior changes between releases has a sharper form: a change shipped to everyone cannot be a two-way door in the sense of Section 8, because users' integrations depend on it (my reading). Longitudinal data on retention after changes to an adaptive feature would settle the size of the trust cost; the sources have none.

What is changing

Metric definitions are being rewritten for traffic that is not human. PostHog's handbook lists as a Q2 2026 product-management theme "make sure our products & tracking are set up for a agent-first world," including "tracking human vs agent actions, and defining success for each," and, separately, ensuring "metrics definitions are mature enough for our scale (e.g. inconsistencies in activation & product intent)"15. A developer product whose callers are agents inherits the same problem, in my reading: a seven-day-active count (Section 6.2) stops meaning a person came back. The handbook is a living page and likely to change within a year. The fetched evidence on offline-versus-online evaluation is a 2019 paper about classifiers and rankers13, and the re:Work OKR guide is an archived 2016 record5. Nothing in the source map tests either claim for generative-model features.
